## Definition

Kaldewey's *Wahrheit und Nützlichkeit: Selbstbeschreibungen der Wissenschaft zwischen Autonomie und gesellschaftlicher Relevanz* (transcript 2013, originally a Bielefeld dissertation) reconstructs how modern science describes itself to others. The central claim is that science does not have one self-description but two semantic poles it continuously re-balances: **truth** (knowledge as an end in itself, grounding the autonomy discourse) and **usefulness** (societal relevance, grounding the practice discourse). The historically shifting ratio between them is what allows science to absorb external demands without surrendering its claim to autonomy.

## Intuition

Neither pole alone can carry the legitimacy of science. Pure autonomy cannot explain why society should fund it; pure usefulness cannot explain why scientists should be left to choose their own problems. The two therefore operate as a pendulum: when external pressure for relevance rises, the usefulness pole is emphasized; when instrumentalization threatens the conditions of inquiry, the truth pole is invoked. The pendulum is not a compromise but a mechanism — it is what makes science adaptable across two centuries of changing societal expectations.

Methodologically, Kaldewey treats these as *semantics* in the Luhmannian sense — communicative self-descriptions with their own history — rather than as philosophical positions to be adjudicated. The question is not whether science really is autonomous but how the distinction does work.

## Formal notation

None; the work is historical-sociological, drawing on systems theory and conceptual history (Begriffsgeschichte).

## Key variants

- **Autonomy discourse** — science as self-governing, with truth as the internal code; legitimation runs through the claim that useful results are a by-product of free inquiry.
- **Practice discourse** — science as a societal problem-solving capacity; legitimation runs through demonstrable contribution.
- **Historical settlements** — Humboldtian purity, the postwar linear model, and the relevance turn each fix the ratio differently while preserving the distinction itself.

## Known limitations

- Concentrates on German and Anglo-American self-descriptions; the generality of the two-pole structure across other science systems is assumed.
- As a semantics-level analysis it deliberately brackets whether the described autonomy exists in practice, which limits what it can say about actual governance.
- The pendulum model explains adaptation well but has little to say about conditions under which the mechanism could fail.

## Open problems

- What happens to the pendulum when the usefulness pole no longer requires the truth pole — that is, when reliable prediction and technical functioning become available without scientific procedure?
- Is a third self-description available, one grounded neither in truth-as-end nor in usefulness, for example in publicly auditable procedures of fact-establishment?
- How do the two discourses interact with metric-based evaluation regimes that formalize the usefulness pole into indicators?

## Relevance to active research

Kaldewey supplies the strongest available account of why the science system has historically absorbed external shocks — and therefore the most precise way to state what would be different about AI. If AI makes prediction and technical functioning obtainable without scientific procedure, the usefulness pole stops depending on the truth pole, the two are no longer mutually implicated, and the pendulum loses its stabilizing function. That reading connects directly to [[verification-bandwidth]] and to work on [[measurability-gap]]: both describe a regime in which output can be had without the procedure that used to be its only source.
