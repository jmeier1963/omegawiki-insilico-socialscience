## Definition

Deliberative polling is a form of democratic assembly developed by political scientist James Fishkin (Stanford, 1988). A random, representative sample of citizens is first polled on a set of policy positions, then provided with carefully vetted and balanced educational materials, brought together to discuss issues in structured small-group conversations, and given the opportunity to pose questions to competing experts. Their views are polled a second time to measure the degree to which opinions changed through informed deliberation.

## Intuition

The goal is to reveal what a representative microcosm of the public *would* think about an issue if given the time and resources to consider it more deeply. Unlike ordinary opinion polling (which captures uninformed or unreflective views) or expert consultation (which samples an elite), deliberative polling captures opinion formation under conditions of information and structured dialogue.

## Formal notation

Not applicable (empirical democratic process methodology).

## Key variants

- **America in One Room**: large-scale national deliberative poll on immigration and economic policy (USA)
- **Meta Community Forum**: online deliberative polling format enabling broader participation
- **Deliberative Poll on Climate**: cross-national applications with climate policy focus

## Known limitations

- Expensive and logistically complex to run at scale
- Participant recruitment may introduce self-selection biases
- Deliberation quality depends heavily on facilitator training and material balance
- May still be susceptible to group polarization dynamics in certain topic contexts

## Open problems

- Whether online/asynchronous deliberation achieves the same epistemic effects as in-person
- How to set normative standards for what counts as "sufficiently deliberative"
