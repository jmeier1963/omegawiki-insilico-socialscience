## Terminological framework

| Term | Definition (used in this document) |
| --- | --- |
| **Reconstructed synthetic data** | Derived from real datasets; preserves statistical distributions and correlations while ensuring privacy compliance |
| **Generative synthetic data** | Produced by statistical or ML models (VAEs, GANs, LLMs) without one-to-one record mapping |
| **Structural / model-based synthetic data** | Generated by rule-based or simulation methods under defined assumptions |
| **Silicon sampling** | Using LLM responses as proxies for survey respondents (Argyle et al. 2023) |
| **Homo silicus** | LLM as computational model of human economic decision-making (Horton 2023) |
| **Algorithmic fidelity** | The degree to which an LLM faithfully mirrors the response distribution of a target human population |

## Use Case 1 — Silicon Sampling: LLMs as Survey Respondents

### Block A — Maturity

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Problem** | Survey research is expensive, slow, and structurally biased toward accessible populations (WEIRD, panel-fatigue, non-response). Rare subgroups (specific political intersections, marginalized demographics) are systematically underrepresented. |
| **Missing data** | Representative subgroup data; responses from hard-to-reach populations; longitudinal panel attrition; attitudes not yet collected in historical archives |
| **Decision relevance** | High in political science (electoral modeling, policy feedback), public health (attitude segmentation), product/UX research; lower for high-stakes decisions given validity constraints |
| **Maturity level** | **Emerging — contested validity**. Proof-of-concept results (2022–2023) are strong; rigorous critique (2024–2026) identifies five structural failure modes. Usable for exploration and pilot work; not ready for confirmatory inference without formal calibration. |

### Block B — Data Type

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Structure** | *Inquiry data* — closed-ended survey items, Likert-scale attitude measures, behavioral economics games |
| **Granularity** | Individual-level response vectors; aggregated subgroup distributions |
| **Time dimension** | Primarily cross-sectional; some longitudinal extrapolation (dangerous — knowledge cutoff risk) |

### Block C — Model Logic

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Model type** | *Generative synthetic data* via LLM with persona conditioning (demographic backstory, interview transcript, or psychographic profile) |
| **Logic** | Prompt includes identity specification → LLM generates token-level response distribution → verbalization or log-probability extraction → aggregate to population distribution |
| **Scenarios vs. forecasts** | Primarily *scenarios* (what would this persona type answer?); occasionally used for distributional forecasts |

**Conditioning approaches by fidelity (ascending)**:

1. Demographic backstory (Argyle et al. 2023) — weakest; 14–15 pp below interview
2. Psychographic profile inferred from social media (SPIRIT framework)
3. Full two-hour qualitative interview transcript (Park et al. 2024) — 85% normalized accuracy on GSS; best available

### Block D — Governance

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Data protection** | Lower than real survey microdata *if* no individual can be identified from the persona specification. Risk increases with interview-grounded approaches (verbatim transcript data). GDPR relevance when using real social media content as persona seed. |
| **Bias risks** | **Structural and well-documented**: (1) Overrepresentation of liberal, educated, high-income worldviews (RLHF training); (2) belief-system over-coherence (Barrie & Cerina 2026 — 28 LLMs, robust finding); (3) heterogeneity ceiling — structural overregularization persists even after persona optimization; (4) Persona conditioning *degrades* alignment for underrepresented subgroups (Assessing Reliability 2026). |
| **Validation available?** | Yes, but method-dependent. 85% GSS accuracy under interview conditioning (GPT-4o, U.S. only); log-probability methods underestimate alignment; verbalization outperforms sampling. Cross-cultural validation largely absent (German GLES failure documented). Martingale violations (order dependence) impair Bayesian inference. |

### Block E — Output Utilization

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Primary use** | *Simulation* (distributional estimates for rare subgroups); *pilot study* (hypothesis generation before fielding real survey); *sensitivity analysis* (how would results change under different demographic compositions) |
| **Appropriate scope** | Exploratory, hypothesis-generating, and pilot-design phases; not confirmatory inference without statistical calibration framework (Argyle et al. 2026) |
| **Communication** | Summary statistics, synthetic crosstabs, distributional comparisons |

**Key papers**: Argyle et al. (2023, *Political Analysis*); Horton (2023, NBER); Park et al. (2024, *Nature*); Bisbee et al. (2024); Barrie & Cerina (2026); Busby et al. (2026)

## Use Case 2 — Automated Social Science: LLMs as Experimental Subjects

### Block A — Maturity

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Problem** | Behavioral experiments (e.g., behavioral economics, social psychology) require human participants — costly, ethically constrained, slow, often underpowered, WEIRD-biased. Replication crisis amplifies the cost of false positives. |
| **Missing data** | Experimental evidence for rare conditions; cross-cultural replications; multi-site variation; experiments that are ethically or practically impossible with real humans |
| **Decision relevance** | Theory testing in behavioral economics; causal inference about social mechanisms; pre-registration benchmarks |
| **Maturity level** | **Promising but epistemologically contested**. Manning et al. (2024) demonstrate SCM-based automation; Hewitt et al. (2024, 70 pre-registered experiments) show 91% treatment-effect variation prediction. Fundamental limits: no validation against human populations in original automated studies. |

### Block B — Data Type

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Structure** | *Behavior data* — experimental outcomes from structured interaction protocols (bargaining, bail hearings, job interviews, auctions) |
| **Granularity** | Individual agent decision + aggregate effect-size estimates (OLS-fitted SCM coefficients) |
| **Time dimension** | Point-in-time experimental interactions; no longitudinal structure |

### Block C — Model Logic

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Model type** | *Structural / model-based synthetic data* — Structural Causal Models (SCMs) operationalize hypotheses as causal graphs; factorial design generates treatment combinations; LLMs enact agent roles |
| **Logic** | SCM identifies: agents (nodes), outcomes (endogenous), causes (exogenous), interaction protocol. Factorial combinations exhaust treatment space. Post-interaction survey captures LLM-reported outcomes. OLS fits SCM to data. Key insight: "The LLM knows more than it can immediately tell" — direct prompting for causal coefficients fails (13× overestimate); fitted SCM succeeds. |
| **Scenarios vs. forecasts** | *Scenarios* (if condition X, then outcome Y); causal identification possible under stated assumptions |

### Block D — Governance

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Data protection** | No real human data unless calibrating on real behavioral datasets. IRB requirements unclear / actively debated. |
| **Bias risks** | RLHF-trained models may produce systematically pro-social, conflict-avoidant behaviors (too polite); SCM-generated data lacks ecological validity; results may reflect training data biases rather than genuine human behavioral patterns |
| **Validation available?** | Partial: Manning et al. validate qualitatively against economic theory; no cross-species (human/LLM) quantitative comparison in original paper. Hewitt et al. provide 70-experiment validation suite. |

### Block E — Output Utilization

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Primary use** | *Simulation* for causal hypothesis generation; *pilot* for effect-size priors before human subject studies |
| **Decision scope** | Legitimate for theory development and pre-registration; not for policy-relevant causal inference without human validation |
| **Communication** | SCM coefficient plots; effect-size tables; counterfactual scenario comparisons |

**Key papers**: Manning et al. (2024, automated social science); Hewitt et al. (2024, 70 experiments); Argyle et al. (2026, calibration framework)

## Use Case 3 — Multi-Agent Social Simulation (Emergent Social Dynamics)

### Block A — Maturity

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Problem** | Classical agent-based models (ABMs) require hand-coded behavioral rules — brittle, cannot capture nuanced linguistic/reasoning behaviors, require expert calibration. Social dynamics (polarization, norm diffusion, policy shock response) are hard to study ethically in the real world. |
| **Missing data** | High-resolution behavioral data on social interaction under counterfactual conditions (policy changes, external shocks, novel technologies) |
| **Decision relevance** | Policy simulation (UBI effects, health interventions); platform design (content moderation impacts); crisis response planning (pandemic, disaster) |
| **Maturity level** | **Emerging infrastructure**. Park et al. (2023, Generative Agents) established the core memory-reflection-planning architecture. AgentSociety (2025) demonstrated 10,000-agent scale. Concordia (Google, 2023) provides general-purpose framework. Qualitative pattern validation is available; quantitative validation is rare and limited. |

### Block B — Data Type

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Structure** | *Behavior data* — agent actions (mobility, communication, economic exchange, social interaction); *structural data* — network topology, institutional rules |
| **Granularity** | Individual agent action + meso-level emergent patterns (polarization index, mobility statistics, health outcomes) + macro-level trend alignment |
| **Time dimension** | **Dynamic** — multi-step simulations; emergent temporal patterns (information diffusion curves, polarization growth, shock recovery) |

### Block C — Model Logic

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Model type** | *Structural / model-based synthetic data* — agents are LLM-backed; environment encodes institutional rules, network structure, physical constraints |
| **Architecture** | **Memory-Reflection-Planning** (Park et al.): memory stream (natural language, recency-importance-relevance retrieval) → periodic reflection → hierarchical daily/hourly planning → reactive action. **AgentSociety** adds: dual-stream memory (Event Flow + OCC-model appraisals), Maslow hierarchy for need-driven behavior, Ray distributed computing (43K MQTT msg/s). **Concordia**: Game Master architecture separates agent cognition from environment physics; component-based working memory. |
| **Scenarios vs. forecasts** | *Scenarios* — counterfactual policy conditions, exogenous shocks. Not forecasts in the predictive sense (no quantitative calibration to real-time data). |

### Block D — Governance

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Data protection** | Agents are synthetic; risk arises if initialization uses real individual-level microdata for calibration (requires pseudonymization) |
| **Bias risks** | RLHF training produces overly pro-social, conflict-avoidant agents; instruction-tuning suppresses extremist/fringe behaviors; English/US-centric behavioral priors; economic simplifications (no goods/labor markets in AgentSociety); polarization dynamics may be systematically muted |
| **Validation available?** | Qualitative pattern alignment (AgentSociety: UBI effects align with Texas trial, hurricane mobility aligns with real data). Quantitative calibration absent. Concordia acknowledges no validation in paper. |

### Block E — Output Utilization

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Primary use** | *Simulation* — policy scenario analysis; emergence mapping; stress-testing institutional designs |
| **Appropriate scope** | Qualitative insights and hypotheses; not quantitative prediction. Strong for: (a) identifying mechanisms not predictable from agent-level rules; (b) stress-testing counterfactual policies; (c) generating hypotheses for real-world study |
| **Communication** | Time-series plots of emergent measures; agent trajectory visualizations; counterfactual comparison tables |

**Key papers**: Park et al. (2023, Generative Agents, UIST); Park et al. (2022, Social Simulacra); Zhao et al. (2023, Concordia); Pang et al. (2025, AgentSociety); Tessler et al. (2025, LLM attitude diffusion)

## Use Case 4 — Synthetic Survey Data for Privacy-Preserving Data Sharing

### Block A — Maturity

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Problem** | Social survey microdata (census, panel studies, administrative registers) cannot be shared openly due to re-identification risk. Statistical disclosure control (suppression, rounding, perturbation) degrades analytical utility. |
| **Missing data** | Shareable microdata at individual record level; rare-event data with small cell sizes; cross-national comparative data restricted under data protection regimes |
| **Decision relevance** | High for public statistics agencies, academic data archives, cross-national research; compliance with GDPR, US Privacy Act, HIPAA analogs |
| **Maturity level** | **Established for reconstructed synthetic data** (classical differential privacy, CTGAN, copula-based synthesis). LLM-based generation for this purpose is at **early research stage** — promising but analytically unvalidated for complex survey microstructures. |

### Block B — Data Type

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Structure** | *Inquiry data* — survey microdata (demographic attributes, attitudes, behaviors, income); *administrative data* — registry linkages |
| **Granularity** | Record-level individual units; preserving marginal distributions, joint distributions, and analytic utility (regression coefficients, subgroup estimates) |
| **Time dimension** | Primarily cross-sectional release; repeated cross-sections (e.g., annual SOEP/GLES releases); panel data synthesis remains harder |

### Block C — Model Logic

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Model type** | *Reconstructed synthetic data* (classical approach: differential privacy, CTGAN, sequential synthesis, copulas) or *generative synthetic data* (LLM fine-tuned on microdata — Polypersona approach; German General Personas from ALLBUS) |
| **Logic** | Classical: fit distributional model to real data → sample synthetic records → validate utility (U-score: regression coefficient similarity, propensity overlap). LLM-based: fine-tune with LoRA on microdata records → generate personas → validate against held-out real respondents |
| **Scenarios vs. forecasts** | *Data substitutes* — no scenario projection; goal is empirical fidelity, not counterfactual generation |

### Block D — Governance

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Data protection** | Core objective. Differential privacy provides formal guarantee (ε-DP). LLM-based synthesis (Polypersona) uses LoRA + 4-bit quantization; Synonymix provides privacy-by-design via knowledge graph merging (max 13% source contribution per merged node). |
| **Bias risks** | Classical synthesis: rare subgroups compressed or suppressed. LLM synthesis: training data biases imported; Polypersona evaluated only against LLM-generated references — circular validation risk. |
| **Validation available?** | Classical: established utility metrics (U-score, R-U map, propensity overlap). LLM-based: BERTScore high (>0.88) but validated against synthetic references, not real respondents — **methodologically weak**. German General Personas validated against ALLBUS classifiers. |

### Block E — Output Utilization

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Primary use** | *Data sharing / dissemination* — open research datasets, cross-institutional collaboration, teaching datasets |
| **Appropriate scope** | Secondary analysis, methodological research, teaching; **not** primary empirical inference about real populations without independent validation of synthetic vs. real data equivalence |
| **Communication** | Released microdata files; data quality reports; utility-privacy trade-off documentation |

**Key papers**: Busby et al. (2025, more parameters than populations); Colglazier et al. (2025, Polypersona); Huber et al. (2025, German General Personas); Brinkmann & Yáñez (2026, Synonymix)

## Use Case 5 — Synthetic Respondent Data for Pre-Testing Questionnaires and Study Design

### Block A — Maturity

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Problem** | Pre-testing survey instruments (cognitive interviews, pilot surveys) is expensive and slow. Researchers need to evaluate question wording, order effects, response option design, and likely distributions before fielding costly real studies. |
| **Missing data** | Pre-field estimates of likely response distributions; cognitive burden indicators; wording effects |
| **Decision relevance** | Survey methodology; questionnaire design optimization; sampling frame decisions |
| **Maturity level** | **Early-to-developing**. LLMs are used informally by practitioners for question wording. Formal evaluation of this use case is limited; "Survey Response Generation" paper (Veselovsky et al. 2025, 32M responses × 8 SRGMs) provides most systematic evidence. |

### Block B — Data Type

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Structure** | *Inquiry data* — draft survey items, response option sets |
| **Granularity** | Item-level response distribution estimates; potential mode effects; flagging likely ceiling/floor effects |
| **Time dimension** | Point-in-time (pre-fielding stage) |

### Block C — Model Logic

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Model type** | *Generative synthetic data* via LLM with or without persona conditioning |
| **Key finding** | **Survey Response Generation Method (SRGM) choice is the dominant determinant of apparent alignment** — not persona specification or model size. Restricted generation outperforms sampling; chain-of-thought reasoning inconsistently helps. Prior work silently varied SRGMs, making cross-study comparisons unreliable. |
| **Scenarios vs. forecasts** | Wording effect scenarios; distributional forecasts (weak evidence base) |

### Block D — Governance

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Data protection** | Low risk — no real individual data; LLM responses are synthetic |
| **Bias risks** | SRGM choice can change substantive conclusions about which subgroups are well-simulated; liberal/educated priors inflate apparent alignment for some subgroups |
| **Validation available?** | Yes (Veselovsky et al. 2025 is definitive here): 10 LLMs × 8 SRGMs × 4 real surveys, demonstrating SRGM sensitivity |

### Block E — Output Utilization

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Primary use** | *Decision support* for questionnaire revision; *simulation* of likely distributions for power calculations; *communication* of expected response variation to study teams |
| **Appropriate scope** | Questionnaire development and power analysis; not a substitute for actual cognitive interviewing or pilot fielding |

**Key papers**: Veselovsky et al. (2025, Survey Response Generation); Tjuatja et al. (2024, distributional alignment benchmarking)

## Use Case 6 — Homo Silicus: LLMs as Economic Agents

### Block A — Maturity

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Problem** | Behavioral economics experiments require human participant pools for each treatment; calibrating heterogeneous agent types across many game configurations is intractable at scale. Economic theorists lack computational tools for rapid theory testing. |
| **Missing data** | Behavioral responses under rare parametric conditions; cross-game generalization of calibrated types |
| **Decision relevance** | Economic theory building; policy impact modeling (minimum wage, welfare reforms); institutional design |
| **Maturity level** | **Promising — reproducible in narrow scope**. Horton (2023) establishes methodology; qualitative replications of behavioral economics classics confirmed. Calibration-and-generalization approach shows strong within-paradigm results. No quantitative cross-human validation in main paper. |

### Block B — Data Type

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Structure** | *Behavior data* — experimental game outcomes (dictator allocations, minimum wage choices, status quo bias magnitudes, prospect theory parameters) |
| **Granularity** | Individual agent decision (token-level distribution) → calibrated mixture-of-types distribution → aggregate effect |
| **Time dimension** | Point-in-time strategic interactions; no intertemporal dynamics in canonical experiments |

### Block C — Model Logic

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Model type** | *Structural / model-based synthetic data* — economic game structure defines the interaction; LLM response functions as agent decision logic |
| **Calibration approach** | Mixture-of-types model fitted to in-distribution game data; tested on structurally different games (out-of-distribution). Calibrated agents reduce MSE from 0.182 → 0.094 vs. persona-less baselines. |
| **Scenarios vs. forecasts** | Primarily *scenarios* — parametric variation within game structure; limited cross-game forecast generalization |

### Block D — Governance

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Data protection** | No real individual data; LLM responses are synthetic |
| **Bias risks** | RLHF training suppresses genuine conflict-seeking, exploitation-seeking, and purely self-interested behavior; pro-social bias documented. Memorization risk: LLMs may produce game-theoretically "correct" results from training data rather than behavioral responses. |
| **Validation available?** | Qualitative: 5/5 behavioral economics classics replicated (dictator games, fairness, status quo bias, prospect theory, min wage). Quantitative calibration MSE reduction documented. No comparison to actual human participants across same games. |

### Block E — Output Utilization

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Primary use** | *Simulation* of behavioral economic mechanisms; *hypothesis generation* for experimental designs; *sensitivity analysis* on parametric variations impractical at human-subject scale |
| **Appropriate scope** | Hypothesis generation and theory refinement; not causal inference about real economic behavior |
| **Communication** | Game outcome distributions; calibrated type profiles; effect magnitude comparisons to behavioral economics literature |

**Key papers**: Horton (2023, Homo silicus, NBER); Manning et al. (2024, automated social science)

## Use Case 7 — Opinion Dynamics and Attitude Diffusion Modeling

### Block A — Maturity

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Problem** | How attitudes spread, polarize, or converge through social networks is difficult to study empirically: it requires repeated individual-level attitude measurement during natural social interaction, which is impractical at scale. Classical opinion dynamics models (Deffuant, Hegselmann-Krause) use toy behavioral rules. |
| **Missing data** | Mechanism-level data on how individual interactions translate to opinion change; counterfactual network topologies |
| **Decision relevance** | Platform content policy (filtering, recommendation algorithms); counter-disinformation strategy; political communication |
| **Maturity level** | **Early research**. Tessler et al. (2026) demonstrate LLM-backed agents in small-world networks with live news retrieval; AgentSociety shows polarization dynamics at scale. No quantitative ground-truth validation of diffusion dynamics. |

### Block B — Data Type

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Structure** | *Behavior data* — agent opinion states over time; *structural data* — network topology; *inquiry data* — attitude item trajectories |
| **Granularity** | Individual opinion vector (multidimensional attitudes) + network-level polarization indices + population distribution shifts |
| **Time dimension** | **Dynamic** — temporal opinion trajectories; convergence/divergence curves |

### Block C — Model Logic

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Model type** | *Structural / model-based synthetic data* — network topology defines interaction probabilities; LLM agents execute Observe-Think-Act loops with belief update |
| **Logic** | Real-time news retrieval augments agent context → agent interprets news through existing belief system → interaction with neighbor agents → belief update based on discourse → repeat. Homophilic vs. heterophilic network topology as key experimental parameter. |
| **Scenarios vs. forecasts** | *Scenarios* — what happens to polarization under different network topologies or information diets? Not calibrated forecasting. |

### Block D — Governance

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Data protection** | Agents are synthetic; risk if network initialization uses real user-graph data |
| **Bias risks** | RLHF models are systematically too moderate; extremist/fringe opinion dynamics are systematically underrepresented; heterogeneous belief representations impaired (Barrie & Cerina 2026) |
| **Validation available?** | AgentSociety: homophilic network 52% more polarized vs. heterophilic — directionally consistent with empirical literature but not quantitatively calibrated. No comparison to real online deliberation dynamics. |

### Block E — Output Utilization

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Primary use** | *Simulation* for platform design decisions; *scenario analysis* for regulatory impact assessment |
| **Appropriate scope** | Mechanism illustration and hypothesis generation; not quantitative prediction of real-world opinion shifts |
| **Communication** | Opinion trajectory plots; polarization index time-series; network topology sensitivity analyses |

**Key papers**: Tessler et al. (2026, attitude diffusion); Pang et al. (2025, AgentSociety); Park et al. (2023, information diffusion in Generative Agents)

## Use Case 8 — Social System Design and Prototyping

### Block A — Maturity

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Problem** | Designing online communities, content moderation systems, or participatory platforms requires understanding how diverse populations will interact with and within them — before the platform exists. Standard user testing is expensive and cannot cover rare or adversarial user behaviors. |
| **Missing data** | Pre-deployment interaction data; adversarial behavior patterns; norm-violation responses; community formation dynamics |
| **Decision relevance** | Platform design; content policy; HCI research; civic technology |
| **Maturity level** | **Operational**. Social Simulacra (Park et al. 2022) is the canonical application: Generate (populate community) + WhatIf (counterfactual what-if scenarios). Designers use it iteratively; Turing-test studies show synthetic behaviors are often indistinguishable from real. Well-suited because it doesn't require distributional accuracy — design iteration only needs relative/qualitative insight. |

### Block B — Data Type

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Structure** | *Behavior data* — posts, replies, anti-social behaviors, norm violations, community formation |
| **Granularity** | Individual interaction episodes; community-level interaction patterns; policy counterfactuals |
| **Time dimension** | Session-level simulation; no longitudinal continuity requirement |

### Block C — Model Logic

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Model type** | *Structural / model-based synthetic data* — community rules define the interaction frame; LLM agents populate it |
| **Logic** | Designer specifies community purpose and rules → LLM generates diverse synthetic members → simulates interaction → Generate shows candidate behaviors → WhatIf replaces an actor or rule to branch the scenario |
| **Scenarios vs. forecasts** | *Counterfactual scenarios* — what happens if we change this rule? Not population-representative forecasts. |

### Block D — Governance

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Data protection** | Low risk — no real user data required |
| **Bias risks** | LLMs may fail to simulate genuinely extremist users; fringe / minority behaviors underrepresented; cultural contexts outside training data poorly handled |
| **Validation available?** | Turing-test with Prolific users: synthetic behaviors often indistinguishable from real. But validity criterion is *design plausibility*, not distributional accuracy — a less demanding standard |

### Block E — Output Utilization

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Primary use** | *Simulation* for iterative design; *decision support* for platform policy choices; *communication* of design scenarios to stakeholders |
| **Appropriate scope** | Design iteration and discovery; not prediction of actual user behavior at scale |

**Key papers**: Park et al. (2022, Social Simulacra, UIST)

## Use Case 9 — Legal and Institutional Simulation

### Block A — Maturity

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Problem** | Legal system dynamics (crime, prosecution, sentencing, corruption) evolve over long time horizons and are hard to study experimentally. Policy changes (sentencing reform, police deployment) have diffuse, lagged, and interactive effects that ABM can model but classical ABMs have lacked behavioral realism. |
| **Missing data** | High-resolution behavioral data on individual legal decision-making; counterfactual crime/prosecution scenarios under different institutional rules |
| **Decision relevance** | Criminal justice policy; legal system reform; institutional design |
| **Maturity level** | **Early research**. Law in Silico (2025) is the primary demonstration: hierarchical LLM framework, real crime statistics for initialization. Qualitative validation only. |

### Block B — Data Type

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Structure** | *Behavior data* — individual crime/compliance decisions; *structural data* — institutional rules, sentencing guidelines, network of legal actors |
| **Granularity** | Individual agent decisions + aggregate crime trends + institutional corruption indices |
| **Time dimension** | **Dynamic** — multi-period simulation of policy interventions |

### Block C — Model Logic

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Model type** | *Structural / model-based synthetic data* — hierarchical LLM agent framework; real statistical distributions used for initialization (grounded in actual crime data) |
| **Logic** | Agents represent citizens, police, prosecutors, judges with role-specific decision functions; institutional rules encoded as game-master constraints; emergent dynamics from agent interactions |
| **Scenarios vs. forecasts** | *Scenarios* — policy counterfactuals (sentencing reform, police deployment reallocation) |

### Block D — Governance

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Data protection** | Aggregate crime statistics used for initialization — no individual-level data required |
| **Bias risks** | LLMs reflect training data biases about criminality, policing, and legal systems; racial/class biases documented in NLP applications likely persist in legal simulation; RLHF suppresses genuinely adversarial or corrupt behavior |
| **Validation available?** | Qualitative: macro-level crime trends reproduced; micro-level cat-and-mouse behaviors emerge; institutional corruption patterns align directionally. No quantitative comparison to real criminal justice statistics. |

### Block E — Output Utilization

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Primary use** | *Simulation* for policy scenario analysis; *communication* of mechanisms to policymakers |
| **Appropriate scope** | Exploratory policy analysis; not quantitative prediction |

**Key papers**: Cai et al. (2025, Law in Silico)

## Use Case 10 — Synthetic Data for LLM Training (Persona-Driven Capability Generation)

### Block A — Maturity

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Problem** | Training data diversity is a bottleneck for LLM capability: models trained on uniform or narrow data generalize poorly. Human-generated diverse data is expensive at scale. |
| **Missing data** | Diverse training examples covering rare domains, cultural contexts, demographic perspectives |
| **Decision relevance** | LLM training pipelines; benchmark construction; instruction-following alignment |
| **Maturity level** | **Operational**. PersonaHub (Ge et al. 2024) demonstrates at scale: 1B synthetic personas auto-curated from web data → persona-driven data synthesis → 7B model trained on persona-math achieves 65% MATH, matching GPT-4-Turbo at the time. 350 citations in one year. |

### Block B — Data Type

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Structure** | *Inquiry / behavior data* — synthetic Q&A pairs, mathematical reasoning chains, diverse instruction-following examples |
| **Granularity** | Instance-level training examples; population-diverse coverage |
| **Time dimension** | Static corpus (no temporal structure) |

### Block C — Model Logic

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Model type** | *Generative synthetic data* — billion-scale persona collection → persona-conditioned synthesis of diverse examples |
| **Logic** | Large-scale web text → profile extraction → 1B persona embeddings → persona-conditioned data generation → downstream model fine-tuning. Diversity is the key property, not alignment to a real population distribution. |
| **Scenarios vs. forecasts** | Not applicable — purpose is capability, not prediction |

### Block D — Governance

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |

## Cross-Cutting Validity Constraints

The following structural failure modes apply across most use cases and set hard limits on what in-silico social data can deliver:

| Failure Mode | Mechanism | Affected Use Cases |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Belief-system over-coherence** | LLMs produce unrealistically high inter-attitude correlations; ideology is over-concentrated on first principal component (Barrie & Cerina 2026, 28 LLMs) | 1, 2, 6, 7 |
| **Heterogeneity ceiling** | Structural overregularization persists even after persona optimization; no segmentation strategy fully restores within-group variance | 1, 3, 7 |
| **Martingale violations** | Response distributions depend on question order; violates exchangeability assumption required for Bayesian inference | 1, 5 |
| **SRGM sensitivity** | Survey Response Generation Method choice dominates alignment; prior comparisons unreliable due to uncontrolled method variation | 1, 5 |
| **Cross-cultural failure** | Most validation is US/English/WEIRD; documented failures for German political opinion (GLES); conditioning degrades further for underrepresented subgroups | 1, 2, 6 |
| **RLHF pro-social bias** | Fine-tuning suppresses extremist, conflict-seeking, and selfish behaviors that are sociologically consequential | 3, 7, 9 |
| **Training data contamination** | LLMs may produce "correct" outcomes by retrieving training examples rather than generalizing behavioral rules | 2, 6 |

## Implications for Research Practice

**What in-silico data can already do well**:

- Rapid questionnaire iteration and pilot design (Use Cases 5, 8)
- Hypothesis generation before expensive human-subject studies (UC 2, 6)
- Exploring mechanism dynamics under counterfactual conditions (UC 3, 7, 9)
- Populating and stress-testing platform designs (UC 8)
- Generating training data diversity for LLM capability (UC 10)
- Recreating known subgroup distributional patterns with interview-grounded conditioning (UC 1 under specific conditions)

**What requires additional development**:

- Formal statistical calibration frameworks to enable confirmatory inference (UC 1, 2)
- Cross-cultural and non-WEIRD validation corpora
- Methods to restore heterogeneity at the belief-system structure level (not just marginals)
- Quantitative calibration of agent-based simulations against real temporal panel data (UC 3, 7)
- Independent validation of LLM-based synthetic data generation against real microdata (UC 4)

**Governance priorities**:

- Mandatory transparency reporting on persona specification (Whose Personae? checklist: Application, Population, Data Source, Ecological Validity, Reproducibility, Generalizability)
- SRGM pre-registration to enable cross-study comparisons
- Subgroup fairness reporting (Demographic Parity Difference) for all simulation studies
- Clear disclosure distinguishing synthetic data from human-collected data in all publications
