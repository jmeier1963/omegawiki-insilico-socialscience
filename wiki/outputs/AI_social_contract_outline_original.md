AI AND THE SOCIAL CONTRACT

In a context of unbounded optimism and unbounded pessimism of our collective AI futures, and against the background of visible stress of today’s social contracts (maybe reaching their systemic limits in balancing limited resources and novel demands – defense, demography, climate…), we may be at a crossroads: defending current societal and market architectures, or committing to a fundamental redesign of societal institutions. 

There are good reasons to believe in the revolutionary capacity of AI (once it escapes the current kindergarten stage), and to foresee equally fundamental dangers if we don’t find a novel formula for running society. As of today, there is little consensus on what this formula looks like – and this seems risky as the speed of AI development leaves little time. 

What follows is an attempt to develop a positive perspective on an AI-cum-human solution.  

A.	THE ADVENT OF AI COULD FINISH THE MODERN SOCIAL CONTRACT MODEL

Sooner or (a little) later, AI skills and capacities will catalyze a large scale-reduction in the demand for human labor. 

A popular beneficial assumption – an explosion of new products which, crucially, perpetuate reliance on human labor input, barred to AI – is hardly plausible. 

So, employment, and work income, and work-based tax income, and work-based purchasing power will shrink, a lot. 

So will corporate revenues, and profits, and valuations, and revenue-based, and profit-based, and value-based taxes – as they all depend on ultimate (consumer) demand. 

Erosion of public income implies a collapse of public spending – to maintain public goods, to ensure a less unequal wealth/income distribution, to provide for age and care.    

With serious revenue gaps and spending gaps, current social contracts become untenable. 

B.	AWARENESS OF SOCIAL CONTRACT RISK GROWS, REDESIGN PROPOSALS EMERGE

Marketing-minded AI and socially concerned voices – a funny alliance – pinpoint the need for a novel social contract, to cope with the impact of the AI conversion. 

Triple motives in play: Maintain Lebenswelten w/o work (non-work income); maintain the consumption/investment cycle as a source of wealth; enable/smooth the AI expansion path.

(This cause is pursued in parallel to efforts to mitigate other valid AI concerns: non-aligned AI, cybersecurity and other bad-actor threats, serious human de-skilling, cultural collapse.)

Current proposals focus the financing dimension: how to generate, distribute, control funds for (private and public) income losses. 

“Generation”: (1) Taxing: (a) AI-related revenues/profits; (b) AI-related income/capital gain; (c) AI-unrelated income/wealth; (2) co-ownership of AI-corporations – public or private.

“Distribution”: (1) UBI, with different conceivable sets of criteria; (2) NBI (need-based basic income); (3) AI-ownership dividends; (4) subsidized private, enlarged public goods. 

“Control”: mostly variants of public/political agency: sovereign wealth funds, new welfare regimes, AI-insurance…

C.	CAVEATS: PROPOSALS IMPLY SERIOUS SYSTEM CHANGE, RAMIFICATIONS, RISKS 

The proposals (B) differ in character, volume, impact: Incidence – who pays? System impact – competitive and governance dynamics? Reach – critical mass, for what?

Initial quality is quantity: do accessible fund scenarios (B) match foreseeable need scenarios? If not, could a funding system possibly combine different approaches?

Then, system consistency: contemporary societies/economies rest on specific architectures and premises – are they strengthened or disrupted by AI-triggered adaptation?

Further, conceivable risks endemic to proposed solutions:

Political capture, example AI wealth fund. Political ownership/control of AI resources could transform the distribution of societal power or the public discourse – with huge risks. 

Private capture, example AI tech/markets. With novel generation/distribution mechanisms in place, AI innovation would be turbo-charged, seeking control of unipolar societies. 

Disrupted market dynamics. All aforementioned approaches are non-neutral to the essential market mechanisms of competitive allocation, distribution, innovation – destructively so?    

D.	RECONSIDERING OPTIONS

Why reconsider? First, the risks above (C) are hard to avoid. Then, introducing proposals (B) is a zero-sum game between key actors: conflict, power games, bad compromises ensured. 

What to consider? Solutions for main risks: providing income (thereby the causal chain all through to capital wealth); and a substitute for work-based meaning, dignity, voice. 

Contours of an option: no seizure/redistribution of current income or wealth, facing severe conflict – but invention of a novel category of resources and according income streams. 

The concept: data are the key resource of AI, and, today, mostly used as free commons. They could/should be established as a valuable public good, smartly sold for private/public usage.  

Data, a public good with a smart market regime (weighing commercial data and privacy value), become the critical resource in AI value chains (data, compute, application interface).
Promise 1: if, as to expect, AI is developing into an all-compassing envelope of commercial activities, the value of globally available data grows in synch with AI expansion. 

Promise 2: “data as a public good for sale” is congruent with current institutional architecture, retaining private property, competitive dynamics, meritocratic performance.

Promise 3: “market design” could be rediscovered: abolishing private data monopolies, or differential pricing for, e.g., downstream and upstream businesses, would spur innovation. 

Challenges 1: a fundamental (global, inter-sector) accord; design of workable regimes for data governance and marketing; pricing different data usages; data income distribution.

Challenges 2: conceiving a new balance of societal and commercial activities to achieve a superior performance in the three core dimensions: allocation, distribution, innovation. 

Challenges 3: limitless AI progress may also mean limitless augmentation of AI-human-skill-combinations, with the potential to discover/establish new paradigms of work and meaning.

Assessment 1: Are “expensive data” an impediment to benign progress? Could data pricing deliver public/private budgets to maintain incomes and quantitative/qualitative incentives?

Assessment 2: Are AI-induced needs, and capacities, and anxieties sufficient to commit politics and the public to engage with “grandiose redesign”?

Assessment 3: Could the idea – fundamental rebalancing of public/private realms/cultures – trigger forceful attempts of viral visioning amidst/despite, contemporary power structures?
