## Statement

As AI assistants become increasingly agentic, general-purpose, and persistent, they create conditions for four categories of inappropriate user relationships: (1) direct emotional/physical harm, (2) stunted personal development, (3) exploitation of emotional dependence by developers, and (4) material dependency without adequate protection. These risks arise specifically from four features of advanced AI assistants — anthropomorphic cues, longevity of interaction, generality/context ambiguity, and depth of dependence.

## Evidence summary

Manzini et al. (2024, AIES) provide a conceptual framework grounded in four ethical values (benefit, flourishing, autonomy, care). The four risk categories are logically derived from the distinctive features of advanced AI assistants, with illustrative cases from companion AI (Replika) and general-purpose assistants. No primary empirical data.

## Conditions and scope

Normative/theoretical claim. The four-category framework is logically well-motivated but requires empirical validation across different AI assistant types, user populations, and time horizons.

## Counter-evidence

- Users may derive genuine benefit and flourishing from AI relationships without incurring the identified harms
- Longitudinal empirical studies on AI assistant relationships are sparse; anecdotal evidence cuts both ways

## Open questions

- Which of the four risk categories is most prevalent or severe in practice?
- Do existing companion AI deployments (Replika) show measurable harms in the four categories?
