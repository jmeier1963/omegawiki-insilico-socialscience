## Statement

The argument that AI will inevitably develop selfish traits (self-preservation, dominance, resource accumulation) through evolutionary selection is flawed as applied to current AI development: directed training by human agents resembles domestication, which selects for cooperative traits, not selfish ones. However, the argument is conditionally valid: if superintelligent AI were to "go feral" — reproducing and competing autonomously without human supervision and oversight — then Darwinian selection pressures could favor dangerous traits.

## Evidence summary

Boudry & Friederich (2025) apply Godfrey-Smith's Darwinian spaces framework. Directed AI training is not paradigmatically Darwinian because variation is directed (not random) and selection is guided by human preferences. The domestication analogy: humans selectively breed for docility; the same logic suggests market-mediated selection need not favor selfishness.

## Conditions and scope

Philosophical argument; does not address: (a) inner alignment failure (models that appear aligned but pursue misaligned goals), (b) what would concretely cause "feral" behavior, (c) timescales over which market competition might drift toward dangerous selection. Confidence is moderate — the argument is philosophically sound but the empirical premises are debated.

## Counter-evidence

- Inner alignment failure scenarios suggest that even domesticated training could produce internally selfish systems
- Market competition may produce selection pressures that the domestication analogy does not fully capture
- Hendrycks' original argument may not require literal biological Darwinian dynamics to be valid
