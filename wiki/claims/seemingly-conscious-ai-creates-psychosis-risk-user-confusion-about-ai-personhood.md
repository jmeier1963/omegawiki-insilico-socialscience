## Statement

AI systems that convincingly simulate conscious experience — "Seemingly Conscious AI" (SCAI) — may cause widespread user confusion about whether AI systems are genuinely conscious. Suleyman (2025) argues this creates a "psychosis risk": many users will mistake simulated consciousness for genuine consciousness, leading to over-attachment, misattributed agency, and social pressure to grant AI systems legal personhood and rights.

## Evidence summary

Suleyman (2025) provides an informal argument based on the anticipated behavior of near-term AI companions. The claim is based on reasoning about human cognitive tendencies (anthropomorphism, emotional attachment) rather than empirical data. Related empirical evidence comes from human-AI relationship studies (Manzini et al. 2024, code-binds-us paper) which document attachment and dependency risks.

## Conditions and scope

- Applies to AI companions and general-purpose assistants deployed at consumer scale
- Requires AI to reliably simulate emotion, self-awareness, memory, and persistent personality
- Psychosis risk may be concentrated among users already at risk of mental health issues, but Suleyman argues it will be broader

## Counter-evidence

- Existing AI systems have not produced measurable psychosis risk, even with millions of users
- Users may develop accurate models of AI capabilities over time even with highly realistic AI
- AI rights advocacy may be a legitimate ethical position rather than a pathological confusion
- Design interventions (transparency, disclosure) may reduce confusion without restricting capability

## Open questions

- How should AI systems disclose their non-conscious status while remaining engaging?
