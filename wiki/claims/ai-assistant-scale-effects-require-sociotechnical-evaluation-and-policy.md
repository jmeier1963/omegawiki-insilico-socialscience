## Statement

When AI assistants are deployed to large user populations, emergent effects arise at the multi-agent and societal levels — including coordination dynamics, impacts on democratic institutions, epistemic monoculture, and inequitable access — that cannot be detected by evaluating individual user interactions or technical safety benchmarks. This makes sociotechnical evaluation methods and proactive policy frameworks necessary components of responsible AI assistant deployment.

## Evidence summary

Gabriel et al. (2024) provide the most comprehensive published analysis of this claim to date. Their 274-page synthesis by 30+ authors at Google DeepMind identifies the gap between technical benchmark evaluation and the full risk surface of AI assistant deployment as a central finding. They document specific failure modes at the individual (manipulation, trust miscalibration), multi-agent (adversarial coordination, systemic bias amplification), and societal (misinformation, power concentration, equity) levels. The call for holistic sociotechnical evaluation is stated as an explicit recommendation.

## Conditions and scope

- Applies specifically to general-purpose AI assistants deployed at consumer scale (millions of users)
- Assumes persistent, personalized, agentic AI assistants (not narrow task tools)
- Policy coordination argument is strongest for frontier AI assistants; less clear for narrow deployments

## Counter-evidence

- Survey claim; no empirical demonstrations of specific emergent failure modes from AI assistant deployment at scale
- Some multi-agent coordination risks may be architectural (solvable by design), not inherently policy-requiring
- Existing platform governance frameworks (for social media, search) may extend to AI assistants without requiring entirely new approaches
