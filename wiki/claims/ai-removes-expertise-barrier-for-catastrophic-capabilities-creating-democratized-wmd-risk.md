## Statement

Powerful AI democratizes access to catastrophic capabilities — especially biological weapons — in a way that is qualitatively different from all previous dual-use technology risks. Previous mass-casualty technologies (nuclear weapons, advanced pathogens) required state-level resources or deep specialized expertise. AI can remove the expertise barrier while leaving only raw material access as the constraint, potentially enabling individuals or small groups to create weapons capable of killing millions.

## Evidence summary

Amodei (2026) identifies this as one of five primary risk categories from powerful AI, arguing it is novel in kind rather than degree. The argument is that nuclear weapons required massive industrial infrastructure; advanced pathogens required deep virology expertise; AI removes the expert knowledge barrier while materials (pathogens, precursors) may remain accessible. The risk is characterized as asymmetric: AI safety training is imperfect and jailbreaks exist, while defensive monitoring has a detection lag.

Zhang, Knight et al. (Scale AI/SecureBio 2026) provide the strongest empirical evidence to date: in a multi-model, multi-benchmark human uplift study, novices with LLM access achieved 4.16× higher accuracy than internet-only controls on biosecurity-relevant tasks, outperforming human experts on 3 of 4 benchmarks with available expert baselines. 89.6% of participants reported little difficulty obtaining dual-use information despite safety filters. The paper notes that outright refusals may be less effective deterrents than plausible-but-misleading responses.

## Conditions and scope

- Applies specifically to AI models with expert-level biological/chemical knowledge
- Requires AI safety training to be bypassable (jailbreaks, fine-tuning, open-weight models)
- Does not require AI to be generally superintelligent — domain-expert-level capability in biology may be sufficient
- Partially mitigated by defensive applications of AI (early detection, countermeasure development)

## Counter-evidence

- Expertise may not have been the main barrier — raw material access and operational capability may dominate
- AI labs can implement biosecurity filters; governments can regulate dangerous bio-AI applications
- Defensive AI (biosurveillance, early detection) may outpace offensive AI capability
- Current AI models already have bio-safety restrictions; this risk is acknowledged and being addressed
