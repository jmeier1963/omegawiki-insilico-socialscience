## Statement

Under a person-affecting ethical framework (caring about the wellbeing of currently existing people, not potential future people), decision-theoretic models incorporating safety progress over time, temporal discounting, quality-of-life differentials, and concave QALY utilities suggest that developing superintelligence is often the optimal choice even when the probability of catastrophic outcomes is high. This is because forgoing superintelligence entails the ongoing costs of disease, aging, and death. The "risky surgery" analogy applies: not having the surgery also has fatal consequences.

## Evidence summary

Bostrom (2026, working paper) demonstrates this through formal models. The result is robust across several parameter settings but sensitive to the chosen ethical framework and assumed safety progress rate.

## Conditions and scope

The conclusion depends critically on the person-affecting ethical stance. Under total consequentialist or longtermist frameworks, the conclusion may differ dramatically. The optimal timing analysis does not engage with specific technical alignment pathways.

## Counter-evidence

- Under longtermist or total consequentialist frameworks, even small catastrophe probabilities may not be worth accepting given vast future populations at stake
- Safety progress may not increase monotonically with time; pausing research might not help
- The "brief pause before deployment" strategy assumes a level of coordination that may be infeasible
