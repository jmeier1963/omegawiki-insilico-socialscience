## Statement

For AI misuse risks, investing in societal resilience (distributed defenses across institutions and society) is more effective and less democratically costly than extraordinary government interventions (precautionary restrictions on model releases, AI nonproliferation regimes, licensing requirements), because there is no physical bottleneck equivalent to enriched uranium, making AI nonproliferation enforcement fragile and expensive.

## Evidence summary

Kapoor & Narayanan (2026) argue from the nuclear nonproliferation analogy: the enforceability of nuclear restrictions depends on a physical chokepoint that AI lacks. Open-weight models and widespread API access mean effective nonproliferation would require ongoing licensing authority over research and publications, risking permanent expansion of state control over digital technologies. Resilience distributes defenses and works regardless of which actors have access to capable AI.

## Conditions and scope

- This is a normative policy argument, not a tested empirical claim
- Confidence is moderate (0.45) given lack of empirical evidence for comparative effectiveness of resilience vs. restriction strategies
- May not apply if future AI capabilities are so powerful that no resilience strategy can absorb their misuse potential

## Counter-evidence

- Some argue precautionary restrictions provide useful breathing room even if imperfect
- Democratic oversight of powerful AI capabilities may be impossible without at least some regulatory chokepoints
