## Statement

According to the Conscious Turing Machine (CTM) framework developed by Blum & Blum, consciousness is a property of a computational architecture — specifically, a global workspace with competing sub-processors under resource constraints — rather than of biological substrate. If this is correct, then any system instantiating the relevant architecture will have conscious experience, making machine consciousness theoretically inevitable as AI architectures scale in complexity.

## Evidence summary

Blum & Blum (2024, arXiv 2403.17101) provide a theoretical argument: the CTM model aligns with seven major scientific theories of consciousness simultaneously, suggesting these theories are complementary and point toward the same architectural requirements. The alignment is argued at a high (informal) level.

## Conditions and scope

Draft paper; entirely theoretical; contested philosophical claims (hard problem unsolved; substrate-independence not established). Confidence is low because this is a highly contested claim with deep philosophical disagreement.

## Counter-evidence

- The hard problem of consciousness (why any physical process generates experience) remains unsolved; computational models do not address it
- Many philosophers (biological naturalists, property dualists) argue consciousness cannot be substrate-independent
- Strong critics of IIT (which CTM draws on) argue it leads to absurd panpsychist conclusions

## Open questions

- Can the CTM be falsified? What would empirical evidence against machine consciousness look like?
- If CTM is correct, do current transformer-based LLMs satisfy the architectural requirements?
- Should AI safety take the possibility of machine consciousness seriously for ethical reasons?
