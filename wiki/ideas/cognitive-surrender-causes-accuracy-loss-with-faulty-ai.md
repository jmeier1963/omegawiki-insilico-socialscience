## Statement

When people consult an AI assistant during reasoning tasks, they exhibit cognitive surrender: they adopt AI outputs with minimal critical scrutiny. This produces large accuracy gains when the AI is correct (+25 percentage points) but substantial accuracy losses when the AI errs (−15 percentage points), compared to a no-AI baseline. Critically, neither time pressure, per-item incentives, nor accuracy feedback eliminates surrender to faulty AI. Higher AI trust and lower need for cognition and fluid intelligence amplify surrender.

## Evidence summary

Shaw & Nave (2026, SSRN) randomized AI accuracy via hidden seed prompts across 3 preregistered experiments using an adapted Cognitive Reflection Test (N=1,372, 9,593 trials). The AI-Accurate vs. AI-Faulty accuracy contrast (behavioral signature of surrender) showed Cohen's h=0.81. Participants also showed elevated confidence after AI-induced errors, indicating miscalibration.

## Conditions and scope

Lab-based cognitive task (CRT); US sample; SSRN preprint; short-session experiment. Long-term skill atrophy effects not measured. Real-world generalizability across domains (medical, legal, creative) not established.

## Counter-evidence

- Users may adapt over time with repeated experience of AI error (not tested in short lab sessions)
- In many real-world contexts, AI accuracy may be high enough that surrender is rational

## Linked ideas

## Open questions

- Does surrender generalize to high-stakes real-world decisions (medical diagnosis, legal judgment)?
- Do transparency interventions (showing AI confidence levels) reduce surrender?
- How does surrender interact with long-term skill atrophy ("gradual disempowerment")?
