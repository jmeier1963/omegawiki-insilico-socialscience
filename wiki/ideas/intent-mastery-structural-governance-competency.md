## Motivation

The vision of individual mastery in a hybrid AI society requires three forms of proficiency: epistemic mastery (distinguishing correct from plausibly wrong), intent mastery (specifying objectives, constraints, and accountability logic so agents optimize within the right solution space), and social-ethical mastery (bearing responsibility for outcomes). Of these, intent mastery is the most directly connected to governance architecture: the competence to specify objectives and constraints for AI agents is also the competence required of regulators, compliance officers, and citizens writing the rules under which agents operate. Yet "intent mastery" — as a distinct, bounded competency — does not yet appear as a named category in workforce or regulatory frameworks. The closest existing work: Microsoft Research (2026) argues goals should be "first-class abstractions" in human-AI systems; the DOL AI Literacy Framework (Feb 2026) mandates prompt engineering and task delegation training; PRISM (practitioner framework, 2025) structures intent articulation as professional skill; the SHRM 8-Pillar Model (May 2026) includes delegation modes and oversight structures. All treat intent specification instrumentally (how to use AI better), not as a structural governance competency (what regulatory mandate ensures humans maintain the capacity to specify intent). The gap: no governance framework mandates verified intent mastery as a precondition for high-stakes AI deployment authorization.

## Hypothesis

Intent mastery — the capacity to specify objectives, constraints, trade-offs, and accountability logic precisely enough that AI agents optimize within the intended solution space rather than a proxy of it — is a structural governance competency that should be mandated as a pre-condition for high-stakes AI deployment authorization, analogous to the requirement for licensed professionals in other high-stakes domains. Without this mandate, the diffusion of AI decision-making into consequential domains (hiring, credit, medical triage, legal judgment, public policy) will proceed without institutional assurance that human principals can specify what they intend the AI to optimize.

## Approach sketch

1. **Conceptual differentiation**: Develop intent mastery as a distinct conceptual category, differentiated from (a) AI literacy (knowing how AI works), (b) prompt engineering (technical skill in eliciting desired outputs), and (c) epistemic mastery (calibrating confidence in AI outputs). Intent mastery specifically covers the capacity to specify: objectives (what to optimize), constraints (what cannot be violated), trade-offs (how to resolve conflicts between objectives), scope conditions (when the specification applies), and accountability logic (who bears responsibility for what outcomes). Draw on the Microsoft Research 2026 "goals as first-class abstractions" framework and Bostrom's principal-agent theory.

2. **Workforce framework mapping**: Map existing frameworks (DOL AI Literacy, SHRM 8-Pillar, SFIA AI Skills Taxonomy, UNESCO AI Competency Framework, PRISM) against the intent mastery conceptual definition. Identify what each framework covers and what it misses relative to the full intent mastery definition. Produce a "gap matrix" showing which components (objective specification, constraint specification, trade-off articulation, accountability logic) are covered vs. absent in existing frameworks.

3. **Regulatory design**: From the conceptual definition and gap matrix, design a governance mandate for intent mastery in high-stakes AI deployment contexts. The mandate structure: (a) define which deployment contexts require verified intent mastery (hiring, credit, medical, legal, public policy); (b) specify the certification design — what tasks must a practitioner demonstrate mastery on, assessed independently of AI assistance; (c) specify the audit mechanism — how regulators verify that practitioners maintain intent mastery over time, not just at initial certification; (d) specify the liability structure — what regulatory consequences follow from AI harm attributable to inadequate intent specification.

4. **Analogical grounding**: Ground the governance mandate in analogical precedents from other high-stakes professional licensing requirements. Candidates: aviation pilot type-rating requirements (must demonstrate manual control capability before authorized to operate automated systems), nuclear plant operator licensing (must understand underlying physics, not just automated control interface), medical prescribing authority (requires pharmacological understanding, not just prescription-writing ability). Identify which features of these licensing regimes are transferable to intent mastery certification for AI deployment.

5. **Pilot assessment design**: Design a pilot assessment battery for intent mastery — a small set of structured tasks where a practitioner must specify the objective, constraints, trade-offs, and accountability logic for a realistic AI deployment decision (e.g., automated hiring screen, credit model specification, medical triage prioritization). Validate the battery structure with 5-10 domain experts. Produce a publishable assessment instrument that regulatory bodies can adapt.

## Expected outcome

A formal conceptual framework for intent mastery as a governance competency, a gap analysis of existing workforce frameworks, a regulatory mandate design for verified intent mastery in high-stakes deployment contexts, and a pilot assessment battery. The output directly operationalizes the "intent mastery" concept from the individual mastery vision into actionable governance architecture — completing the link between individual competency and regulatory design that existing frameworks leave open.

## Risks

- Intent mastery may be too abstract to operationalize as a regulatory competency; the assessment design is genuinely difficult — specifying what counts as "correct" constraint specification requires domain expertise that varies by context
- Professional licensing mandates are politically contested; extending them to AI deployment contexts will face industry resistance and jurisdictional fragmentation
- The Microsoft Research "goals as first-class abstractions" framing is primarily technical (ML systems design), not governance; translating it to regulatory design requires non-trivial conceptual work
- Assessment design for intent mastery faces the same construct validity challenge as any complex professional competency — inter-rater reliability and face validity are hard to achieve simultaneously
