## Motivation

The argument that redistribution mechanisms must be built into AI governance frameworks from the start — not retrofitted — rests on the premise that visible redistribution causally improves public support for governance. This premise is plausible: the AJPS (2026) paper linking AI anxiety to anti-globalization microfoundations suggests that concentrated gains without redistribution will activate the same anti-reform coalitions that blocked carbon pricing. South Korea's citizen dividend (May 2026) and the Bores AI dividend proposal (April 2026) are the first concrete political experiments in this direction. However, no controlled experimental evidence links redistribution framing to governance legitimacy. The claim that "legitimacy is built in, not retrofitted" is normative; the causal mechanism — redistribution → public support for governance → durable policy coalitions — has not been empirically tested. Without this evidence, the argument remains susceptible to the standard response that redistribution and governance are separable and that bundling them reduces the political feasibility of both.

## Hypothesis

The governance legitimacy deficit of AI regulation is partly attributable to perceived concentration of AI gains. Visible redistribution mechanisms bundled with governance proposals (dividend, taxation-and-reinvestment, public infrastructure) causally increase public support for those governance frameworks, above and beyond the effect of simply informing publics about AI risks or benefits. The causal mechanism is structural fairness signaling: redistribution communicates that governance design reflects the interests of the non-beneficiary majority, not only technology incumbents. This effect is strongest among income quintiles most displaced by AI adoption and is moderated by partisan identity (stronger in non-partisan and center-left identifiers).

## Approach sketch

1. **Pre-registered vignette experiment** (n ≈ 800, nationally representative survey panel): Randomize whether respondents see identical AI governance proposals (compute threshold regulation, mandatory disclosure requirements, deployment pause provisions) with or without bundled redistribution mechanisms. Three redistribution conditions: (A) no redistribution (governance only); (B) royalty/dividend model ("AI companies pay royalties on training data; distributed as annual dividend"); (C) public investment model ("AI productivity tax funds public education and infrastructure"). Redistribution amount held constant across B and C to isolate mechanism type.

2. **Outcome measures**: Primary — support for the governance proposal (7-point scale); secondary — perceived fairness of AI governance, trust in government AI policy, willingness to accept regulatory costs (higher prices, slower deployment), and coalition identity ("I would support politicians who champion this"). Include manipulation checks for redistribution salience and AI competence perceptions to isolate the fairness mechanism.

3. **Heterogeneity analysis**: Pre-registered subgroup analysis by income quintile, partisan identity, AI exposure (job displacement risk, AI tool usage), and country (run in US + one EU country). Predict: strongest redistribution effect in lower-income quintiles and in non-partisan identifiers.

4. **Live case study**: Use South Korea citizen dividend (May 2026) as a natural experiment anchor — track approval ratings for AI governance policies in South Korea before/after dividend announcement, compared to matched control countries. This provides ecological validity for the vignette findings.

5. **Mechanism test**: Mediation analysis — test whether the redistribution effect on governance support operates through perceived fairness (structural signaling mechanism) vs. direct economic self-interest (income effect). Distinguish: does redistribution improve governance support because it is fair, or because respondents personally benefit?

## Expected outcome

Redistribution-bundled governance proposals will show 15-25% higher support among lower-income quintiles and non-partisan identifiers, compared to governance-only proposals. The effect will be mediated by perceived fairness rather than direct economic self-interest, supporting the structural fairness signaling interpretation. The South Korea case study will show increased governance approval following dividend announcement. This would provide the first causal experimental evidence for the claim that redistribution legitimizes governance rather than merely accompanying it.

## Risks

- Vignette experiments may not translate to real policy tradeoffs — respondents may not feel the true costs of governance restrictions
- Redistribution framing may shift perceived government competence (not just fairness), requiring careful manipulation checks to isolate the legitimacy mechanism
- "Governance legitimacy" is multi-dimensional; construct validity requires pre-specified measurement model to avoid demand effects
- South Korea case study faces confounding from concurrent political events (presidential transition, technology industry dynamics)
- The effect may not replicate across political contexts with different redistribution norms (e.g., US vs. Germany)
