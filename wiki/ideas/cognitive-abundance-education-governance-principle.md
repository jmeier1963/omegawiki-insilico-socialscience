## Motivation

The argument that transforming education belongs inside an AI governance plan — not downstream of it — rests on a strong but underspecified claim: that the shift from knowledge-distribution to governance-of-learning requires structural reform of educational institutions, not merely incremental AI literacy additions. This structural claim needs a theoretical foundation. Adjacent frameworks exist: Acemoglu et al.'s (2026) Dynamic Knowledge Ecosystem Model shows that AI-driven cognitive availability can erode learning incentives (a "knowledge collapse" dynamic); the EdTech Hub's Three Horizons Framework (2025) distinguishes tactical AI additions (Horizon 1) from structural redesign (Horizon 3 — "Education in the Age of AI"); UNESCO/OECD 2025 policy documents address AI-era skills but remain within an incremental AI-literacy frame. What is missing is a unified theoretical frame that takes "cognitive abundance" — the AI-era analog of information abundance at the reasoning level — as a first-order concept, derives structural (not incremental) implications for educational governance, and proposes measurable institutional redesign criteria. Without this frame, the argument that education transformation belongs *inside* governance plans rather than downstream remains a programmatic statement without structural grounding.

## Hypothesis

"Cognitive abundance" — the condition in which sophisticated reasoning outputs are available on demand from AI systems at near-zero marginal cost — represents a qualitative shift in the epistemic environment of education that renders incremental AI-literacy additions a category error. Just as information abundance made attention (not information) the scarce resource, cognitive abundance makes judgment (not knowledge) the scarce cognitive resource. Educational governance under cognitive abundance must therefore be reorganized around the development and preservation of judgment — the capacity to act well under uncertainty, weigh competing values, and maintain epistemic agency — rather than around knowledge distribution. This reorganization requires structural institutional change (governance structures, curriculum architecture, assessment design, teacher role definition) rather than incremental AI literacy additions.

## Approach sketch

1. **Theoretical framework development**: Develop "cognitive abundance" as a formal concept, distinguishing it from information abundance and knowledge abundance. Key elements: (a) reasoning outputs available at near-zero marginal cost; (b) quality of AI reasoning outputs approaching human expert level across increasing domains; (c) resulting shift in cognitive scarcity from knowledge → judgment → what kind of judgment. Draw on Acemoglu's knowledge collapse formalism to model the incentive effects of cognitive abundance on learning behavior.

2. **Diagnostic taxonomy**: Map existing educational governance frameworks (OECD Learning Compass, EdTech Hub Three Horizons, UNESCO AI in Education guidelines, OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026) against the cognitive abundance concept. Classify each framework as: incremental (Horizon 1-2 — adds AI literacy within existing knowledge-distribution architecture) vs. structural (Horizon 3 — redesigns governance toward judgment preservation). Identify which specific institutional features distinguish judgment-preserving from cognitive-offloading architectures.

3. **Design criteria derivation**: From the theoretical frame and taxonomy, derive measurable institutional redesign criteria for "judgment-preserving" educational governance: (a) assessment design criteria (measures capability after AI scaffolding removed, per "Agentivism" learning theory arXiv:2604.07813); (b) curriculum architecture criteria (protected judgment-development phases analogous to "AI-free training phases" in the governance text); (c) teacher role criteria (orchestrator of judgment development vs. knowledge distributor); (d) institutional governance criteria (who decides what counts as judgment-preserving?).

4. **Comparative analysis**: Apply the design criteria to 3-4 contrasting educational systems (e.g., Finland's phenomenological curriculum, Singapore's structured assessment system, university liberal arts core, vocational training systems) to assess which existing governance structures are most compatible with cognitive abundance governance, and which face the deepest restructuring challenges.

5. **Policy translation**: Translate the framework into specific governance mandates: what must AI governance plans mandate for educational institutions (minimum protected judgment-development time, assessment requirements, AI-tool disclosure requirements) as part of the AI governance architecture itself — not as downstream education policy.

## Expected outcome

A publishable theoretical framework for cognitive abundance as an education governance concept, with a diagnostic taxonomy of existing governance frameworks (incremental vs. structural), a set of measurable institutional redesign criteria for judgment-preserving governance, and a comparative analysis of 3-4 education systems. The output directly operationalizes the claim that education transformation belongs inside AI governance plans with specific mandates, and fills the "cognitive abundance" gap in both the AI governance and education policy literatures.

## Risks

- "Cognitive abundance" may be contested as a frame: critics may argue that AI reasoning outputs are not yet at the threshold that makes incremental approaches insufficient
- "Judgment" as the target of educational governance is philosophically contested; operationalizing it without collapsing it into measurable skills (which would make the argument circular) is difficult
- The institutional redesign criteria may be too abstract to be actionable for policymakers without further empirical grounding
- Acemoglu's knowledge collapse model makes specific formal assumptions that may not transfer cleanly to the educational governance context
