## Problem setting

Deciding whether a given AI system's apparent grasp of consciousness-related concepts (subjective experience, qualia, an inner life) reflects something like genuine understanding, as opposed to fluent repetition of what humans have written about their own consciousness — a distinction that matters for AI-welfare and moral-status questions but that ordinary conversational probing cannot settle, since a system trained on enough human text about minds can produce competent-sounding answers regardless of whether anything is actually going on inside it.

## Mechanism

Probe a system with questions about subjective experience and evaluate whether it displays an innate, unprompted grasp of consciousness concepts — reasoning about hypothetical scenarios involving consciousness in ways that suggest genuine conceptual understanding rather than retrieval of memorized human descriptions. The test's validity depends entirely on the system under test not having been extensively pretrained on human writing about mind, sentience, and subjective experience, since such pretraining would let a system produce plausible-sounding answers by imitation rather than by any relevant internal process.

## Procedure

1. Confirm the candidate system has not been massively pretrained on human text discussing consciousness, subjective experience, or philosophy of mind (a precondition, not a step that can be worked around).
2. Present the system with questions and hypothetical scenarios probing consciousness-related concepts it was not explicitly trained to answer.
3. Evaluate whether responses display an innate, unprompted grasp of the relevant concepts — reasoning that goes beyond surface-level pattern completion — versus answers that could be produced by imitation of training data alone.
4. Interpret a positive result as evidence worth taking seriously, not as proof; the test is a filter for further scrutiny, not a verdict.

## Assumptions

- Consciousness-relevant concepts can be probed through language interaction at all.
- A system's lack of prior exposure to human writing about consciousness can be reliably established or engineered.
- Genuine conceptual grasp of subjective experience is distinguishable, in principle, from sufficiently sophisticated imitation.

## Limitations

- **Explicitly inapplicable to the systems most people currently want to test.** Large language models trained on internet-scale text have "hopelessly contaminated" answers about consciousness, by the test designers' own admission — ACT cannot be used on today's chatbots at all, leaving no operational method for the case that motivates most public interest in AI consciousness.
- No worked scoring criterion, threshold, or published validation study accompanies the test in its originating source; "innate, unprompted grasp" is not operationally defined.
- Even where applicable, a positive result establishes something short of consciousness (sophisticated conceptual reasoning about consciousness), leaving a gap between passing the test and the underlying metaphysical question.

## Tradeoff profile

Trades broad applicability for a cleaner inferential path: by restricting itself to systems without consciousness-related training exposure, ACT avoids the contamination problem that makes any consciousness-probing of LLMs uninterpretable — but this restriction excludes essentially all systems currently in public deployment and debate. It functions today more as a marker of a research program and a demonstration that operationalization is possible in principle than as a tool with active applications.
