## Definition

Digital sovereignty refers to the ability of a state (or bloc of states) to control their critical digital infrastructure, data flows, and technology stacks without dependence on foreign platforms, cloud providers, or AI systems. In the AI context, digital sovereignty encompasses: regulatory sovereignty (ability to set rules for AI use), data sovereignty (control over data processed by AI systems), and infrastructure sovereignty (domestic or allied control of compute and AI platforms).

## Intuition

A state that stores citizen data on US cloud servers and runs critical services on foreign AI models cannot independently set privacy policy, cannot withstand foreign sanctions, and may receive AI outputs aligned with foreign rather than domestic values. Digital sovereignty aims to restore the same territorial control over digital infrastructure that states have over physical infrastructure.

## Formal notation

Not formally modeled; assessed qualitatively through infrastructure ownership metrics, data residency laws, and platform market share.

## Variants

- **Regulatory sovereignty**: ability to set binding rules for digital services (EU approach: GDPR, AI Act, DMA/DSA)
- **Infrastructure sovereignty**: domestic ownership of cloud, compute, and AI platforms (EuroStack approach)
- **Data sovereignty**: control over where citizen/government data is stored and processed
- **AI model sovereignty**: ability to deploy domestically developed AI models rather than relying on foreign frontier models

## Comparison

| Approach | EU (Regulatory) | EuroStack (Infrastructure) | China (State control) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary mechanism | Rules and standards | Owned platforms | State-owned infrastructure |
| International effect | Brussels Effect | Limited | None beyond China |
| Innovation impact | Moderate constraint | High constraint | Complex |

## When to use

When analyzing: EU AI policy, US-China tech competition, cloud market structure, AI governance frameworks, data protection policy, AI Act implementation.

## Known limitations

- "Sovereignty" concept can be stretched to justify protectionism
- Infrastructure sovereignty is very expensive; often lags frontier capabilities significantly
- Data sovereignty requirements may impede legitimate cross-border research collaboration

## Open problems

- Is regulatory sovereignty (EU approach) sufficient for AI, or is infrastructure sovereignty necessary?
- Can democratic sovereignty be achieved without authoritarian-style state control of platforms?

## My understanding

Digital sovereignty is the central concept in European AI governance debates. The tension between regulatory and infrastructure approaches reflects genuine strategic uncertainty about whether rules alone can prevent dependence on foreign AI infrastructure.
