## Definition

A framework (Narayanan & Kapoor) holding that AI should be analysed like prior general-purpose technologies: its societal and economic impact is governed less by raw capability than by the slow, contingent processes of **adoption, diffusion, and the structural bottlenecks** (regulatory, organisational, adversarial, human) that sit between a capability and its real-world effect. Capability advances do not automatically produce the predicted transformation.

## Intuition

A model passing the bar exam or writing most of a codebase tells you little about whether legal fees fall or engineers are laid off. Between "the model can do the task" and "the economy changes" lie layers that capability alone doesn't penetrate: laws about who may practice, the need to decide *what* to build and *deliver* it accountably, and adversarial settings where everyone getting better cancels out. Treating AI as normal technology means studying those layers, not extrapolating from benchmark scores.

## Formal notation

Informal. Useful decompositions: the **decide–execute–deliver sandwich** (AI compresses *execute*; *decide* and *deliver* resist); and the **bottleneck taxonomy** (regulatory barriers, adversarial dynamics, human involvement).

## Variants

- **Decide-execute-deliver sandwich** — AI automates the middle (execution) layer of knowledge work while the surrounding decision and delivery layers resist (software-engineering essay).
- **Three bottlenecks to transformation** — regulatory barriers, adversarial dynamics, and human involvement block capability→cost-reduction (legal-services essay).
- **AI washing** — attributing layoffs/decisions to AI when the real drivers are financial or organisational.

## Comparison

Opposes "capability-threshold → mass disruption" narratives and CEO "country of geniuses" framings. Convergent with Anthropic's empirical [[human-ai-division-labor-agentic-work]] ("humans decide what, agents decide how") and with the [[ai-science-adoption-gap]] (diffusion, not capability, gates impact).

## When to use

Whenever a capability result is used to predict economic/labor outcomes — to ask what adoption and bottleneck factors stand in between.

## Known limitations

A structural argument that can understate genuinely fast transitions; mostly US-framed; risks being read as complacency about disruption.

## Open problems

Which bottlenecks erode (and how fast) as agents take on decide/deliver layers; how to measure adoption-gated impact empirically.

## My understanding

The framework's strength is forcing the capability→impact inference to be earned rather than assumed. Its risk is symmetry with the pacing-problem rhetoric: the same "look past the hype" move can rationalise either calm or alarm.
