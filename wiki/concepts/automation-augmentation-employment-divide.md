## Definition

The empirical pattern that generative AI's labor-market impact depends on whether it *automates* or *augments* an occupation's tasks: employment falls (most sharply for early-career workers, ages ~22–25) in AI-exposed occupations where AI substitutes for labor, but holds or grows where AI complements it — with early adjustment showing up in employment (fewer hires) rather than in wages.

## Intuition

"AI exposure" alone doesn't predict job loss; what matters is substitution vs. complementarity. Where AI does the task (customer service scripts, boilerplate code), firms hire fewer juniors; where AI makes a worker more productive without replacing them, employment can rise. Because wages are sticky, the first-order effect appears as reduced hiring of entry-level workers — the "canaries" — rather than pay cuts, making it easy to miss.

## Variants

- **By career stage:** concentrated in early-career (22–25); experienced workers buffered (except, with weaker buffering, for some non-college workers).
- **By AI use type:** automating uses → declines; augmenting uses → stable/growth (measured via whether LLM queries substitute or complement occupational tasks).
- **Margin of adjustment:** employment (quantity) vs. compensation (price) — early evidence is quantity-first.

## Comparison

Grounds the macro [[post-labor-economy]] and financial-fragility ([[ai-displacement-market-fragility]]) arguments in micro payroll evidence. Distinct from [[self-service-labour-displacement]], which is a specific channel (customers self-serving via AI); this concept is the occupation-level automation/augmentation split. It refines generic "AI will take jobs" claims into a testable, sign-dependent prediction.

## Known limitations

- Evidence is observational and from one payroll dataset (ADP); exposure and automation/augmentation are proxies.
- Identifies differential (relative) effects, not the full general-equilibrium employment level.
- Early window (through late 2025); wage effects may emerge later, and augmenting uses may convert to automating ones.

## Open problems

- Are entry-level "canaries" a leading indicator of broader displacement or a bounded level shift?
- Does augmentation durably protect jobs as models improve?
- Why employment-first rather than wage-first adjustment, and is the wage stickiness temporary?

## My understanding

The distinction that earns its keep is automation vs. augmentation: it turns "AI and jobs" from a slogan into a sign-dependent, measurable claim, and predicts *who* gets hit (early-career, automating occupations) rather than a blanket effect. The employment-not-wages result is the part most likely to be underestimated by anyone watching aggregate unemployment or pay, because the damage shows up as jobs that were never posted.
