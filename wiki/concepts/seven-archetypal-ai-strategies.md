## Definition

A taxonomy of seven mutually exclusive strategic postures a state (in the originating analysis, the United States) could adopt toward the development of advanced AI and superintelligence, organized into three families by their judgment on human-AI coexistence: **Coexistence strategies** — A. Dominance (race to an exclusive lead, suppress rivals), B. Co-Development (joint governance of superintelligence with rivals, CERN-for-AI style), C. Preparedness (informal coexistence mobilization via voluntary commitments, phased deployment, incident response); **Denial strategies** — D. Moratorium (a verifiable global halt above capability thresholds), E. Deterrence (suppress rival programs by threat or force, e.g. Mutually Assured AI Malfunction), F. Continuity of Society (hardened, distributed human settlements as a last-resort survival fallback); and standing alone, **G. Acceleration** (maximize development and diffusion, treating governance friction as the larger danger). Each archetype is defined by a canonical public proponent/statement, a "win state," and a "structural pathology" — the way it degenerates even when functioning as designed. Which archetype is favored depends on five pivotal uncertainties: danger proximity, coexistence feasibility, restraint feasibility, decisive-strategic-advantage attainability, and suppression feasibility.

## Intuition

Public AI-strategy debate is usually conducted as a clash between named camps (accelerationists, pause advocates, race-to-the-top hawks) without a shared map of what, precisely, they disagree about. This taxonomy supplies that map: every strategic disagreement can be located as a difference in judgment on one or more of the five pivots, and every strategy's "obvious flaw" can be named precisely as its structural pathology rather than left as vague unease.

## Variants

- **Coexistence family (A-C)**: accepts that humans and advanced AI can coexist with agency intact; strategies differ on how to build the enabling ecosystem and security architecture.
- **Denial family (D-F)**: judges coexistence infeasible (for now or permanently); strategies differ on whether restraint is cooperative (Moratorium), coercive (Deterrence), or abandoned in favor of pure survival (Continuity of Society).
- **Acceleration (G)**: rejects the premise that a security architecture is needed at all, treating markets and rapid iteration as the safety mechanism.
- **The five pivotal uncertainties** as a sub-taxonomy: danger proximity, coexistence feasibility, restraint feasibility, decisive strategic advantage, suppression feasibility — each resolving asymmetrically (four can be confirmed but not ruled out; suppression infeasibility is the one pivot that can be conclusively demonstrated, by a rival succeeding despite it).

## Comparison

- Distinct from a simple hawk/dove or accelerationist/decelerationist spectrum: the taxonomy separates *goal* (coexistence vs. denial vs. acceleration) from *mechanism* (unilateral vs. cooperative vs. coercive), producing seven distinguishable postures rather than a single axis.
- Complements [[mutually-assured-compute-destruction]], which operationalizes one specific mechanism (compute-hostage-based deterrence) for archetype E (Deterrence); the taxonomy situates that mechanism within a broader strategic-choice framework rather than treating it as a standalone proposal.
- Distinct from the [[freedom-action-ai-strategy]], which is not an eighth archetype but a meta-strategy for deciding when and how to select among the seven.

## Known limitations

- Asserted as exhaustive of "current debates" rather than derived from a formal enumeration; hybrid or genuinely novel strategies outside this space are not ruled out.
- Each archetype's "canonical proponent" is a single representative statement (e.g., Aschenbrenner for Dominance, Hendrycks/Schmidt/Wang for Deterrence); real-world advocates of a given archetype may diverge substantially from that canonical framing.
- The five-pivot resolution asymmetry (four pivots can be confirmed but never disconfirmed) means the taxonomy structurally cannot guarantee any strategic question gets resolved in finite time except suppression feasibility.

## Open problems

- Can the five pivotal uncertainties be operationalized with measurable indicators, so that "compelling evidence" for commitment has an agreed-upon threshold rather than remaining a matter of judgment?
- How stable is the archetype taxonomy against emerging developments — e.g., would a verified international AI-safety-evaluation regime blur the Preparedness/Co-Development boundary?
- Do other major AI powers (China, EU, UK) map onto the same seven-archetype space, or does the taxonomy implicitly encode U.S.-specific strategic assumptions?

## My understanding

The taxonomy's value lies less in the specific seven labels than in forcing every AI-strategy argument to specify which of the five pivots it is making a claim about — this converts otherwise talking-past-each-other disagreements (e.g., "racing is reckless" vs. "restraint is naive") into falsifiable-in-principle disputes about danger proximity, coexistence feasibility, or suppression feasibility specifically. Its biggest vulnerability is exactly its biggest strength: by construction it treats the strategic-choice space as fully covered by these five variables, which risks understating the possibility that AI strategy is shaped by considerations (domestic political economy, alliance structures, bureaucratic path-dependence) the framework doesn't capture.
