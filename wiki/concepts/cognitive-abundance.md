## Definition

A condition, produced by broadly capable AI (LLMs, autonomous agents, predictive/generative tools), in which the marginal cost of producing a substantial category of cognitive output — strategic analysis, market forecasts, risk assessments, synthesis, recommendation — approaches zero. It is the cognitive counterpart of Rifkin's "zero marginal cost society," applied specifically to knowledge and managerial labor rather than to information goods or physical production.

## Intuition

Modern corporations were architected as rational responses to *cognitive scarcity*: hierarchies, reporting chains, and a managerial class exist to aggregate, filter, and relay costly information. If AI can perform that relay-and-analysis function at negligible marginal cost, the scarcity premium on knowledge work erodes and the inherited architecture becomes not just inefficient but structurally mismatched to the economics of intelligence. The strategic question flips from "how do we attract and retain scarce knowledge workers" to "how do we orchestrate human-AI hybrids around comparative advantage."

## Variants

- **Operational cognitive abundance** — near-zero-cost automation of routine analytic/codifiable knowledge tasks (the well-studied case in labor economics).
- **Executive-grade cognitive abundance** — the paper's novel extension: the same collapse reaching strategic, managerial, and C-suite cognition, previously assumed automation-resistant.

## Comparison

Distinct from [[self-service-labour-displacement]], which is a demand-side mechanism (the *customer* self-serves and the intermediary job disappears); cognitive abundance is a supply-side cost collapse *inside* the firm that makes the cognitive input itself cheap. It is the micro/organizational premise whose macroeconomic endpoint is the [[post-labor-economy]] (labor share → 0), and it motivates the human-AI re-division of strategic work in [[human-ai-division-labor-agentic-work]].

## Known limitations

- Asserted from the capability frontier rather than measured: "marginal cost → zero" is a directional claim, not a quantified one, and ignores the substantial supervision, verification, and integration costs that human-AI workflows still incur.
- Treats "cognitive work" as fungible; in practice the cost collapse is highly uneven across task types, and high-stakes judgment may stay expensive.
- Originating source is a single non-peer-reviewed working paper, so the construct's empirical grounding is thin.

## Open problems

- How to measure the marginal cost of executive-grade cognition empirically, and how fast it is actually falling.
- Which cognitive functions retain a durable scarcity premium (the "Human Moat") as models improve.
- Whether abundance of cognition raises or lowers *aggregate* demand for human judgment (a Jevons-style question for knowledge work).

## My understanding

A useful one-line reframing: the corporation is a scarcity artifact, and AI removes the scarcity it was built around. The concept earns its keep by pushing the automation question up the org chart — from clerks to the C-suite — which is where most AI-and-work writing stops short. Its weakness is the same as most "marginal cost → zero" claims: it understates the new costs (verification, orchestration, accountability) that abundance creates.
