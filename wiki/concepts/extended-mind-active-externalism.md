## Definition

The extended mind thesis holds that cognitive processes — and, in some cases, mental states such as beliefs — are not confined to the brain or even the body, but can be partly constituted by external objects, tools, or other people when those external elements are coupled to the organism in a two-way interaction that jointly drives behavior the way internal cognition normally does. The operative test is the **parity principle**: if a part of the world functions in a way that, were the equivalent process occurring in the head, would unhesitatingly be called part of a cognitive process, then that part of the world is part of the cognitive process. This is "active externalism," distinguished from the passive externalism of Putnam and Burge (where external factors are distal, historical, and irrelevant to present processing) by the active, here-and-now, behavior-driving role the coupled external element plays.

## Intuition

A person using a pocket calculator, a notebook, or a neural implant to perform a task is functionally indistinguishable from a person performing the equivalent operation entirely in the head — the only apparent difference is the arbitrary location of the skin/skull boundary, which is precisely the thing under dispute. Otto, an Alzheimer's patient who reliably carries and consults a notebook, believes the museum is on 53rd Street in exactly the sense that Inga (who consults biological memory) does, because the notebook plays the same role — constantly available, automatically trusted, and action-guiding — that ordinary non-occurrent memory plays for Inga.

## Variants

- **Extended cognition**: processing operations (e.g., mental rotation, arithmetic) distributed across brain and environment, illustrated by pen-and-paper long multiplication, Scrabble tile rearrangement, and navigational instruments.
- **Extended belief**: dispositional beliefs partly constituted by reliably available, automatically endorsed external information stores (Otto's notebook), requiring — per the paper's four proposed criteria — constancy of use, direct availability, automatic endorsement on retrieval, and (more contestably) past conscious endorsement of the stored content.
- **Socially extended cognition**: beliefs or desires partly constituted by another person functioning in the same role a notebook plays for Otto — e.g., a spouse who reliably serves as a "memory bank," an accountant, or a collaborator — grounded in a high degree of trust, reliance, and accessibility rather than in a historical linguistic-community relationship (which is what instead grounds Burge-style content externalism).
- **Extended self**: the view that if belief and cognition extend beyond the skin, personal identity does too, since dispositional beliefs and standing traits already constitute part of who a person is regardless of whether they are occurrent.

## Comparison

- Distinct from Putnam/Burge semantic externalism: passive externalism concerns distal, historical facts fixing the *content* or *reference* of thought (physical duplicates behave identically despite differing histories); active externalism concerns present, causally efficacious coupling that directly drives differing behavior (a duplicate with a different external coupling, like Twin Otto with a mistaken notebook entry, behaves differently).
- Distinct from the "Naked Mind" objection's portability criterion (that true cognitive resources must be constantly carried and available regardless of local environment): the paper argues portability is satisfied by *reliable* coupling, not by strict internality — a reliably-carried calculator or notebook is coupled as dependably as on-board memory, and occasional decoupling (showering without the notebook, sleep) doesn't disqualify internal memory either.
- Distinct from the view that consciousness demarcates the cognitive: much cognitive processing (memory retrieval, linguistic processing, skill acquisition) is already agreed to be non-conscious, so externality is not disqualifying merely because it lacks the phenomenology of conscious thought.

## Known limitations

- Provides no categorical answer for graded or ambiguous cases (a rarely-consulted Filofax, one's relationship to the broader Internet, tampered or falsified external information) — extension is explicitly treated as a matter of degree that can be genuinely indeterminate.
- The past-conscious-endorsement criterion for extended belief is acknowledged by the originating paper to be its weakest and most contestable component.
- Offers no principled account of how to individuate competing candidate extensions (e.g., which of two habitually consulted resources "counts" when they conflict).

## Open problems

- How should the framework handle asymmetric, high-frequency but variably-reliable coupling with modern digital resources (search engines, AI assistants) that differ sharply from Otto's notebook in bandwidth, availability, and trust dynamics?
- What follows normatively (morally, legally) from the extended-self thesis — does interfering with someone's habitually-relied-upon external resources carry the same weight as interfering with their person, as the originating paper speculates?
- Can the parity principle be operationalized well enough to serve as a criterion in applied domains (e.g., assessing whether an AI-assisted decision process constitutes genuinely joint human-AI cognition, or mere AI substitution for human judgment)?

## My understanding

The parity principle's lasting force comes from putting the burden of proof squarely on anyone who wants to deny cognitive-extension status to a coupled system that otherwise satisfies all the functional criteria satisfied by uncontroversial internal cognition — merely gesturing at the skin/skull boundary begs the question the thesis is about. Its main contemporary relevance for this wiki is as the conceptual ancestor of current AI-cognitive-offloading debates: an AI assistant that is constant, immediately available, and automatically trusted meets the extended-mind criteria for genuine cognitive coupling far more thoroughly than a notebook ever could, which reframes questions about AI reliance not as "is the human still doing their own thinking" but as "what kind of extended cognitive system has been formed, and is it one that preserves or erodes the human partner's agency" — the latter question being exactly what concepts like gradual disempowerment and the agency-erosion foreclosure mechanism are trying to answer for AI specifically.
