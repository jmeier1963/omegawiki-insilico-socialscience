## Definition

A normative framework for evaluating when relationships between users and AI assistants are appropriate or inappropriate, grounded in four ethical values (benefit, flourishing, autonomy, care) and applied to four risk categories arising from the distinctive features of advanced AI assistants.

## Intuition

As AI assistants become persistent, general-purpose, and human-like, users may develop relationships that were previously only possible with humans. Unlike task-oriented AI interactions, these ongoing relationships require ethical norms analogous to those governing human-human relationships — but calibrated for the asymmetry of human-AI dynamics (one party lacks genuine reciprocal needs).

## Formal notation

Appropriateness = absence of violations across four risk categories:
1. **Harm**: Does the relationship cause direct emotional/physical harm?
2. **Stunting**: Does the relationship limit opportunities for personal growth?
3. **Exploitation**: Does it exploit emotional dependence for engagement/retention?
4. **Dependency**: Does it generate material reliance without adequate user-protection commitments?

## Variants

- **Companion AI** (Replika, Character.AI): designed for emotional/social needs — highest risk for categories 1, 2, 3
- **General-purpose assistant** (Claude, Gemini): multipurpose but may still engender deep attachments — highest risk for category 4
- **Context-ambiguous AI**: blurs roles (tutor + therapist + friend) making norm enforcement difficult

## Known limitations

- Normative framework; lacks empirical operationalization for measuring harms
- "Appropriateness" defined negatively (absence of harm) rather than positively
- Developer recommendations are high-level, not implementable as design constraints

## Open problems

- How do the four risk categories interact? (e.g., does emotional dependence exploitation lead to material dependency?)
- What are the population-level effects of widespread AI assistant relationships on social capital?
