## Definition

Big-tent AI safety (Narayanan & Kapoor, 2026) separates two diagnoses that share the "AI safety" label:

1. AI **amplifies existing systemic risks** (cyber, bio, accumulative institutional decay). These call for prevention and resilience.
2. AI development **is itself an existential risk**. This calls for alignment of superintelligence or a ban on building it.

A big-tent movement makes room for both diagnoses and puts first the policies on which they overlap. A small-tent movement is defined by the second diagnosis alone.

## Intuition

A generalized existential threat admits one policy lever, stopping development, and probabilistic framings ("10% extinction if we build it") reinforce that. Catastrophic risks from *existing* systems instead need "a hundred disparate interventions to shore up defenses" rather than "one mammoth, flashy wall." Because the two diagnoses prescribe different remedies, they compete for attention and resources. The authors call this **misdirection**, which is stronger than the familiar "distraction" objection.

## Variants

| Dimension | Big tent | Small tent |
|---|---|---|
| Beliefs | Includes x-risk skeptics focused on cyber, bio, accumulative risk and resilience | Centered on superintelligence |
| Values | Pluralist; does not presuppose EA/utilitarian frameworks; positive visions rather than panic | Ideologically homogeneous; at risk of a purity spiral |
| Policy | Transparency, liability, embedded evaluation; builds capacity for stronger measures (e.g. banning fully autonomous recursive self-improvement) by persuading skeptics | Policy by panic; deprioritizes "merely" catastrophic risks |

## Known limitations

- The two-cluster binary is admitted to be a simplification; many actors hold both views.
- The prediction that x-risk framing is counterproductive (through polarization and misdirection) is speculative and offered with explicit uncertainty.
- Leaves open how to adjudicate intermediate proposals (compute governance, RSI-without-oversight bans) that both camps may endorse for different reasons.

## Open problems

- Is the competition for resources between the two diagnoses empirically observable in funding and legislative attention?
- Can transparency and liability actually build the "muscle" for stronger interventions?
- Does a high-salience incident with direct harm to ordinary people close the concern/salience gap that the framing relies on?

## Relationship to foundations

Draws on social-movement theory (coalition breadth against ideological purity), the political psychology of salience versus concern, and resilience-oriented risk governance from pandemic preparedness and catastrophic-insurance practice.

## My understanding

The sharp idea is misdirection: the dispute is not whether people can care about both kinds of risk, but that the remedies differ and policy bandwidth is finite. It is worth noting that a serious x-risk-motivated policy agenda such as [[what-if-automating-ai-triggers-intelligence]] lands mostly on transparency, reporting and incident response, which are big-tent policies. So the camps' framings may conflict more than their actual policy menus do.
