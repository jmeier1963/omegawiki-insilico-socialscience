## Definition

Collective attention is a situationally informed mental state in which agents represent the world from a first-person-plural point of view ("we are attending to X"), brought about by explicit or implied co-attention within a social group. It is distinct from individual attention and from mere shared beliefs — it specifically concerns concurrent, coordinated attention to the same stimulus.

## Intuition

When a breaking news event captures the nation, when a viral video circulates simultaneously across millions of feeds, or when a political rally focuses thousands of people on the same speaker — these are collective attention phenomena. The "we" of collective attention is not metaphorical: it produces shared encoding, emotional amplification, and common knowledge that individual-only attention does not. Platform algorithms now engineer which stimuli receive this coordinated focus.

## Formal notation

Collective attention is modeled as a dyadic relation: collective-self → attended-target ("we → X"), rather than as triadic mind-reading. This minimal representation is computationally tractable and makes testable predictions: simultaneous co-attention amplifies neural processing, learning, and affective responses to the target.

## Variants

- **Joint attention**: dyadic/triadic gaze coordination (developmental precursor, requires physical co-presence cues)
- **Mediated collective attention**: mass media and algorithmic platforms create co-attention without physical co-presence
- **Algorithmic collective attention**: engineered co-attention via recommendation systems optimizing for engagement
- **Moral contagion**: moralized content preferentially captures collective attention via in-group amplification

## Comparison

Distinct from **joint attention** (requires gaze-following or overt signaling) and **shared beliefs** (static epistemic states). Collective attention is dynamic, amplifying, and produces common knowledge — the recursive "everyone knows that everyone knows" — that underwrites coordination. Its scarcity is the basis of the **attention economy**.

## When to use

Relevant for analyzing information diffusion, misinformation spread, political agenda-setting, platform design, and AI's epistemic effects at the population level. The collective attention frame highlights that AI is not just changing individual cognition but reshaping what populations attend to together.

## Known limitations

- Collective attention duration has been shortening; accelerating topic cycles may undermine sustained engagement with complex problems
- Algorithmic amplification of novel/emotional content gives misinformation systematic sharing advantages
- Cross-cutting exposure experiments (Bail et al.) show that even well-intentioned interventions can backfire

## Open problems

- How should AI governance account for collective attention externalities (not just individual harms)?
- Can AI systems be designed to lengthen rather than compress collective attention cycles on complex societal problems?
- What is the relationship between collective attention collapse and epistemic polarization?

## My understanding

The collective attention frame recontextualizes AI governance: the question is not just "does AI harm individual users?" but "how does AI reshape the shared epistemic commons that makes democratic deliberation possible?" The finding that individual user choices (not just algorithms) drive echo chambers is important — it suggests purely algorithmic regulation is insufficient.
