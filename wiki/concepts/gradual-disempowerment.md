## Definition

Gradual disempowerment is a family of AI-risk scenarios in which humans do not lose control through a sudden machine takeover but through repeated, locally rational acts of delegation, replacement, institutional optimization, and competitive pressure — each individually reasonable, but collectively transferring power and agency from humans to AI systems.

## Intuition

Every step looks rational: a firm automates routine work to cut costs; a government agency uses AI to optimize allocation decisions; a user delegates tasks to tools that outperform them. No single step is catastrophic, but the cumulative effect is a world in which human agency, skills, and institutional authority have progressively eroded. The danger is not that AI revolts — it is that humans choose to transfer power because each transfer appears profitable, convenient, or strategically necessary.

## Formal notation

Let A(t) = human agency at time t, D(t) = cumulative delegation to AI by time t. Gradual disempowerment: ∂A/∂t < 0 due to D(t) → local D(t+δ) increases appear rational even as global A(t) decreases toward a threshold of irreversibility.

## Variants

David Krueger (2026) identifies ten frames:
1. **Replacement of labor** — AI automates human work across domains
2. **Institutional indifference** — organizations optimize for metrics, not human flourishing
3. **Capitalism** — market pressure rewards AI adoption regardless of externalities
4. **Meta-crisis** — multiple concurrent crises create pressure for AI-managed "solutions"
5. **Metrics/IT** — more domains become legible, controllable, centrally optimized
6. **Evolutionary competition** — AI-centered systems outcompete human-centered ones
7. **Outsourcing decisions** — delegation expands beyond individual to systemic level
8. **Moloch/coordination failure** — instrumental optimization overtakes terminal values
9. **Spectrum with rogue AI** — complements rather than opposes catastrophic AI risks
10. **Deskilling** — humans lose the capacity to perform delegated functions over time

## Known limitations

- Conceptual framework, not yet empirically tested at scale
- Does not necessarily imply malicious intent; most scenarios rely only on competitive/institutional incentives
- Strong counterargument: near-term AI may complement humans rather than replace; human roles may shift

## Open problems

- Are there observable early warning signals of gradual disempowerment in labor markets or institutional decision-making?
- What governance mechanisms can preserve human agency without preventing beneficial AI adoption?
- How does gradual disempowerment interact with cognitive surrender at the individual level?
