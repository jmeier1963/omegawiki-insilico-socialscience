## Definition

The artificial state names the historical endpoint one recurring strand of computing-era political prediction has warned against for seven decades: a political order in which the mechanisms of liberal democracy — consent of the governed, public deliberation, electoral accountability — are functionally displaced by computational prediction and data-driven commercial control. Under this account, "drones" replace "the demos": people are modeled, sorted, and targeted rather than represented and persuaded, and governance shifts from negotiated collective decision-making to calculated management of a population treated as a dataset.

## Intuition

The concept is historical and pattern-level rather than a single prediction: at every major computing inflection point across roughly seventy years (mainframes, data processing, personal computers, the early internet, the web), the same paired forecast recurs almost verbatim — a "computopian" hope that the technology will produce egalitarian abundance and more direct forms of democracy, paired with an "automated state" fear that the same technology will instead let concentrated power model, predict, and manage populations in place of governing them with their consent. The artificial state is the name for the pessimistic pole of that recurring pair, observed as a template that gets reapplied to each new technology rather than a claim specific to any one of them.

The concept's most load-bearing historical claim is that the automated-state outcome, where it has occurred, was not technologically inevitable — it followed from specific, contingent political choices (in the originating account, the failure of liberal democracy to limit corporate power over politics and technology policy, exemplified by concrete 1990s-era deregulatory decisions) rather than from an unavoidable logic of the technology itself.

## Variants

- **Computopian pairing** — the artificial state's optimistic counterpart in the same recurring predictive template: technology enabling egalitarian abundance and more direct democratic participation rather than displacing democracy.
- **Techno-libertarian capture** — a specific historical mechanism proposed for how the automated-state outcome wins: an ideological fusion of anti-democratic technocracy movements with 1980s computopian optimism, capturing the culture and policy environment of an emerging technology sector.
- **Deregulatory-hinge account** — the causal claim localized to specific, identifiable policy decisions (e.g., dismantling technology-assessment oversight capacity, communications-law architecture choices) rather than to a diffuse cultural or technological drift.

## Comparison

- Distinct from [[digital-sovereignty]], which concerns sovereign authority over technology infrastructure between nation-states; the artificial state concerns sovereign authority over governance itself, within a polity, once technology mediates the relationship between a state and its people.
- Related to [[gradual-disempowerment]] as a much longer-horizon, more explicitly political-institutional version of the same underlying concern — where gradual disempowerment focuses on erosion of human agency relative to AI systems, the artificial state focuses specifically on democratic institutions and consent-based governance being displaced by computational management.
- Provides a critical lens for reading contemporary AI scenario and forecasting documents: framed as a recurring 70-year-old predictive template rather than a novel discovery, it argues that today's AI-specific utopia/doom framings should be read with awareness of their genre history, not taken as freshly derived from AI-specific analysis alone.

## Known limitations

- Originates from a work of narrative history built on curated primary-source quotation rather than a systematic or comparative historical methodology; the selection of prescient warnings is not benchmarked against contemporaries whose predictions, in either direction, were simply wrong.
- The central causal claim — that democratic failure to limit corporate power, rather than technological necessity, produced automated-state outcomes — is asserted with only a small number of concrete supporting policy data points, not established through a comparative account of seriously considered and rejected alternative regulatory paths.
- The originating historical narrative extends only through roughly 2000; its application to the current LLM/AI-agent era is an extrapolation from the pattern, not a demonstrated continuation of the same evidentiary chain.

## Open problems

- Does the recurring computopia/automated-state predictive pattern actually explain anything about which outcome occurs, or does it only describe that the same forecasts get made regardless of outcome?
- Which specific, contingent policy choices in the current AI era would be the analogues of the 1990s deregulatory hinge points this concept's originating account identifies, and are they still reversible?
- Can the "failure of liberal democracy to limit corporate power" thesis be tested against cases where democratic institutions did successfully constrain a computing technology's political trajectory, as a comparison class?

## Relationship to foundations

Draws on the long tradition of technocracy-critique and computopia/dystopia forecasting in science and technology studies (Mumford's critique of "cybernetic intelligence," Winner's critique of technological politics), read here as a recurring genre rather than a series of independent, technology-specific predictions.

## My understanding

The durable value here is not the historical narrative's specific 1958–1996 content but the meta-observation it supports: utopia/doom framing around a new computing technology is a template, reapplied with each technological wave rather than freshly derived. That is a useful discipline for reading any current AI scenario or forecasting document — it does not tell you whether this time is different, but it does tell you to ask that question explicitly rather than assume novelty by default. The concept's weakest point is exactly where the underlying historical account is thinnest: it wants to license strong inferences about the present AI moment while its own evidentiary chain stops well before that moment begins.
