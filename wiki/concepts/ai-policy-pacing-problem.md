## Definition

The structural mismatch between the exponential pace of AI capability growth (driven by scaling laws) and the slow, deliberate pace of policy and legislation. Because institutions take years to act while AI can move from "amusing toy" to transformative in the same span, policy responses tend to arrive systematically behind the risks and opportunities they target.

## Intuition

Amodei's Treebeard metaphor: AI is the army at the gates; legislatures are Treebeard, taking a day to say hello to another tree. The slowness is often *appropriate* (governments wield grave powers and shouldn't act hastily), but the mismatch means that by the time a risk is legible enough to legislate against, it may already be hard to manage — a restatement of the Collingridge dilemma for AI.

## Formal notation

Informal. Characterized by the gap Δ between the time for a capability/risk to materialize and the time for a governing institution to respond; the pacing problem is acute when Δ is large and the underlying capability grows exponentially.

## Variants

- **Optionality-preserving policy** — early-stage response (transparency, export controls, data collection) that buys time and visibility without committing to specific rules.
- **Anticipatory binding regulation** — acting before full clarity, risking ineffective compliance burdens (the 2024 dilemma Amodei describes).
- **Asymmetric pacing** — frontier AI risks under-regulated (too slow to bind) while downstream AI applications are over-regulated (legacy systems too slow to approve benefits).

## Comparison

Sharpens generic "regulation lags technology" observations by tying the lag to *exponential* capability growth and to a specific design dilemma (act early and miss, or act late and lose control). Closely related to [[frontier-ai-compute-governance]] (one proposed response) and [[ai-race-dynamics]] (competitive pressure that worsens the lag).

## When to use

Framing why AI governance proposals emphasize speed, optionality, and staged escalation; comparing policy stances across jurisdictions and authors.

## Known limitations

The framing can be used to justify either urgency or deregulation depending on which side of the asymmetry one emphasizes; it presumes scaling laws continue.

## Open problems

How to build institutions that can ramp response speed with capability; how to avoid both anticipatory over-regulation and reactive under-regulation.

A useful organizing lens that recurs across the 2026 AI policy literature. Its rhetorical power is also its weakness: the same "we're behind the exponential" claim motivates opposite prescriptions.
