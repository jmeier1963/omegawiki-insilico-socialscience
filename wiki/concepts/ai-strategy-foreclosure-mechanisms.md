## Definition

A taxonomy of five distinct mechanisms by which a strategic option (one of the [[seven-archetypal-ai-strategies]]) can become permanently or practically unavailable, even without any decision to abandon it: **institutional decay** (atrophy of expertise, organizational knowledge, or state capacity needed by a strategy — e.g., verification arrangements or diplomatic channels for Co-Development or Moratorium that erode when unmaintained); **deployment lock-in** (embedding a technology so deeply in social/economic/institutional life that reversal becomes politically and economically prohibitive, even though each individual deployment decision was reasonable and reversible when made); **geopolitical fait accompli** (a rival's independent success removes the option from outside — e.g., an adversary achieving and using decisive AI-enabled advantage before the U.S. commits); **agency erosion** (the collective capacity to exercise any strategic choice atrophies through accumulated dependence and cognitive offloading, so an option remains formally open while the practical ability to act on it drains away); and **epistemic foreclosure** (diffusion of model weights, methods, or talent beyond a controlled boundary removes the exclusive leverage some strategies require, quickly and often irreversibly). Foreclosure occurs specifically when a precondition required by a strategy is removed for long enough that the strategy cannot be reconstituted within the window a decision would call for it.

## Intuition

An option can die two ways: someone chooses to give it up, or it silently rots while attention is elsewhere. The five mechanisms are ways of noticing the second kind before it's too late — each pairs a description of *how* the rot happens with a historical analogue (Open Skies Treaty withdrawal for institutional decay; carbon-energy infrastructure for deployment lock-in; the 1949 Soviet nuclear test foreclosing the Baruch Plan for geopolitical fait accompli; individual-level cognitive-offloading evidence for agency erosion; 1990s-2000s encryption export-control liberalization for epistemic foreclosure) that shows the same pattern has happened before with other technologies or institutions.

## Variants

- **Action-triggered foreclosure**: premature commitment to one strategy actively destroys the preconditions of others (e.g., committing to Dominance lets Co-Development's cooperative-verification institutions atrophy through neglect — institutional decay triggered by choice, not passivity).
- **Inaction-triggered foreclosure**: deferring commitment forecloses options by default, since long-lead capabilities (verification technology, diplomatic channels, coexistence research) decay while waiting for conclusive evidence — described in the source paper as a government that defers "effectively executing Acceleration in practice."
- **Externally-inflicted foreclosure**: geopolitical fait accompli is unique among the five in being inflicted by another actor's success rather than by the strategist's own choices or neglect.
- **Abrupt vs. gradual foreclosure**: some mechanisms are dateable and visible as they happen (a treaty withdrawal, a nuclear test, a major model-weight leak); others (deployment lock-in, agency erosion) accumulate invisibly until the loss is only clear in retrospect.

## Comparison

- Distinct from ordinary opportunity cost: foreclosure specifically concerns the *removal of a strategic option's precondition*, not merely the resource cost of pursuing one strategy over another.
- Related to the economic concept of quasi-option value (Arrow and Fisher 1974) under irreversibility and improving information, which the source paper cites explicitly as the decision-theoretic grounding for why keeping options open has calculable value even before any option is exercised.
- Parallels [[gradual-disempowerment]]'s account of incrementally, individually-rational delegation eroding human agency: the "agency erosion" foreclosure mechanism is essentially gradual disempowerment applied specifically to a polity's capacity to execute AI strategy, rather than to individual human agency generally.
- Parallels [[digital-sovereignty]]'s concerns about dependence on foreign AI infrastructure: "epistemic foreclosure" is the geopolitical-strategy framing of the same underlying diffusion dynamic digital-sovereignty advocates worry about from a different angle (state control vs. strategic optionality).

## Known limitations

- The "agency erosion" mechanism is explicitly flagged by the source paper as observed only at the individual level so far; the collective, strategic-level version (a polity losing the capacity to choose and execute a strategy) has not been empirically observed, and the paper concedes measuring it is an open research problem.
- The five mechanisms are derived from historical analogy (nuclear-era treaties, encryption controls, energy infrastructure) rather than from any AI-specific causal model; whether AI-era foreclosure follows the same dynamics as these precedents is asserted rather than tested.
- No quantitative method is offered for estimating how close a given option is to being foreclosed, or how much investment is needed to keep it alive — the taxonomy is descriptive/qualitative throughout.

## Open problems

- Can any of the five mechanisms be given leading indicators specific enough to serve as an actual early-warning system, rather than being recognizable only in hindsight (as the source paper concedes for several of its own historical analogues)?
- Does agency erosion at the individual level (the one form with some empirical support) reliably aggregate into collective/institutional agency erosion, or could the two come apart — a society whose institutions retain strategic capacity even as many individuals experience cognitive offloading, or vice versa?
- How should a Freedom-of-Action-style strategy actually weigh the five mechanisms against each other when they conflict — e.g., when building deterrence capability (guarding against fait accompli) simultaneously accelerates deployment lock-in?

## My understanding

The taxonomy's genuine contribution is distinguishing foreclosure mechanisms that respond to different remedies: institutional decay and epistemic foreclosure call for active maintenance and control regimes; deployment lock-in and agency erosion call for early, deliberate friction against convenience-driven dependence; geopolitical fait accompli is largely unpreventable by unilateral action and instead argues for speed or hedging. Treating "keep your options open" as five separable failure modes rather than one vague risk is what makes the concept operational rather than merely rhetorical — though the weakest link (agency erosion) is also the one most directly relevant to this wiki's broader gradual-disempowerment literature, and it's the one the source paper itself admits it cannot yet measure.
