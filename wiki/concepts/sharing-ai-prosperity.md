## Definition

The problem (and proposed mechanisms) of distributing the economic gains of advanced AI broadly rather than letting them concentrate in a few firms or owners of capital. It spans predistribution (worker voice, broadened capital access, "right to AI"), redistribution (UBI, taxes on AI firms/capital gains), and institution-building (a renegotiated social contract analogous to the Progressive Era / New Deal).

## Intuition

AI can lower costs and raise productivity, but the gains can accrue to model providers and capital owners while workers see productivity rise without sharing the upside. Whether abundant "intelligence" becomes broad prosperity or concentrated wealth is a *policy choice*, not a property of the technology — so it requires deliberate mechanisms for sharing returns and access.

## Formal notation

Informal. Assessed via distributional metrics (who captures AI's productivity gains), access metrics (affordable model availability), and institutional design (worker voice, ownership, transfers).

## Variants

- **Predistribution** — worker voice in deployment, capital access, "Right to AI" as basic infrastructure (OpenAI industrial policy).
- **Redistribution** — UBI / income support financed by taxes on AI firms or capital gains (cf. [[policy-ai-exponential]] macro section).
- **New social contract** — New-Deal-scale institution-building for the AI transition.
- **AI wealth funds** — public equity stakes / sovereign-fund schemes (Altman/Trump voluntary donations; Sanders forced transfer; broad levies). Per-capita payouts are small at current scales ([[how-share-ai-riches]]) — value is mainly political/insurance.

## Comparison

The counterweight to [[gradual-disempowerment]] and wealth-concentration scenarios. Distinct from [[ai-normal-technology]] (which questions whether disruption is even automatic) — this concept assumes large gains and asks who captures them.

## When to use

Analysing proposals for distributing AI's economic benefits and the politics of an AI-era social contract.

## Known limitations

Proposals are often mechanism-thin; self-interest of proposers (frontier labs) complicates them; redistribution depends on fiscal/political feasibility.

## Open problems

Which concrete mechanisms (equity, sovereign funds, taxes, access mandates) actually broaden returns without entrenching incumbents; how to fund a "right to AI."

## My understanding

A convergence point of the 2026 policy literature: lab CEOs, the Economist, and economists all arrive at "the distribution question." The disagreement is over mechanism and whether predistribution or redistribution does the work.
