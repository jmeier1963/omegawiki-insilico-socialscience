## Definition

A mechanism of technological labour displacement in which a technology does not automate a worker's task *inside* a service but instead enables the *customer* to do the work themselves, collapsing demand for the intermediary service. The diagnostic question is "can the customer do without this job?" rather than "can a machine do this job?"

## Intuition

The washing machine didn't replace the laundress by doing her job better — it let households do without her. ATMs, by contrast, made an existing service (banking) cheaper and *grew* teller employment (a Jevons paradox) — until mobile deposit let customers self-serve, at which point teller demand collapsed. AI generalizes self-service to professional and manual domains: chatbot boiler diagnosis, patients decoding their own test results.

## Formal notation

Informal. Conditioned on the Jevons-paradox boundary: in-service automation → cheaper service → possible employment growth; self-service enablement → demand for the service collapses. When execution cost → 0, the binding constraint becomes human verification — which self-service offloads onto the customer.

## Variants

- **In-service automation (Jevons paradox)** — makes the existing service model more efficient; can expand demand and employment.
- **Self-service enablement** — lets people do the work themselves; demand for the intermediary collapses.
- **Measurement blind spot** — consumer-absorbed work disappears from GDP/labour statistics (links to AI satellite accounts).

## When to use

Predicting which services AI threatens (intermediaries whose function customers can self-serve) and diagnosing why standard automation analyses mispredict.

## Known limitations

Hard to quantify relative to in-service automation; not all services are self-serviceable; the GDP-blindspot magnitude is unestimated.

## Open problems

How large is the self-service channel; how to measure consumer-absorbed labour in national accounts.
