## Problem

Can social media and Big Tech platforms actually be regulated effectively, and what do recent political gambles by Meta and X reveal about the underlying dynamics?

## Key idea

Financial Times analysis (January 2025) by David Allen Green arguing that Big Tech's recent political realignments (Meta and X under Zuckerberg/Musk cozying up to Trump) reflect business logic from a position of weakness, not strength. The deeper argument: regulation of social media is fundamentally difficult because it isn't clear what "better regulation" would look like technically. Uses Juvenal's "quis custodiet ipsos custodes?" as framing for the meta-problem of governing the governors of digital public space.

## Method

FT opinion/analysis. 5 pages. David Allen Green (regulatory and media lawyer, FT contributing editor).

## Results

- Meta/X political alignment with Trump: business logic (avoiding adversarial regulation), not strength
- "Better regulation" demand is often naive: assumes regulation can solve problems it cannot
- Social media regulation faces: technical complexity, jurisdictional fragmentation, pace of change
- The "quis custodiet" problem: who governs the platforms that now govern public discourse?
- Content moderation is inherently political; neutrality is a fiction

## Limitations

- FT opinion piece; normative analysis
- Written January 2025; rapidly evolving political context

## My take

Sharp analysis of why Big Tech regulation is harder than political demand for it implies. The "weakness not strength" argument about Meta/X political realignment is analytically useful. Importance 2 (FT opinion; analytically substantive but brief).
