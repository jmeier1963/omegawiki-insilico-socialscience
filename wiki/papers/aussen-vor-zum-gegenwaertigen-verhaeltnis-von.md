## Problem & Context

Rohe opens with a Jürgen Klopp joke ("Scientists have discovered. But then they went straight back inside") as a folk-wisdom encapsulation of a longstanding structural tension: science needs protected internal autonomy (its own theories, methods, laboratory conditions) to produce anything worth offering society, yet this same "Inhäusigkeit" (in-house-ness) is what makes science's contribution to and accountability toward society perpetually contested. Rohe frames this via Luhmann's systems theory as the durable tension between science's *self-reference* (autonomy, truth-seeking, internal paradigms) and its *external reference* (usefulness, societal legitimation, funding dependence) — two poles that are not mutually exclusive but constitutively co-present, and whose historical negotiation (per David Kaldewey's and Peter Strohschneider's work, both cited at length) has so far been flexible enough to absorb periodic frictions without provoking a paradigm break.

The essay's animating question: are there signs, beyond this normal flexibility, of a more fundamental shift in the "border traffic" between science and society — voiced by figures like Dennis Snower (a possible end to science and society's "long lasting love affair") and Strohschneider (society's "autonomy tolerance" toward science may be eroding)? Rohe examines five candidate shifts in turn.

**Note on scope**: this essay is not primarily about AI. It is a sociology-of-science essay about the changing relationship between science and society; AI appears in exactly two sentences, both brief (see Section IV below). It is catalogued here because one of those two sentences independently corroborates a claim tracked elsewhere in this wiki, not because the essay is centrally about AI.

## Key idea

**The quality/excellence discourse is not a neutral measure of scientific merit but a self-protective surrogate science invented to avoid being held directly accountable for the societal benefits it promises.** Rohe's sharpest and most original move (Section I) inverts Luhmann's own diagnosis: Luhmann described external pressure on science as an externally-caused "fever" — society's demands as an infection the science system's "immune response" (increasingly loose, inflated truth-claims) reacts to. Rohe argues this is one-sided: science's retreat into quantified "quality" and "excellence" metrics (number and type of successfully passed peer-review procedures, following Caspar Hirschi) is not primarily imposed from outside but is science's own strategy for avoiding direct demonstration of its societal usefulness — a **self-immunization that has curdled into autoimmune disease**: research topics are now determined by publishability rather than by problem-solving value, and careers are driven by the plannability of incremental milestones rather than genuine inquiry — "a departure into the already-known" ("Aufbruch ins Bekannte").

The essay's broader argument, across all five sections, is that science should engage more openly and directly with its "gesellschaftliche Anlehnungskontexte" (societal points of contact/dependency) rather than retreating into an autonomy ideal that increasingly functions as an evasion.

## Method

An essay in the German sociology-of-science tradition: systems-theoretic argument (primarily via Niklas Luhmann's *Die Wissenschaft der Gesellschaft*, 1991) combined with citation of a specific secondary literature (David Kaldewey, Peter Strohschneider, Caspar Hirschi, Samuel Moore et al.'s "Fetishisation of Excellence," Daniel Sarewitz, John Ioannidis, Bruce Alberts/Harold Varmus) and illustrated with concrete institutional examples drawn from Rohe's own apparent professional context at the Stiftung Mercator (e.g., the Mercator Research Institute on Global Commons and Climate Change under Ottmar Edenhofer; the Mercator Forum Migration und Demokratie under Hans Vorländer), plus an anecdote from the 2018 Falling Walls conference. No original data collection; this is theoretical/normative argument with illustrative institutional cases, not an empirical study.

The essay develops five theses in sequence:

1. **Eroding autonomy tolerance and the quality/excellence surrogate** (detailed above).
2. **"Grand Challenges" as a self-inflicted legitimation trap.** Rohe notes (via Kaldewey 2018) that the "Grand Challenges" framing was itself originally produced by science as a legitimation strategy — grounding science's value in solving global problems (water, food, disease, climate, migration) rather than in the quality discourse. Society then "took science at its word": having promised solutions, science cannot easily walk the promise back. Rohe's own institutional prescription (drawn from Stiftung Mercator practice): fund research *oriented toward* societal problems without prescribing solutions in advance ("optionales Denken" — exploring alternative solution paths and their costs/risks/trade-offs, rather than dictating outcomes), citing the IPCC's own principle "to be policy relevant but not policy prescriptive," reformulated for science funding as "to be policy relevant but not *science* prescriptive."
3. **Unbounded institutional growth.** Because the pursuit of knowledge has no natural stopping rule (every study ends with "more research is needed"), doubling the number of contributors doubles the number of open questions, producing unbounded and continuously growing funding demand untethered from a clear endpoint — a dynamic Rohe says science has itself diagnosed (citing Sarewitz's "to feed the beast" and Ioannidis) without resolving.
4. **The collapsing discovery-to-application time lag.** Engineer Charles Kettering's answer to "what's the difference between basic and industrial research" ("20 years") has compressed dramatically since the 1950s; in some fields, discovery and application now happen simultaneously or even in reverse order — products anticipate theoretical understanding. Rohe's explicit example: **"Cutting Edge Research" in AI research or computer hardware is now found in companies, in private 'Innovation Labs,' where the generation and deployment of knowledge can no longer be distinguished** — and he poses, without answering, the question of whether market-ready AI-based products can exceed the state of research represented by the underlying algorithms, explicitly attributing this view to developers themselves ("Das jedenfalls ist die Sicht der Entwickler" — "that, at least, is the developers' own view").
5. **Blurring of scientific and non-scientific knowledge online.** The internet's permanent, ubiquitous availability of scientific knowledge, editable and extensible by an effectively unlimited number of producers, erodes the sharpness of the boundary between "emphatically scientific" knowledge and merely science-*based* knowledge circulating outside institutional science — Peter Weingart's "societalization of science," which Rohe notes Weingart already identified over 20 years ago.

## Experiment & Results

Not applicable — a theoretical/normative essay, not an empirical study. Its "results" are the five diagnosed shifts above and a closing set of programmatic recommendations: science should invest its "Möglichkeitssinn" (Robert Musil's "sense of possibility") more into developing solution options for societal problems rather than only cultivating problems for their own sake, while avoiding Strohschneider's critiqued "solutionism"; science should not leave society alone with the burden of selecting among possibilities it has itself expanded (citing Hermann Lübbe's 1965 point that science already participates in defining what is politically desirable, even though political processes ultimately resolve power questions, not truth questions); and more research should be invested specifically into the *mutual responsiveness* between science and other social systems.

## Limitations

- **Author's institutional position is not disclosed as a limitation by the author himself.** Rohe writes from evident personal experience at the Stiftung Mercator, using its programs as his positive illustrative cases; this is transparent in the text but not flagged as a potential source of selection bias.
- **Essay form, not argument subjected to counter-evidence.** Claims are supported by citation of aligned secondary literature rather than tested against disconfirming cases; Rohe does note and argue against one dissenting reading (Hirschi's claim that the quality/excellence fixation was politically "infiltrated" rather than self-generated), more engagement with a counter-view than is typical for the genre, but it remains the only one directly engaged.
- **The AI-relevant claim (Section IV) is a single, unelaborated aside**, not a developed argument: Rohe poses the question of whether AI products can outrun the research state of their underlying algorithms and explicitly declines to answer it himself, attributing the affirmative view to "the developers." Readers should not treat it as an argued conclusion.
- **No explicit definition of "quality" or "excellence" is offered before critiquing them** — the critique draws on Hirschi and Moore et al.'s prior definitional work rather than developing its own.
- Entirely Germany/German-academic-system-centered in its examples and institutional referents; portability to other national science systems is not discussed.

## Open questions

- Does the "quality/excellence as autoimmune self-protection" diagnosis generalize beyond Germany/Europe to funding systems (e.g., the US NIH/NSF grant system) with different institutional histories, or is it specific to the European "Exzellenzinitiative"-style funding landscape?
- Rohe's own prescription ("optionales Denken": fund toward problems without prescribing solutions) is illustrated by two cases from his own institutional environment — has it been tested against comparable funding regimes that do prescribe solutions, to see whether it produces measurably different research quality or uptake?
- If cutting-edge AI/hardware knowledge production really has migrated substantially into private corporate labs (Section IV's claim), does industry R&D face the same publishability-driven distortion the essay diagnoses for academic science, a different one, or none at all?
- Is internet-driven blurring of scientific/science-based knowledge actually eroding science's authority, or has it just changed which credentialing signals the public relies on?

## My take

This is a genuine, closely-argued sociology-of-science essay, not a piece about AI, and it should be catalogued and linked accordingly — the AI-relevant content is exactly two sentences and should not be oversold. That said, those two sentences are worth having in the wiki: they are a crisp, independently-arrived-at formulation of a claim this wiki already tracks from the AI-industry side — that frontier AI capability results increasingly bypass peer-reviewed academic science entirely, appearing first as shipped products or corporate technical reports. Rohe arrives at the same observation from sociology-of-science theory rather than from AI-industry analysis, which makes it a useful independent corroboration rather than a duplicate of existing wiki content.

The stronger and more durable contribution, independent of AI, is Section I's autoimmune-disease reframing of the quality/excellence discourse: recasting bibliometric quality signaling as science's own accountability-avoidance strategy, rather than as an externally-imposed distortion (Luhmann's "fever" framing), is a genuinely sharper diagnosis than the standard "metrics are gamed" critique, and it travels well to any discussion of how *AI research itself* is evaluated (leaderboards, benchmark chasing, publish-or-perish dynamics around arXiv preprints) — a connection Rohe does not make himself but that this wiki's existing material on peer review makes easy to draw.
