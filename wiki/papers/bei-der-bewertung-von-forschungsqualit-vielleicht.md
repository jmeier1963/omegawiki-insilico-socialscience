## Problem & Context

The essay opens with a scene from 2006: Berlin's new science senator Jürgen Zöllner tells the heads of the city's research institutions that science has a massive quality problem and they must act urgently. Pressed for evidence, he points to the absence of routine replication testing in medical and natural-science research, to results that cannot be repeated, and to fabricated data and image series that circulate and get cited as authentic. Markschies — then president of the Humboldt-Universität, later president of the Berlin-Brandenburg Academy, and a long-serving journal editor — records his initial irritation and the subsequent twenty years that made the diagnosis harder to dismiss.

## Key idea

Quality assessment fails when it is generic. The standards that make a result credible are constituted inside disciplinary cultures — what counts as evidence, what counts as replication, what counts as an adequate argument differ by field — and evaluation regimes that abstract away from those cultures measure something other than quality. The recommendation is to rebuild assessment on discipline-specific criteria owned by the disciplines themselves.

## Method

Reflective essay drawing on two decades in editorial, university-leadership and academy roles. Not a study; the evidence is professional experience organized argumentatively.

## Experiment & Results

No empirical results. The substantive claims: that the quality problem is real and was under-acknowledged when first raised; that indicator-based assessment has not addressed it and in places aggravated it; and that responsibility for standards cannot be delegated to generic procedures or to publishers.

## Limitations

- Anecdotal by design; the argument rests on the author's authority and vantage point.
- German science-system specific in its institutional references.
- Names the failure of generic indicators more sharply than it specifies what discipline-owned assessment would look like operationally.

## Open questions

- How can discipline-specific standards be made legible to cross-disciplinary funding decisions without collapsing back into generic indicators?
- Who arbitrates when disciplinary standards conflict within an interdisciplinary submission?
- What is the analogous move for evaluating AI-assisted work, where the relevant standard is tool-specific as well as field-specific?

## My take

The transferable point is the argument against generic policy. Applied to AI, it implies that generic publisher-level AI policies — disclose use, remain responsible — cannot do the work, because whether a given use is legitimate depends on what counts as evidence in the field. Physics and history do not share a standard of proof and cannot share a standard for admissible machine assistance. That reframes AI policy as a task for disciplinary communities rather than for publishers or funders.
