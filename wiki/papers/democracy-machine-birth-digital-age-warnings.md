## Problem & Context

Lepore opens by naming a felt absence: the current AI moment "doesn't have much of a past" — public discourse treats today's anxieties and hopes about AI as unprecedented, when in fact "a lot of people saw this all coming." Her organizing concept is **the artificial state**: "the replacement of the liberal democratic nation-state, the consent of the governed, with the rule of people by machines" — democratic deliberation replaced by "prediction by calculation," the public sphere replaced by data-driven commerce, "drones" replacing "the demos." The piece's question is why decades of explicit warnings about this trajectory were not heeded.

## Key idea

A historical throughline, not a new empirical claim: from the 1950s through the 1990s, every new wave of computing technology (mainframes → data processing → PCs → Arpanet/internet → web) provoked the *same* paired predictions — a "computopia" of egalitarian abundance and direct democracy, versus an "automated state"/"compudystopia" of machine rule — regardless of which specific technology was doing the provoking. Lepore's thesis is that the automated-state outcome was not inevitable: "The digital revolution didn't have to play out the way it did in the realm of politics and government; it played out the way it did because of the failure of liberal democracy to limit corporate power over politics and government."

She locates the pivot in the 1990s: **techno-libertarianism**, which she describes as marrying "the technocracy crusade of the 1930s — an anti-democracy movement led, in the US, by Elon Musk's grandfather — to the computopianism of the 1980s," captured Silicon Valley's Ayn Rand-influenced culture and, via specific 1990s policy choices, won.

## Method

Not applicable — this is narrative history/journalism (a trade-book excerpt), not a study. Its evidentiary method is a chronological chain of primary-source quotations and named historical actors, each cited to a year and a source, rather than data or experiment.

## Experiment & Results

Not applicable — no empirical claims or data. The piece's "results" are a documented sequence of predictions and events, spanning:

- **1958**: the "mechanisation of thought processes" conference (Teddington, near London); Edward L. Greenfield and Ithiel de Sola Pool's proposal for a "People Machine" — an "information bank" sorting voters by type, later called "the A-bomb of the social sciences."
- **1959–1970**: the Simulmatics Corporation, hired by the DNC and then JFK's campaign (credited by the press with his 1960 win); went bankrupt in 1970 for lack of paying clients outside politics — "the problem wasn't the computers, or the model. The problem was an insufficiency of data."
- **1964–1976**: dystopian responses — Eugene Burdick's novel *The 480*; Lewis Mumford's *The Myth of the Machine* (1966) warning of "cybernetic intelligence" reducing people to "passive, purposeless, machine-conditioned animal[s]"; Joseph Weizenbaum (creator of ELIZA, 1966) calling AI-driven state automation "obscene" (1976).
- **1963–1969**: JCR Licklider's foundational internet memos and his measured position between computopia and automated state, including an explicit 1969 warning about "clandestine artificial intelligence programs, searching through the databases, altering files, fabricating records and erasing their own audit trails."
- **1971–1973**: Chile's Project Cybersyn, ended by Pinochet's coup.
- **1972–1978**: computopian optimism (Stewart Brand in *Rolling Stone*; Amitai Etzioni predicting a return to direct Athenian-style democracy; Daniel Boorstin's "republic of technology").
- **1980–1984**: Reagan campaign's own "People Machine"; Langdon Winner's critique that "the computer revolution is conspicuously silent about its own ends"; Apple's 1984 Macintosh ad.
- **1980s**: early trolling and the first white-supremacist bulletin boards (George Dietz's Liberty Bell Network, 1983; Aryan Nations Liberty Net and others, 1984).
- **1994–1996**: cyberspace-as-lawless-frontier culture (Carmen "humdog" Hermosillo's "it is a black hole"; John Perry Barlow's *Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace*, 1996) versus norm-building attempts (Sally Hambridge's 1995 netiquette guide; Michael Hauben's 1995 Proposed Declaration of the Rights of Netizens).
- **1995–1996**: the concrete policy hinge — Gingrich's 1995 closure of the Congressional Office of Technology Assessment, and Clinton's 1996 Telecommunications Act, which Lepore frames as delivering "an almost entirely unregulated internet."
- **1995**: Netscape's IPO and the dot-com gold rush.

## Limitations

- **Selective by design.** As a book excerpt, the piece necessarily curates toward prescient critics who support the thesis; it does not engage contemporaries whose predictions (in either direction) were simply wrong in ways that don't fit the computopia/automated-state binary.
- **The central causal claim is asserted, not demonstrated.** "Failure of liberal democracy to limit corporate power" is stated as the explanation for why deregulation won, but the excerpt offers only two concrete policy data points (OTA closure, Telecom Act) rather than a comparative account of alternative regulatory paths that were seriously considered and rejected.
- **An unelaborated genealogical claim.** The assertion that techno-libertarianism's technocracy-movement lineage was "led, in the US, by Elon Musk's grandfather" is stated flatly with no citation or elaboration within the excerpt.
- **Thin connective tissue to the present.** Despite framing the piece around "this dizzying, headlong moment" of 2026 AI discourse, the historical narrative itself stops essentially at 2000; the link from 1990s deregulation to today's LLM-era AI governance debates is asserted in the opening/closing frame rather than argued in the body.

## Open questions

- Does Lepore's full book carry the throughline past the dot-com era into 2020s AI governance specifically, or does the excerpt represent close to the whole of the "digital age" argument, with the present-day framing serving mainly as a hook?
- Is "failure of liberal democracy to limit corporate power" sufficient as an explanation, or does it understate the contingency of specific, arguably reversible choices (OTA closure, Section 230/Telecom Act architecture) that a different Congress could plausibly have made differently for reasons unrelated to a generalized democratic "failure"?
- How does the Musk's-grandfather/technocracy-movement genealogy hold up against other standard histories of libertarian tech culture?

## My take

This is a well-sourced, honest piece of popular history, not a testable analytical contribution, and it should be read in this wiki proportionately to that: a primary-source-quality anchor for the historical prehistory of today's AI-and-democracy debates, not as evidence for any specific claim about 2026 AI policy.

The one genuinely portable observation is structural rather than historical: the same paired prediction — utopia versus automated-state — recurs almost verbatim at every major computing inflection point across 70 years, independent of the specific technology. That is itself evidence that today's AI-specific utopia/doom framing is a *template* being reapplied, not a discovery about this particular technology. It is a useful corrective lens for reading any speculative-future document, including this batch's own [[beyond-tomorrow-four-scenarios-world-2050]] (BCG's 2050 scenarios), as continuous with a 70-year-old genre rather than as novel forecasting grounded in AI-specific analysis.

Where the piece is thin is exactly where its framing device needs it to be strong: it wants 2026 readers to feel the weight of a half-century of ignored warnings, but the excerpt's own evidentiary chain effectively ends in 1996–2000, leaving the most AI-relevant 25 years unaddressed within this text. The causal thesis (democratic failure, not technological necessity) is a good thesis for the book to argue at length; the excerpt states it more than it earns it.
