## Problem & Context

When calculators entered classrooms in the 1970s they provoked the same two reactions LLMs provoke now: fear of intellectual deskilling and hope of more efficient learning. The analogy has become the standard rhetorical move for normalizing LLMs — a tool that automates a mechanical step, leaving the real thinking intact. Voinea, Porsdam Mann, Savulescu and Earp take the analogy seriously enough to test where it holds and where it breaks.

## Key idea

The analogy is a useful starting point and ultimately insufficient. Calculators and LLMs mediate learning through different *affordances*, and it is at the points where the affordances diverge that the distinctive pedagogical challenges of LLMs appear. The constructive consequence: realizing the educational potential of LLMs requires cultivating epistemic virtues suited to human–AI interaction — patience, reflective engagement, intellectual vigilance, humility — and, symmetrically, designing systems that actively foster those virtues through built-in prompts, feedback loops and reflective questions.

## Method

Conceptual analysis in the philosophy-of-technology and virtue-epistemology traditions. The authors decompose the analogy into the affordance structure of each technology and locate the disanalogies, then derive the virtue requirements from those disanalogies rather than from general worries about AI.

## Experiment & Results

No empirical component. The results are the mapped disanalogies and the derived virtue list.

The core asymmetries the paper identifies: a calculator has a narrow, well-specified operation whose correctness is verifiable and whose output does not resemble reasoning, whereas an LLM produces open-ended output across the full range of intellectual tasks, in a form that presents itself as reasoning, without a comparable correctness guarantee. That difference relocates the burden onto the user, who must supply the vigilance the tool does not.

The design claim is the paper's less common move: epistemic virtue is treated as something a system can be built to elicit, not only something a user must bring.

## Limitations

- Entirely conceptual; the virtue prescriptions are not tested against learning outcomes.
- Virtue-epistemic vocabulary places the remedy with individual character, which sits awkwardly with the structural incentives (time pressure, assessment design) that drive uncritical use.
- The design recommendations (built-in prompts, feedback loops) are sketched rather than specified, and there is a tension between friction-by-design and the commercial pressure toward frictionlessness.

## Open questions

- Do the proposed design interventions measurably change use, or are they defeated by habituation?
- Which of the named virtues are trainable, and by what curriculum?
- Does the affordance analysis survive as models become more agentic — i.e. when the output is an action rather than a text?

## My take

This is the paper that does the work the calculator analogy usually avoids. Instrument comparisons — calculator, microscope, statistics software — are the standard rhetorical device for treating AI as a normal technology, and their force comes from not being examined. Voinea et al. examine one and find it insufficient in exactly the place that matters: the calculator does not simulate the activity it replaces, and the LLM does.

The design half is the more interesting contribution and the more fragile one. Making a system elicit patience and vigilance is a direct bet against the product incentive to remove friction, and the paper does not say who would build such a system or why.
