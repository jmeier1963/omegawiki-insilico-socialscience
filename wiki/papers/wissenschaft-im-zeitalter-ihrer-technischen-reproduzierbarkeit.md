## Problem & Context

Lars Hochmann (Professor für Transformation und Unternehmung, President of the Hochschule für Gesellschaftsgestaltung, Koblenz) published this guest essay on the Wiarda blog on 17 August 2026. Its structural claim: LLMs do not digitalize science, they make scientific *thinking* technically reproducible.

The analogy is Benjamin's 1935 essay on art in the age of mechanical reproduction. What photography was to painting, the LLM is to the scientific text. An academic work has an aura too — its singularity, its boundness to a standpoint, its genesis out of a concrete question — and that aura is now reproducible and infinitely scalable.

## Key idea

Three moves.

**Cult value vs. exhibition value.** Science has a cult value: the process of research itself, the resistant engagement with an object, the demand of interpretation, the struggle for the adequate concept. That cult value is a *Bildungswert*, because persons become personalities in the process. The decades-long drift toward exhibition value — impact factor, h-index, preprint flood, counting points in appointment procedures — is pushed to its limit by LLMs, because exhibition value can now be produced almost without cult value: texts that look like science without the labour of self-formation behind them.

**The Epistemically Unconscious.** Benjamin's "optical unconscious" was the camera's capacity to show what the eye cannot — zoom stretches space, slow motion stretches time. LLMs make visible an *Epistemisch-Unbewusstes*: patterns no human mind could detect, cross-disciplinary connections that meet in no single consciousness. But the optical unconscious also *directed* the gaze — film replaced contemplative immersion with distracted perception, and LLM-assisted research evokes its academic successor: a science that receives instead of reflecting, scrolls instead of reading, generates instead of asking.

**Socializing the criteria.** Once anyone can produce scientifically plausible text, negotiating the criteria of good science behind closed doors no longer suffices. Hochmann calls for the *Vergesellschaftung der Kriterien guter Wissenschaft* — society must be able to understand what distinguishes scientific knowledge from other kinds, and to demand it of institutions.

## Method

Essayistic argument by analogy, drawing on Benjamin and on the capabilities approach of Nussbaum and Sen.

## Experiment & Results

No empirical content. The load-bearing claims:

- **The productivity race is lost by definition.** Machines will always publish faster, formulate more smoothly, process larger datasets, find more patterns. Any attempt to defend science against LLMs on productivity is lost in advance *and wrong*, because it measures science by an economic rather than an epistemological category.
- **The maxim**: use LLMs to train what is already there, not to compensate for what is missing — because there is a categorical difference between a person who can think using an LLM and a person learning to think with one.
- **The alternative yardstick**: the capabilities approach asks not how much was produced but what real possibilities a person has. Transferred to science: what has it enabled people to think, understand and shape that they could not have without it? Capability instead of productivity shifts nearly everything — evaluation standards, teaching and examination formats, institutional self-understanding.
- **Why this defends academic freedom rather than attacking it**: a society that can no longer distinguish systematic knowledge from industrially produced semblance will stop protecting the freedom of science, because it no longer grasps its value.

## Limitations

- Blog essay, not peer-reviewed; the argument rests on the strength of an analogy.
- The Benjamin parallel is productive but not defended against the obvious disanalogy — a reproduced painting is a copy of an original, while an LLM text has no original.
- "Vergesellschaftung der Kriterien" is a direction, not a mechanism; who convenes it and how disciplinary standards survive it is left open.
- The capabilities approach is invoked rather than operationalized for research assessment.

## Open questions

- What would a capability-based research evaluation actually measure?
- Can quality criteria be made publicly intelligible without being flattened into the indicators the essay rejects?
- Does the cult value survive as anything other than a private good once it stops being institutionally rewarded?

## My take

Two of the moves here are unusually well aimed. The cult/exhibition distinction gives a compact account of why cheap generation is corrosive rather than merely inflationary: it decouples the visible product from the formative process that used to be its only source, so the indicator survives while what it indicated disappears. And the refusal to compete on productivity is the right conclusion drawn from the right premise — that yardstick was borrowed from economics in the first place.

The socialization proposal is the weaker and more interesting part. It runs against the argument that quality criteria are constituted inside disciplinary cultures and cannot be made generic — publicly intelligible criteria and discipline-specific criteria pull in opposite directions, and the essay does not reconcile them. But the underlying observation stands: criteria that only insiders can apply cannot do public work in a world where anyone can produce plausible science.
