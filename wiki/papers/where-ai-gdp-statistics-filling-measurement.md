## Problem

The US AI economy is growing at over 2,000% per year in quality-adjusted terms, yet it leaves only a small mark in conventional GDP statistics. This measurement gap — if unaddressed — becomes a policy gap, because fiscal authorities, central banks, and policymakers cannot steer what they cannot measure.

## Key idea

Three separable measurements of AI growth tell a coherent but largely invisible story:

1. **Nominal compute spending**: $37B (2023) → $90B (2024) → $219B (2025) — ~145% per year
2. **Raw compute capacity** (H100-equivalent units): >200% per year (chips becoming more efficient per dollar)
3. **Quality-adjusted AI output**: ~2,290% in 2024, ~2,271% in 2025 — driven primarily by algorithmic progress (inference prices fell ~94%/year at fixed benchmark performance)

These figures do not appear in headline GDP because: (a) nominal AI revenues grow only moderately — quality-adjusted gains are offset by collapsing per-unit prices; (b) AI activity is dispersed across many industry codes with no single capture category; (c) conventional hedonic adjustment methods were designed for sectors where quality improves slowly.

The structural reason AI mismeasurement may eventually matter more than past fast-tech cases (internet, semiconductors): AI is a potential **labor substitute**, not merely a complement. Prior technology mismeasurement was bounded by human bottlenecks; AI's is not.

## Method

Policy brief summarizing a companion PIIE Working Paper (Korinek and McKelvey 2026). Methodology:
- GPU rental rates × chip stock → nominal compute spending (cross-checked with global chip-sales data)
- H100-equivalent unit counts → raw compute capacity
- Chained Fisher price index for AI inference tokens at fixed benchmark performance → quality-adjusted output growth
- "AI GDP" framework: partitions value creation between AI computation and human brain activity; estimates ~$250B nominal AI GDP in 2025, growing ~2,600%/year quality-adjusted

Three policy recommendations:
1. Develop **AI satellite accounts** within existing national accounts (similar to digital economy satellite accounts)
2. Structured collaboration between statistical agencies, AI firms, and researchers for targeted disclosure protocols
3. Incorporate AI productive-capacity measures into medium-term fiscal and monetary projections immediately, even before satellite accounts are complete

## Results

- US nominal AI compute spending: ~$219B in 2025, growing ~145%/year
- Quality-adjusted AI output: ~2,290% growth in 2024; ~2,271% in 2025
- Inference prices (at fixed benchmark performance): fell ~94%/year, 2023–2026
- Estimated nominal AI GDP: ~$250B in 2025 — comparable to US scheduled passenger airline industry
- Illustrative GDP adjustment: if AI were deflated using quality-adjusted price index, reported real GDP growth would be ~2 ppts higher in 2024, ~4 ppts higher in 2025 (presented as upper bound)

## Limitations

- Quality-adjusted estimates rely on benchmark-performance anchors rather than directly observed economic value; the mapping from benchmark improvement to value delivered is uncertain
- AI inference is overwhelmingly intermediate input, not final good — quality gains may not proportionally increase final output
- "AI GDP" framework is explicitly preliminary and speculative; requires further development before policy use
- Consumer surplus from AI services is not captured (same limitation as for prior digital economy debates)
- The labor-substitution trajectory that drives the forward-looking argument remains a scenario, not a fact

## Open questions

- When and how will AI become a close enough substitute for labor that its rapidly growing productive capacity begins to register in nominal GDP? What triggers the "phase change"?
- What design of AI satellite accounts best captures both nominal and quality-adjusted growth without requiring headline GDP methodology changes?
- Can we develop measures of AI value delivered to users (economic value) rather than AI capability per benchmark token?
- How should fiscal authorities design tax instruments (compute tax, AI services excise, windfall levy) when the base is currently unmeasured?

## My take

A sharply focused policy brief that makes a compelling case for measurement urgency rather than measurement perfection. The "immiserizing growth" framing (Bhagwati 1958 applied to AI) is elegant: AI services are expanding in volume and quality while nominal revenues grow modestly, so the sector is nearly invisible in GDP statistics even as its productive capacity more than doubles annually. The labor-substitution argument for why AI mismeasurement will eventually dwarf internet/semiconductor measurement debates is the paper's biggest intellectual contribution — and it's a forward-looking claim that will become increasingly testable as AI adoption broadens. The three recommendations are actionable and sensibly calibrated: satellite accounts now, before the gap becomes acute.
