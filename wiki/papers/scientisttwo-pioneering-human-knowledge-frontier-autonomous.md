## Problem & Context

Autonomous research agents have moved from single-metric ML engineering (MLAgentBench, AIDE, MLE-STAR) toward full pipelines that attempt literature review, hypothesis generation, execution and manuscript drafting end to end (AI Scientist, AI Scientist-v2, ScientistOne). But the paper's related-work section argues that even the strongest prior systems share two structural gaps: they optimize a single scalar metric on a single dataset rather than reasoning holistically across multi-dataset, multi-metric benchmarks, and they lack the closed-loop empirical rigor of human researchers — no systematic ablation to isolate *why* a result holds, and no dynamic peer-review process that forces the agent to run new experiments in response to criticism rather than just polishing prose. Every published autonomous-research-agent's own generated papers, when the authors here score them with two independent AI reviewers, in fact draw a **0% acceptance rate** from a held-out reviewer — that 0% baseline across seven prior systems (AI-Researcher, CycleResearcher, AI Scientist-v2, AutoResearchClaw, Zochi, DeepScientist, ScientistOne) is the empirical anchor the paper's claims are measured against.

## Key idea

**ScientistTwo**: an autonomous multi-agent framework built around three components that its ablations argue are jointly necessary — (1) holistic, multi-dataset benchmark reasoning with a subset-first screening strategy to save compute before committing to full-scale runs; (2) ablation-driven hypothesis refinement, where an Ablation Critic Agent inspects component-level contribution and can send a candidate method back for revision even after it beats the baseline; (3) a dynamic peer-review/rebuttal loop in which a simulated Reviewer Agent (ScholarPeer) scores drafts on the 1–10 ICLR scale, and — if the score is below 8 — a Rebuttal Planner + Rebuttal Coding Agent design and *execute* new supplementary experiments (not just rewrite text) to answer the reviewer, followed by a Meta-Review Agent that can force the whole idea-refinement loop to restart if it judges the paper still short of top-tier standard. The evaluation design itself is the paper's second key move: rather than a synthetic or verifier-scored benchmark, ScientistTwo is tested against **107 problems whose ground truth is an already-published, peer-reviewed ICLR/ICML/NeurIPS paper** ([[accepted-paper-baseline-benchmarking]]) — the human-authored accepted paper supplies the state-of-the-art baseline and the problem specification, and the agent must independently discover something that beats it.

## Method

Formally, the framework is a composition of ~15+ specialized agents built around a single-idea state machine: given a problem, the Limitation Extractor/Verifier loop identifies actionable gaps in the human SOTA method; an Idea Generator produces and novelty-scores seed ideas; a Subset Coding/Critic/Engineering loop cheaply triages each idea as Good/Bad/needs-Engineering before any idea is scaled to the full benchmark; an Idea Evolver mines both successful and failed execution traces to propose refined hypotheses in subsequent rounds, deliberately mixed with fresh unevaluated seed ideas to avoid local-optimum lock-in; a Selector Agent picks the best full-scale candidate; an Ablation Planner/Coding/Critic loop isolates which components of the winning idea actually drive the gain and can trigger a further refinement round; and finally a Drafter (built on PaperOrchestra) writes the manuscript, a Reviewer Agent (ScholarPeer) and a held-out Stanford Agentic Reviewer score it, a Rebuttal Agent runs new experiments against reviewer critique, and a Meta-Review Agent gates final acceptance. All primary experiments use Gemini 3.6 Flash and Claude Opus 4.8 (via Claude Code) for coding; a generalization check swaps in Antigravity/Gemini 3.8 Flash on 5 ICLR-2026 tasks. A post-hoc "CoE Integrity Audit" (borrowed from the ScientistOne paper, Meng et al. 2026) checks four things per generated paper: reported scores reproduce on re-execution, no reward-hacking/spec violations, no hallucinated citations, and the paper's method section actually matches the code.

## Experiment & Results

- **Head-to-head vs. 7 prior autonomous-research-agent systems** (86 ScientistTwo papers vs. 2–21 papers each for baselines): ScientistTwo scores **7.5/10 avg. (ScholarPeer, in-distribution reviewer)** with a **91.9% acceptance rate**, versus the best prior baseline (ScientistOne) at 3.8/10 and 14.3%. On the held-out **Stanford Agentic Reviewer**, every one of the 7 prior systems scores **0% acceptance**; ScientistTwo alone reaches **5.7/10 avg. and 72.1% acceptance**.
- **Vs. accepted human papers at the source venues**: human-accepted baselines score 6.2–6.9/10 under ScholarPeer with 60–80% acceptance; ScientistTwo's generated papers on the *same* 107 problems score higher on average (7.0–7.6/10, 88–100% acceptance) under ScholarPeer, and are roughly comparable under the held-out Stanford reviewer (5.4–5.7 vs. 5.2–6.1 for humans). Overall: **86/107 problems successfully advanced (80.4%)**, with a mean relative improvement of **25.2%** over the human SOTA baseline. For context, 4 AI-generated papers accepted at Agent4Science 2025 (the first AI-generated-paper venue) score only 3.0/10 and 0% acceptance under the same reviewers — a calibration point showing the reviewers aren't simply rubber-stamping AI text.
- **Vs. AutoSOTA** (a narrower system that only edits existing codebases for a single scalar metric): ScientistTwo shows a larger average and median relative improvement over human SOTA on the shared NeurIPS-2025 subset, though it under-performs slightly on ICLR-2026 tasks; a manual pass over the 5 shared ICLR papers found none of AutoSOTA's edits introduced a new algorithmic component (configuration-level changes only), which the authors use to argue their gain comes from genuinely new methodological ideas rather than hyperparameter search.
- **Ablations** (49 ICML-2026-Spotlight tasks): relative gain over baseline rises through iterative idea-refinement rounds, with the largest jumps early; after the first round, most winning ideas come from the Idea *Evolver* rather than the original seed pool. The Rebuttal Agent measurably improves acceptance under both reviewers, including the held-out one, even though only ScholarPeer's own critiques are used as the reward signal for rebuttal — evidence the rebuttal loop generalizes rather than overfits to one grader. Without any rebuttal step at all, ScientistTwo's first-draft acceptance rate is still ~50% under both reviewers, ahead of the prior SOTA. A worked case (idea "LFR-Engram" beating human baseline Engram, but rejected as insufficiently novel by the Meta-Review Agent, then superseded by "FCD-Engram" after review-driven refinement) is used to argue the meta-review gate is not decorative.
- **CoE integrity audit**: with all four dedicated audit-refinement agents in place, ScientistTwo passes all four integrity checks; removing them produces sporadic failures (reward hacking, hallucinated citations, code/paper mismatch) — the audit machinery is load-bearing, not just a reporting layer.
- **Human evaluation** (33 NeurIPS-derived papers, 9 expert reviewers, 1–5 Likert): all six dimensions score above the 3.0 "positive endorsement" threshold (3.7–4.3 standalone). In head-to-head comparison against the corresponding human-authored paper, ScientistTwo is preferred on benchmark breadth and ablation design (3.1–3.5, human < ScientistTwo), judged at parity on overall scientific maturity (3.0), and rated behind humans specifically on methodological soundness/rigor (2.9, human > ScientistTwo) — the one dimension where the paper concedes a real, not just cosmetic, gap.
- **Compounding discovery** (case study, incremental BPE tokenization): ScientistTwo first discovers VD-STrans (+10.9% over the existing SOTA), then, given VD-STrans as context in a second discovery cycle, discovers BXT-Transducer (+9.6% over VD-STrans), then SBR-Transducer (+8.2% over BXT-Transducer) in a third cycle — evidence of self-compounding improvement across sequential runs, not just single-shot gains.
- **Cost/time**: average full-pipeline cost **$3,765–$3,800** per problem, 2–3 days wall-clock, with most time spent in idea refinement, dynamic peer review and meta-review — the iterative benchmark-evaluation/code-execution loop, not idea generation, dominates cost.

## Limitations

- **Self-graded on two of its own components.** ScholarPeer is used both as the training/refinement signal *and* as one of the two evaluation reviewers; the paper's defense (rebuttal gains transfer to the held-out Stanford reviewer too) is real but partial — the primary 91.9% acceptance figure is still an in-distribution number.
- **The ground-truth baseline is an already-published, already-solved problem.** ScientistTwo is told the exact scientific question a human team already answered successfully and is handed that team's benchmark/dataset/metric setup; this is a different (weaker) evaluation design than "shadow evaluation" against a genuinely *unpublished* question (cf. [[ai-agents-conduct-open-ended-ai]]) — success here demonstrates the agent can out-execute a known-solvable problem, not that it can find and correctly frame an open one.
- **No held-out check on problem selection/publication bias.** 107 problems were chosen from top venues; the paper doesn't report how many additional candidate problems were tried and excluded, so a survivorship effect on the reported 80.4% success rate can't be ruled out.
- **Cost is explicitly flagged by the authors themselves** (~$3,800/task) as a barrier to academic-lab or independent-researcher adoption.
- **The authors' own concession**: ScientistTwo does not reach spotlight/oral-level "paradigm-shifting" quality — gains are characterized as compounding local refinements, not conceptual breakthroughs, and the human-eval data corroborates this (behind humans specifically on methodological rigor).
- **AI-reviewer validity is assumed, not independently established** — ScholarPeer and the Stanford Agentic Reviewer are both themselves LLM-based systems of unknown and unaudited calibration against actual human program-committee outcomes at scale (the 33-paper human-eval subset is the only genuine human check, and it is not blind to authorship, unlike a real conference review).

## Open questions

- Does the "advance a known solved problem" evaluation design generalize to genuinely open, unsolved questions — the harder and more decision-relevant test that [[ai-agents-conduct-open-ended-ai]]'s shadow-evaluation design targets and where two comparable frontier-agent efforts were unambiguously rejected by expert human graders?
- Is the 25.2% average relative gain concentrated in a few benchmark-friendly subfields (tokenization, time-series forecasting) versus more conceptually demanding areas — the paper's own case studies lean heavily on efficiency/engineering-style gains?
- Can the review-rebuttal loop's apparent generalization (ScholarPeer-trained gains transferring to the held-out Stanford reviewer) survive a reviewer that is deliberately adversarial or trained independently of any shared training data with ScholarPeer?
- What happens to the $3,800/task cost and the 2–3 day runtime as problem difficulty increases beyond the ICLR/ICML/NeurIPS-accepted tier used here?
- Given the "methodological soundness" gap the authors concede (human eval, 2.9/5, humans ahead), is that gap a training/scaffold limitation or a structural one tied to agents lacking the same stakes/consequences as human authors risking their reputation on a claim?

## My take

The honest core result is narrower than the framing implies, and the paper is unusually good about saying so itself: ScientistTwo is very good at out-executing a research problem whose answer already exists in the literature it was (likely, given foundation-model training data cutoffs and web access during the agent run) able to see traces of via citations and community discussion of the target papers' follow-up work. That's a real and useful capability — 80.4% success at beating a known-good human baseline, with actual ablations and an integrity audit that other systems conspicuously lack — but it is a different and easier claim than [[ai-agents-conduct-open-ended-ai]]'s shadow-evaluation result, where two frontier agents were graded on an *unpublished* research question by the paper's own original authors and were unambiguously rejected on judgment grounds (poor resource use, uncreative response to review feedback, premature abandonment of ambitious hypotheses). Read together, the two papers triangulate on the same fault line from opposite sides: agents can already do execution-heavy iterative refinement extremely well (this paper), but judgment under real open-endedness and novelty is still the bottleneck (that paper) — and the one dimension ScientistTwo's own human evaluators rank it behind humans on is exactly "methodological rigor," the closest proxy for judgment in this rubric.

The 0%-acceptance calibration finding for all seven prior baselines under the held-out reviewer is arguably the paper's most durable contribution regardless of how ScientistTwo itself ages: it establishes, for the first time with a reasonably matched evaluation harness, that "autonomous research agent" systems as a class have been producing papers that fail even lenient automated review, and gives a concrete number to a claim that was previously mostly anecdotal.
