## Problem & Context

In an ordinary personal-injury suit (a passenger allegedly injured by a serving cart on an Avianca flight), plaintiff's attorneys filed a brief citing multiple judicial decisions that did not exist — they had been fabricated ("hallucinated") by ChatGPT, which the lawyers used for research without verifying the output. The court (Judge Castel, SDNY, June 2023) addressed sanctions.

## Key idea

Using a generative AI tool does not displace a professional's duty to verify: the attorneys, not the tool, bear responsibility for submitting fabricated citations. The case establishes that AI hallucination is a foreseeable failure mode against which the human user must guard.

## Method

Judicial opinion / sanctions decision. The court examined the fabricated citations, the attorneys' reliance on ChatGPT, their failure to verify when the citations could not be located, and their conduct after the fabrications were questioned.

## Experiment & Results

No empirics (a court decision). Outcome: the attorneys were sanctioned for submitting non-existent authorities and for the manner in which they responded once the citations were challenged. The opinion documents ChatGPT's confident fabrication of case names, citations, and quotations, and the professional-responsibility consequences of unverified reliance.

## Limitations

- A single first-instance decision on specific facts; not a general liability rule for AI.
- About user (attorney) responsibility, not deployer/tool liability.
- Predates most bar guidance on AI use.

## Open questions

- What standard of verification does competent AI-assisted practice require?
- How should courts and regulators treat AI hallucination across professions (medicine, engineering)?
- Where does responsibility shift from user to tool provider, if ever?

## My take

Avianca is the canonical "duty to verify" case: the human is appropriately held responsible, which is the *inverse* of a moral crumple zone (here the human genuinely had control and skipped the check). It anchors one pole of the AI-accountability debate — user liability for unverified AI output — against the deployer-liability pole set by Air Canada. The enduring lesson is that confident fabrication plus unverified reliance is a professional-responsibility failure, not a technology excuse.
