## Problem

Technological progress — especially AI and digitalization — is transforming society faster than governance frameworks can adapt. Previous Catholic Social Teaching (from Leo XIII's Rerum Novarum, 1891, to Laudato Si and Laudate Deum) addressed industrial and environmental challenges through principled frameworks. How should the Church, and humanity, respond to the "res novae" (new things) of AI and digital technology?

## Key idea

The encyclical develops a normative framework for AI governance grounded in the Social Doctrine of the Church: human dignity (image of the Triune God), common good, subsidiarity, and solidarity. Technology is not inherently antagonistic to humanity but must be oriented toward the authentic fullness of human life. The document explicitly rejects transhumanism and posthumanism as governing narratives, defends the dignity of work in a time of AI-driven displacement, calls for an "ecology of communication" against AI-amplified disinformation, and addresses the application of AI to weapons and warfare.

## Method

Theological encyclical — a teaching document of the Catholic Magisterium. Builds on Scripture, Catholic Social Doctrine tradition (Leo XIII through Benedict XVI and Francis), and engagement with the human sciences. Not an empirical study; normative in nature.

## Results

Key positions across five chapters:
1. **On AI governance**: AI is "a valuable tool that requires vigilance." The Church calls for responsibility, transparency, and governance frameworks that embed human values. AI governance should not be left solely to technical experts or market actors.
2. **Against transhumanism/posthumanism**: The promise that AI will free humanity from all weakness and transcend human limits is a "deceitful goal" that contradicts the dignity of the human person. Accepting limits and weakness is constitutive of authentic human development.
3. **On work and dignity**: The encyclical defends the intrinsic dignity of work against AI-driven displacement; calls for policies that value dignity over efficiency, including support for workers and families.
4. **On truth and communication**: Calls for an "ecology of communication" to counter AI-enabled manipulation of the collective imagination; emphasizes digital literacy and educational alliances.
5. **On warfare and weapons**: Explicitly condemns the application of AI to autonomous weapons systems; reinforces the necessity of multilateralism against the "normalization of war."
6. **On subsidiarity**: AI governance should operate at multiple levels, with active roles for civil society, communities, and individuals, not only national or supranational authority.

## Limitations

- Normative theological document; empirical claims about AI impacts are not independently sourced.
- Positions on specific AI applications (weapons, content moderation) are framed in general principles; detailed policy recommendations are sparse.
- Speaks primarily from within the Catholic tradition; engagement with non-Catholic and non-Western perspectives is limited.

## Open questions

- How will Catholic institutions operationalize these principles in AI research, deployment, and education?
- How does the "ecology of communication" translate into specific content governance recommendations?
- What is the threshold at which the Church would endorse restriction of AI capabilities vs. resilience-building?

## My take

A historically significant document — the first papal encyclical directly addressing AI. The framing of transhumanism/posthumanism as the central counter-narrative to reject is sharper than most secular AI ethics documents, which tend to focus on bias and safety rather than metaphysical foundations. The explicit treatment of AI weapons and multilateralism brings the document into concrete policy terrain. Resonates with and provides religious grounding for secular positive-alignment frameworks. Comparable in scope to Laudato Si for environmental ethics — whether it has similar cultural impact depends on implementation and uptake.
