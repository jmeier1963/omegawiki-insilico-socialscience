## Problem & Context

Technological history is invoked on both sides of the AI debate: optimists infer that regulating AI would be counterproductive because new technologies are usually net-beneficial; skeptics point to over-hyped inventions that underdelivered. Tokson argues both readings misuse history, which is less reassuring than either camp assumes.

## Key idea

The lessons of technological history do not support complacency about AI. Examining episodes where elites made grave errors — sometimes from excessive optimism, sometimes from excessive skepticism — shows that novel technologies can exceed expectations, harm society, cause serious environmental damage, and even risk global catastrophe. Policymakers should find little basis in history for a hands-off stance.

## Method

Legal/historical essay. Surveys several historical episodes of elite misjudgment about new technologies and threats, drawing out a two-sided error pattern (both complacent optimism and dismissive skepticism have led to grave outcomes) and applying it to current AI-regulation debates.

## Experiment & Results

No empirics. Argumentative payload: (1) the "history shows tech is beneficial, so don't regulate" inference is unsound — history also shows technologies that harmed society or risked catastrophe; (2) the "tech underdelivers, so AI won't matter" inference is equally unsound — technologies have exceeded expectations; (3) therefore historical analogy cannot justify complacency in either direction, and regulators should not defer to it.

## Limitations

- Selective historical case analysis; not a systematic study.
- Refutes complacency but offers limited positive prescription for how to regulate.
- Legal-essay format; normative rather than empirical.

## Open questions

- If history can't guide AI policy by analogy, what should ground precaution vs. permissiveness?
- How should regulators weigh low-probability catastrophic risks against innovation costs?
- Which historical episodes are the right reference class for AI, if any?

## My take

The essay's value is symmetric debunking: it dismantles the optimist's "history says don't regulate" *and* the skeptic's "history says AI won't matter" with the same move — history contains grave errors in both directions, so analogy licenses neither complacency nor dismissal. That directly challenges the "AI as normal technology" framing's implication that existing institutions suffice, making it a useful counterweight rather than a rival theory. It's stronger at clearing the ground than at building the positive case for a specific regulatory posture.
