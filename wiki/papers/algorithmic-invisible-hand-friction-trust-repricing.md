## Problem & Context

Since Adam Smith (1776), market theory implicitly assumed that the physics of human cognition — latency, fatigue, sequential processing — acts as a natural stabilizer. Existing labor economics addresses task-level automation (Brynjolfsson & McAfee; Acemoglu & Restrepo) but lacks a framework for what happens when cognition itself becomes near-free and never rests. Shaik (dated symbolically from ChatGPT's Nov 2022 release) argues agentic AI ruptures the assumption.

## Key idea

The "Algorithmic Hand" that replaces Smith's metaphor does not sleep, tire, or process sequentially, and can negotiate with a billion counterparties at once — systematically removing the friction that once slowed markets, generated employment, and sustained institutional trust. This defines an **Agent Economy** of near-zero cognitive marginal cost, algorithmic intermediation, and structural obsolescence of friction-dependent roles.

## Method

Theoretical/conceptual monograph-derived paper making three contributions: (1) **Friction Economics** — reconceptualizing human cognitive latency not as inefficiency but as a systemic damping mechanism whose removal increases volatility and collapse risk; (2) the **Trust Standard** — a three-layer model (verification, reputation, relational trust) replacing information arbitrage as the primary source of pricing power; (3) identification of the mechanism-design infrastructure needed to operationalize trust and reprice human capital in the Agent Economy.

## Experiment & Results

No empirics (theory). Core claims: friction was load-bearing (it damped volatility, created employment, anchored trust), so its removal is destabilizing, not merely efficient; pricing power migrates from information arbitrage to layered trust; friction-dependent human roles face structural obsolescence, and human capital must be repriced around what remains scarce (verified, reputational, relational trust).

## Limitations

- Conceptual and metaphor-driven; no formal model or data.
- Single-author monograph derivation; claims are programmatic.
- "Friction as stabilizer" is asserted; the volatility mechanism is not formalized or tested.

## Open questions

- Can Friction Economics be formalized and empirically tested (does friction removal measurably raise volatility)?
- What mechanism-design infrastructure actually operationalizes the Trust Standard?
- Which human roles retain value once cognitive friction is priced out?

## My take

The provocative core is inverting the usual framing: friction is normally the thing to eliminate, and this argues it was a hidden stabilizer whose removal raises collapse risk — a genuinely different lens on the agent economy. The Trust Standard (value migrating to verification/reputation/relational trust) is the constructive half and rhymes with the wiki's cognitive-abundance and cloud-capitalism threads. It's theory without a model, so its value is the framing, not proof; it pairs naturally with the same author's Centaur Organization work.
