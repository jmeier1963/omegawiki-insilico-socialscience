## Problem & Context

The 1985 Product Liability Directive (85/374/EEC) was built for tangible manufactured goods and fit poorly with software, digital services, and AI. Directive (EU) 2024/2853 (adopted 23 October 2024) modernizes strict product liability for the digital age and repeals the old regime.

## Key idea

Bring software and AI systems within strict product liability: a defective AI system is a defective product, its provider can be liable without fault for harm it causes, and claimants facing complex, opaque systems get evidentiary help (disclosure duties and rebuttable presumptions of defectiveness/causation) to overcome the difficulty of proving how an AI failed.

## Method

EU legislative instrument (directive) for transposition into national law. Key modernizations: explicit inclusion of software/AI as "products"; coverage of post-market changes (updates, machine learning); disclosure obligations; rebuttable presumptions easing the burden of proof where technical/scientific complexity makes defect or causation excessively difficult to establish.

## Experiment & Results

No empirics. The consequential provisions for AI: (1) software and AI are products subject to strict liability; (2) "defect" accounts for the product's continued learning/updating; (3) claimants can obtain evidence disclosure and benefit from presumptions of defectiveness/causation in complex cases — materially lowering the barrier to holding AI providers liable.

## Limitations

- A directive requiring national transposition; effects depend on member-state implementation and courts.
- Strict-liability scope for evolving, learning systems raises hard line-drawing (what counts as a defect in an adaptive model?).
- Interacts with, but is separate from, the EU AI Act.

## Open questions

- How will "defect" be applied to systems that change behavior post-deployment through learning?
- Do the presumptions meaningfully solve the opacity/burden-of-proof problem in practice?
- How do product-liability and AI-Act obligations combine for a single AI system?

## My take

This is the EU institutionalizing the deployer-side answer to the AI accountability gap: where Air Canada was a single tribunal treating a chatbot's words as the company's, this makes "defective AI = defective product, provider strictly liable" the default across the bloc, with burden-of-proof relief aimed squarely at AI opacity. The genuinely novel and contestable move is subjecting *learning, self-updating* systems to strict product liability — it presses the responsibility-gap problem (who is liable when a system's post-deployment behavior wasn't foreseeable?) rather than dissolving it.
