## Problem & Context

Germany's National Once-Only Technical System (NOOTS) advances the modest-sounding promise that citizens should not have to resubmit information the state already holds — a plausible, overdue efficiency step. Pretschner argues the public conversation about digital-government modernization ("Registermodernisierung") understates what this technically entails: digitalization's real lever is not automating individual forms but making information usable across previously separate organizational, departmental, and jurisdictional boundaries — some of which are mere technical legacy, but others of which are deliberately designed protections (separation of powers, data protection, division of responsibilities between intelligence services and police).

## Key idea

Digital information detaches data from its original carrier, making it freely copyable, linkable, and recombinable; AI makes this integrability practically exploitable at scale, including for tasks (case assessment, inter-agency coordination, judgment calls, exceptions) that cannot be fully captured in formal rules. This integrability is not a neutral tool: it produces a structural power shift at two nested levels with opposite signs. Internally, the same property that lets the state combine previously-separated data pools makes it more powerful, by dissolving boundaries (federalism, separation of powers, the constitutionally-grounded separation between intelligence services and police, data-protection-as-fundamental-right) originally designed to *limit* state power — Pretschner calls this the "Kernparadox" (core paradox): integration that removes friction and inefficiency simultaneously removes the protective separations whose entire purpose was friction. Externally, the same integrability makes the state more *dependent*, because realizing integration's benefits requires coupling to global cloud, model, and infrastructure providers the state cannot control (the essay's example: data integration with a provider like Palantir) — creating a reversed version of the same trade-off: too much control concentrated in one place internally, too little control over one's own capacity to act externally.

## Method

A structured conceptual argument rather than an empirical study. (1) Distinguishes digital information's generic properties (combinability, modifiability, lossless/unlimited reproducibility, remote/concurrent access, persistence, interface-mediated invisibility, scalability) and argues that for public administration specifically, *integrability* is the property that matters most, because administration is fundamentally "binding information processing across organizational boundaries" and integrability is exactly what erodes those boundaries. (2) Defines a four-level integration hierarchy — data linkage (including semantic linkage), system coupling via interfaces, process consolidation into single workflows, and dissolution of institutional responsibilities — arguing the lower levels are technical while the higher levels are increasingly political, since "integration at the responsibility level is often not a technical but a political problem." (3) Reviews why larger integrated data spaces do not automatically translate into proportional benefit, citing the Solow/Brynjolfsson productivity paradox (massive IT investment without matching measured productivity gains), the Jevons paradox (efficiency gains consumed by induced demand), GDP mismeasurement when digitalization eliminates paid intermediaries in favor of unpaid self-service labor (a concrete worked example: a €1,000 travel booking via a travel agent becomes a €930 platform booking, lowering measured GDP despite the customer paying less — accompanied by a shift from 30 minutes of paid agent labor + 2 minutes of customer labor to 1 minute of platform labor + 20 minutes of unpaid customer labor), the well-established finding that IT alone does not raise productivity without accompanying organizational change, and the observation that administration's actual bottlenecks (case-by-case judgment, inter-agency coordination, accountability for decisions, handling exceptions) resist rule-based formalization in the first place. (4) Argues AI specifically targets exactly these unformalizable bottlenecks by replacing software's traditional binary correctness criterion with continuous, approximate correctness ("adequate in 85% of cases"), which lets it integrate and evaluate heterogeneous, semantically-linked data (case files, notes, free text) that rule-based systems could never combine — and that AI and integration reinforce each other in a loop: larger, cleaner integrated data spaces improve AI, and better AI enables further integration. (5) Invokes the German Federal Constitutional Court's 1983 census ruling (Volkszählungsurteil), which established the right to informational self-determination specifically because individually-harmless data becomes dangerous through linkage — arguing this logic now applies, far more sharply, at the level of entire AI-analyzed integrated data spaces rather than individual record linkages. (6) Proposes three governance principles for the internal trade-off, since (unlike the external trade-off) no redundancy strategy can substitute for separations that are themselves the protection: integration at the responsibility level should never be a byproduct of a purely technical decision but should require explicit negotiation of power relations and a documented power-distribution impact assessment; separation, not integration, should bear the burden of justification; and architectures should remain reversible and auditable so decisions are not rendered irreversible by lock-in.

## Experiment & Results

No empirical experiment; the "result" is the argued structural claim itself — that efficiency gain and power shift are not separable side effects but arise from the same root property (integrability), such that every integration decision simultaneously distributes informational power, whether or not that distribution was consciously intended. The essay explicitly separates the two trade-offs' resolvability: the external trade-off (state vs. global provider dependency) can be managed by politics through redundancy-style strategies — supply-chain resilience, emergency-capacity reserves, redundant critical data/systems, technological "leapfrogging" rather than catch-up imitation, and treating dependency as mutual leverage rather than pure risk — because a second provider can in principle replace a first. The internal trade-off (integration vs. checks-and-balances) cannot be hedged this way for a structural reason: separation itself *is* the protection, and no redundancy substitutes for a separation once it has been dissolved.

## Limitations

- Entirely a conceptual/normative argument; no case study tracing an actual completed NOOTS or comparable integration project through to a measured power-concentration or dependency outcome is presented — the Palantir example is invoked illustratively, not analyzed in depth.
- The claim that AI's shift to continuous/approximate correctness is *the* enabling mechanism for large-scale semantic integration is asserted rather than benchmarked against the actual reliability of current AI systems on the heterogeneous records (case files, notes, free text) the essay cites as newly integrable.
- The three proposed governance principles (power-distribution impact assessment, separation-as-default, reversibility/auditability) are stated as goals without a concrete institutional mechanism for enforcing them — who conducts the impact assessment, with what authority to block integration, is left unspecified.
- Written from a German/EU administrative-law vantage point (citing the 1983 Volkszählungsurteil, Gewaltenteilung, Föderalismus); the generalizability of the specific legal/constitutional framing to jurisdictions without comparable separation-of-powers doctrine is not addressed, though the author positions the underlying mechanism (integrability-driven power shift) as structurally general.

## Open questions

- What would a concrete, auditable power-distribution impact assessment for a specific integration decision actually look like, and who should have the authority to veto integration on its basis?
- Can "integrability" be measured or bounded in a way that lets policymakers set quantitative limits (analogous to compute thresholds in frontier-AI governance) on how far an integrated data space may extend before triggering mandatory review?
- Does the external trade-off's redundancy-based mitigation (multiple providers, leapfrogging, mutual dependency) actually hold once a jurisdiction's administrative, identity, and AI infrastructure is deeply coupled to a single ecosystem, or does lock-in eventually foreclose the "second provider can replace the first" assumption the essay relies on?
- How does the internal power-concentration risk interact with the agentic-AI governance proposals elsewhere in this wiki (e.g. externalized containment architectures) — does externalizing AI controls mitigate or deepen the internal integration/checks-and-balances trade-off, since the controlling infrastructure itself becomes another point of integration?

## My take

The essay's central contribution is identifying *integrability* specifically — not scalability, reproducibility, or any other generic digital property — as the single lever that matters for public administration, and showing that the same lever produces opposite-signed trade-offs at two nested scales (internal power concentration vs. external dependency) rather than two unrelated problems. This reframing directly sharpens this wiki's existing [[digital-sovereignty]] coverage, which has mostly addressed the external leg (infrastructure/data/regulatory sovereignty vis-à-vis foreign providers) without an equally developed internal leg (integration dissolving domestic checks-and-balances) — Pretschner's "Kernparadox" supplies exactly that missing internal half, and ties both legs to a common causal root rather than treating them as a coincidental pairing. The asymmetry the essay draws between the two trade-offs — external risk hedgeable via redundancy, internal risk not hedgeable because separation *is* the protection — is its sharpest normative claim, and is the part most directly actionable for policy: it implies that institutional firewalls (data-protection law, separation of intelligence and police, federalism) deserve categorically different treatment from vendor-dependency risk, rather than being bundled together as generic "digital risks" the way public debate often does.
