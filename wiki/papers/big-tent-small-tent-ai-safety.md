## Problem & Context

Written in October 2026, after the summer's agent-swarm hacks (see [[anatomy-frontier-lab-agent-intrusion-technical]]) and the "Coxon resignation" brought x-risk warnings into mainstream media. Two narratives had formed: either AI existential risk is real and imminent, or the warnings from AI leaders and whistleblowers are insincere (hype, a "psyop," or regulatory capture). The authors propose a third reading that the debate ignores: the warnings are **sincere but wrong, and counterproductive to AI safety itself**. The essay follows directly from their repost on [[ai-existential-risk-probabilities-too-unreliable]].

## Key idea

AI safety covers two distinct diagnoses that compete for the same attention and resources:

1. **Amplified-risk safety.** There are specific risks that predate AI and are amplified by it, catastrophic but short of existential (cyber, bio, accumulative institutional decay). The response is prevention and resilience.
2. **X-risk safety.** AI development is itself an existential threat. The response is to make superintelligence safe (alignment) or to stop it being built.

Because of how the media environment works, the second framing is the one driving public concern, even though it lacks credible causal pathways. The authors predict it will backfire through **polarization** and **misdirection**. Misdirection is distinct from distraction: the two framings are different diagnoses of the same problem, so they lead to different remedies rather than one crowding out attention for the other. A generalized existential threat admits only one policy, a ban, and a ban on "superintelligence" is too vague to be coherent. The remedy they propose is a **[[big-tent-ai-safety]]** movement, pluralist in beliefs, values and policy.

## Method

A perspective-taking argument in three moves:

- **Steelman.** Society systematically underinvests in catastrophic-risk resilience, and EA deserves credit for saying so. Pandemics: a 2019 WHO/World Bank report warned of a pathogen killing 50–80M people and costing about 5% of world GDP, with preparedness costing $1–2 per person per year; most recommendations from 11+ panels since H1N1 went unimplemented. Cybersecurity is worse, with no culture of modeling tail risk; Lloyd's (2022) excludes severe state-backed attacks. Diffuse harms are also real, such as chatbots cutting news clickthrough. Kasirzadeh's notion of *accumulative* risk captures that kind of decay.
- **Media diagnosis.** AI is high-concern but low-salience: about 0.5% of Gallup respondents rate it the top issue. The cyber incidents barely registered, while x-risk is visceral and the safety community stood ready to amplify a newsworthy hook. Public discovery that executives hold a high p(doom) yet keep racing, on a utilitarian value system the public does not share, made the story break through.
- **Consequence analysis.** Partisanship leads to inaction and policy reversals. Panic narrows the solution space to blunt, emotionally soothing measures. Discovering that the "10% extinction" figures have no model behind them will feel like being misled. Bans do nothing about catastrophic risks from existing systems, and pausing datacenter construction is an "impotent" safety measure.

## Experiment & Results

No empirical study. The output is a typology of the two movements:

| Dimension | Big tent | Small tent |
|---|---|---|
| Beliefs | Includes people unconcerned with x-risk, focused on cyber, bio, accumulative risks and resilience | Narrow; centered on superintelligence |
| Values | Pluralist; does not assume EA/safetyist moral frameworks; appeals through positive visions, not panic | Ideologically homogeneous, at risk of a purity spiral |
| Policy | Common ground first: transparency, liability, embedded evaluation; builds capacity for stronger measures (e.g. a ban on fully autonomous recursive self-improvement) by persuading skeptics | Policy by panic; deprioritizes "merely" catastrophic risks; overplays its hand and risks backlash |

Footnotes add that the cybersecurity community resists catastrophe talk because past "Cyber Pearl Harbor" warnings misfired and there are no data points for an actual catastrophe. Measured concentration, for example how much of the web depends on Cloudflare, shows such a catastrophe is possible but is not actionable. The authors also say a ban on recursive self-improvement *without human oversight* is more tractable than a superintelligence ban.

## Limitations

- An opinion essay. Its central prediction, that x-risk framing will prove counterproductive, is stated with explicit uncertainty ("We hope we are wrong").
- The "Coxon incident" and summer agent-swarm hacks are referenced, not documented. The essay depends on context the reader must supply.
- The binary typology is admitted to leave out nuance. Many actors sit in both camps.
- It does not engage the strongest x-risk causal arguments; it asserts that they are unconvincing.

## Open questions

- Can transparency and liability actually build "muscle" for stronger interventions, or do they substitute for them?
- How should a big-tent movement handle intermediate proposals (RSI-without-oversight bans, compute governance) that both camps might endorse for different reasons?
- Is the salience/concern gap stable, or does a visible incident with harm to ordinary people close it?
- Who in the cybersecurity community would own catastrophic and cascading-failure modeling?

## My take

The useful distinction is **misdirection versus distraction**. The authors drop the tired "x-risk distracts from present harms" line and argue something sharper: the two framings prescribe different interventions (one mammoth wall versus a hundred disparate defenses), so they really do compete. That is testable in principle by tracking where safety funding and legislative attention go. The weak link is the assumption that the existential framing can be cleanly separated from the catastrophic one. The essay itself concedes that agent swarms "taking over the entire internet" deserve study, and that is where the two camps' research agendas converge.
