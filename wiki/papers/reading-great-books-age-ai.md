## Problem & Context

Stauffer, Professor of Government at UT Austin and a Cosmos Founding Fellow, writes that his students are increasingly anxious about AI. They fear it will make it harder to live "vigorous, engaged, and autonomous lives." At the same time, the Great Books (Homer, Plato, Machiavelli, Shakespeare) can look like relics: written for readers without information at their fingertips, in societies whose problems are barely recognizable now. The essay asks whether such books still have anything to say once AI can do much of what we call thinking.

## Key idea

The Great Books are **more** valuable in the age of AI, for two reasons:

1. **Habit (form).** Reading them well requires slowing down, following complex arguments, and holding a dialogue with great minds. That is an antidote to what Solzhenitsyn called the "hastiness and superficiality" of modern life, which TikTok, Instagram and AI have intensified. The model is Machiavelli's letter describing four undistracted hours in the "ancient courts of ancient men."
2. **Questions (substance).** They give rich, complex treatments of simple, childlike questions. AI revives one of these: *what does it mean to be human?* Now that machines can "think" faster than we can, we have to ask what it means to think *as human beings*.

## Method

Interpretive essay built around a contrast between two canonical thinkers:

- **Aristotle.** Humans are rational, speaking, justice-sensing political animals who reach their fullest humanity by deliberating and judging together in the polis. AI threatens to take thinking, debating and eventually judging out of our hands, leaving humans who are human in a basic sense but never flourish. Stauffer's image is of people "on couches scrolling through social media feeds while AI does the work of thinking and judging for them."
- **Hobbes.** Humans are matter in motion and more machine-like than we would like. What sets us apart is mutual threat, an endless "desire of power after power," and a distinctive inventive, causal imagination. Hobbes would welcome AI as an expression of that power-seeking and as a way to make lives "interconnected, prosperous, comfortable, civilized, and long."

## Experiment & Results

No empirical component. The argument reaches three conclusions:

- Deciding *who is right, Aristotle or Hobbes*, is something a machine could not get far with. Doing it well requires drawing on lived human experience (anger, hope, longing, moral passion), which serves as a window on human nature. A machine could compile what people have *said* about human nature but could not find those experiences by looking inward.
- To judge the dispute, one must read the books themselves, "not my summaries or AI's."
- The one certain thing about human nature is that it is opaque to us and remains a question. The great authors' disagreement does not cancel them out; it draws the reader into a debate.

## Limitations

- Advocacy, not argument: the claim that AI cannot meaningfully reason about human nature is asserted, not defended against obvious objections (a model trained on human testimony might reason about it competently).
- The Aristotle and Hobbes portraits are deliberately stylized and short. The Hobbes reading in particular (that he would "welcome" AI) is speculative.
- There is no evidence about whether Great Books reading actually builds the attentional habits claimed, or whether those habits transfer.
- It does not address *how* AI could support rather than replace close reading. AI appears only as a source of summaries and distraction.

## Open questions

- Can the attentional benefit of sustained reading be measured, and does it carry over into resistance to [[cognitive-surrender]]?
- Is there a defensible version of the claim that some questions (about human nature, the good life) need first-person experience to answer, or does it collapse under scrutiny?
- Which pedagogies make AI an interlocutor for Great Books reading rather than a shortcut around it?

## My take

A short, well-made humanist essay rather than research. Its most useful move for the wiki is the **Aristotle/Hobbes fork**. It turns the vague worry that "AI threatens our humanity" into two competing anthropologies with opposite verdicts: deliberation and judgment as constitutive of being human, versus power and invention as constitutive of being human. That parallels the anthropological fork in [[wisdom-versus-instrumental-intelligence]], and the Aristotelian worry is close to [[cognitive-surrender]] and to gradual disempowerment at the level of the individual. The asserted limits on machine understanding of human nature are the weakest part and should not be cited as an argument.
