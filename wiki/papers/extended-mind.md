## Problem & Context

Where does the mind stop and the rest of the world begin? Standard answers accept the boundary of skin and skull, or (following Putnam and Burge's semantic externalism) allow that the *meaning* of our words depends on distal, historical facts about the world without granting that present cognitive *processing* itself extends beyond the head. This paper stakes out a third position — "active externalism" — arguing that the environment can play an active, here-and-now role in driving cognition itself, not merely in fixing the reference of our thoughts after the fact.

## Key idea

When part of the world functions as a process which, if it occurred in the head, we would unhesitatingly call part of a cognitive process, then that part of the world is part of the cognitive process — the **parity principle**. Three escalating cases of mental rotation (purely internal; assisted by a screen rotate-button; assisted by a neural implant) are argued to be cognitively equivalent regardless of whether the relevant operation happens inside or outside the skull, because nothing besides the arbitrary skin/skull boundary distinguishes them. This differs from Putnam/Burge-style passive externalism in that the external features here are coupled to the organism in the present and causally drive behavior in real time, rather than sitting inertly at the end of a historical causal chain — a physical duplicate with a different external coupling (e.g., Twin Otto, whose notebook contains different information) behaves differently, whereas Putnam/Burge twins behave identically despite their differing external histories.

## Method

Conceptual argument built around escalating thought experiments rather than empirical data: (1) the three-case mental-rotation sequence (internal, screen-assisted, implant-assisted), motivated by David Kirsh and Paul Maglio's empirical timing data on Tetris players' use of the rotate button, and Kirsh and Maglio's distinction between "epistemic actions" (which alter the world to aid cognition, e.g. physically rotating a Tetris piece to check if it fits) and "pragmatic actions" (which alter the world for their own sake); (2) the central Otto/Inga case pair — Inga recalls the museum's address from biological memory; Otto, who has Alzheimer's, looks it up in a notebook he always carries and immediately trusts — used to argue that non-occurrent (dispositional) belief does not require internal storage, only that information be reliably available, automatically endorsed, and action-guiding; (3) a "Twin Otto" variant (identical to Otto except for a notebook entry with a different, false address) used to show that the notebook's content can be genuinely constitutive of what Otto believes, not just an external prop he consults; (4) systematic rebuttal of six candidate objections (portability/"Naked Mind," reliability of access, bandwidth of access, perceptual vs. introspective access, and others) by showing each objection, applied consistently, would also rule out clearly legitimate cases of ordinary dispositional belief; (5) four proposed criteria that make a case for belief-extension compelling — constancy (rarely acting without consulting the resource), direct availability without difficulty, automatic endorsement upon retrieval, and past conscious endorsement of the stored information; (6) extension of the argument to socially and linguistically distributed cognition and, finally, to the self.

## Experiment & Results

No empirical experiment is original to the paper; it draws on cited empirical results (Kirsh & Maglio 1994's Tetris rotation-timing study: roughly 100ms for a 90-degree physical rotation plus ~200ms to select the button, versus ~1000ms for mental rotation) as motivating evidence and constructs its case for extended cognition through argument. The paper's "result" is the philosophical conclusion that cognitive processes, non-occurrent beliefs, and ultimately the self are not bounded by skin and skull whenever a coupled system meets the constancy/availability/endorsement criteria — with Otto's notebook functioning as the canonical worked example that has since become the field's standard test case.

## Limitations

- Entirely a priori/conceptual; the paper explicitly declines to give categorical answers to many of the puzzle cases it raises (the Filofax, the Internet, socially distributed belief), instead treating extension as a graded, criterion-based matter that can be indeterminate in intermediate cases.
- The fourth proposed criterion (past conscious endorsement) is flagged by the authors themselves as the most arguable of the four and not clearly necessary.
- Does not address how extended cognitive systems should be individuated when multiple external resources, or multiple people, could plausibly satisfy the parity criteria simultaneously (e.g., competing claims between a notebook and a smartphone, or between a spouse and a professional advisor).
- The paper's own examples of decoupling (showering without the notebook, sleeping) are treated as unproblematic exceptions, but the boundary between "occasional decoupling" and "unreliable coupling that undermines belief ascription" is left as a matter of degree rather than given a principled test.

## Open questions

- What exactly individuates one extended cognitive system from another when candidate external resources overlap or compete?
- How does the extended-self thesis bear on moral and legal responsibility, given the paper's own suggestion that interfering with someone's environment could carry the moral weight of interfering with their person?
- Does linguistically- and socially-extended cognition (the waiter, the spouse, the collaborator) admit of the same constancy/availability/endorsement test as the notebook case, or does distributed social cognition need its own criteria?
- How should the framework treat resources (like the modern Internet or an AI assistant) that are consulted so much more often, and so much less reliably session-to-session, than Otto's notebook — does high but inconsistent reliance strengthen or weaken a parity claim?

## My take

This paper is the founding statement of extended-cognition and distributed-cognition research, and the Otto/Inga thought experiment remains the field's reference example decades later — its durability comes from posing the parity principle as a genuinely hard-to-resist argument (any objector must find a "deep" difference between Otto and Inga's cases, not merely point at the skin/skull boundary, which is precisely what's in question) rather than as an assertion. Ingesting it into this wiki alongside contemporary AI-and-cognition literature is directly motivated by how much of the current discourse on AI-driven cognitive offloading, "cognitive surrender," and gradual disempowerment implicitly assumes exactly the boundary this paper argues against: if belief and cognition can already be partly constituted by a notebook (given the right constancy/availability/endorsement profile), then an always-available, high-bandwidth, highly-trusted AI assistant is a candidate for extended-cognitive coupling in a much stronger sense than a notebook ever was — which sharpens rather than resolves the normative question of whether such coupling is a benign extension of mind or a mechanism of the kind of [[gradual-disempowerment]] and agency erosion documented in the AI-safety literature this wiki otherwise covers.
