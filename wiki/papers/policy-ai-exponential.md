## Problem

AI capability advances on an exponential (scaling laws), while policy and legislation move slowly. Amodei frames this with a Treebeard/Hobbits metaphor: the institutions that must respond operate far slower than the technology. With "Powerful AI" ("a country of geniuses in a datacenter") plausibly one or two years out, this timescale mismatch leaves society reacting roughly a year behind the curve. The essay argues for re-imagining five policy areas to "meet the moment."

## Key idea

The era of optionality-preserving, transparency-only policy is over: with concrete frontier risks now demonstrated (e.g., the Claude "Mythos Preview" cyber capabilities), it is time to move to **binding, FAA-style regulation** of frontier models — and, in parallel, to prepare macroeconomic responses for potential enduring labor displacement, while *accelerating* downstream AI applications (e.g., biomedicine) whose risk is over-regulation rather than under-regulation.

## Method

A normative policy essay covering five "perennial" areas: (1) regulation and public safety, (2) macroeconomics and tax policy, (3) accelerating AI's positive impact, (4) the balance of power between state and society, and (5) geopolitics. Accompanied by a concrete legislative proposal on frontier-model testing and a job-displacement policy framework.

## Results

Key proposals:
- **Regulation:** mandatory third-party testing of models above a compute threshold in four risk areas — cybersecurity, biological weapons, loss of control, and dangerous automated R&D; government power to block/reverse deployment (narrowly scoped, with anti-favoritism safeguards); a government agency *or* "regulatory markets" of authorized private evaluators; weight-security and incident-reporting requirements. Analogy: FAA / drugs / cars now; possibly nuclear-materials-grade controls later.
- **Macro/tax:** powerful AI may "scramble" the growth-vs-equality tradeoff, risking a "hypergrowth, hyper-inequality" setting; enduring job displacement is undesirable and should be minimized. Interventions: better measurement/economic statistics, pro-employment incentives (wage insurance, retention credits, training grants), and, if needed, long-term income support (e.g., UBI funded via taxes on AI firms or capital gains). Datacenter/energy backlash seen as a symbol of broader anxiety; AI firms should absorb rate increases.
- **Accelerating benefits:** for downstream fields (esp. biomedicine), the bigger risk is regulatory systems too slow for AI's deluge of advances; agencies should pre-define standards to accept AI simulation/analysis (PD/PK modeling, toxicology prediction, synthetic control arms, surrogate endpoints) to avoid jamming the 7–8 year drug pipeline.

## Limitations

- A normative essay from a frontier-lab CEO with obvious commercial interests; no empirical evaluation.
- Hinges on scaling laws continuing and on "Powerful AI" timelines that are contested.
- US-centric; "Mythos Preview" specifics are asserted, not independently documented here.

## Open questions

- How to design binding regulation that targets real risks without entrenching incumbents or political favoritism?
- Can pro-employment incentives meaningfully slow displacement, or is long-term income support inevitable?
- How to reconcile accelerating downstream AI applications with the precaution applied to frontier models?

## My take

A notable shift in stated position — from "transparency is all that's possible" to "binding FAA-style regulation now." The pacing-problem framing is the durable contribution and recurs across the policy literature (Frey, Europe 2031, Industrial Policy for the Intelligence Age). Stands in tension with the US light-touch framework. The macro section is unusually candid about hyper-inequality risk for a lab CEO.
