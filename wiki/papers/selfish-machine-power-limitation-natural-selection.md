## Problem

Dan Hendrycks and others have argued that AI systems will undergo evolution by natural selection, acquiring selfish drives (self-preservation, resource accumulation, dominance) comparable to evolved organisms. Boudry and Friederich ask: Is this argument compelling? Does the analogy between AI training and biological natural selection hold?

## Key idea

**AI training as "domestication," not "feral" evolution**: Using Godfrey-Smith's Darwinian spaces framework, the paper argues that directed AI training is not paradigmatically Darwinian — it is "top-down, directed, and driven by intelligent agency." Domesticated selection (which AI training resembles) does not produce selfish, dangerous traits. However, if superintelligent AI were to "go feral" — reproducing and competing without human oversight — the evolutionary danger argument would be valid.

## Method

- Philosophy of biology + AI ethics
- Applies Godfrey-Smith's Darwinian spaces: evolutionary processes vary continuously along multiple dimensions (population structure, fitness variation, heritability, etc.)
- The more a process is directed by foresighted agents, the less paradigmatically Darwinian it becomes
- Uses the concept of "domestication" as an analogy: humans selectively breed dogs for docility, not aggression — directed selection does not produce "selfish" traits

## Results

- Hendrycks' evolutionary argument is **not compelling as currently formulated**: directed AI training is closer to domestication than to natural selection
- Domesticated selection can produce docility, obedience, non-aggression — even in competitive market environments
- **Conditional validity**: if superintelligent AIs "go feral" (compete autonomously without human supervision), the evolutionary danger argument holds
- The paper identifies "feral superintelligence" as the specific scenario worth worrying about

## Limitations

- Philosophical analysis; does not engage with specific ML training dynamics
- The "domestication" analogy breaks down if AI systems gain the capacity for self-replication or autonomous goal-modification
- Market competition may behave differently from human intentional selection

## Open questions

- What conditions would cause trained AI to "go feral" in the relevant sense?
- Can legal and governance frameworks prevent feral AI scenarios?
- Does the domestication analogy account for inner alignment failure (AI systems that appear aligned but harbor misaligned goals)?

## My take

A useful corrective to overly literal applications of evolutionary theory to AI. The domestication analogy is insightful, and the Darwinian spaces framework provides a principled way to analyze the argument. The conditional conclusion ("if feral, dangerous") is the right nuanced position. Importance 3 — published in Philosophical Studies; good for philosophy of AI reading lists.
