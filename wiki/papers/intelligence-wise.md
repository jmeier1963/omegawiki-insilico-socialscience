## Problem & Context

A widely held diagnosis in AI discourse holds that stupidity is humanity's main problem and artificial general intelligence is the solution — a position Altinay attributes to Yann LeCun and reads as continuous with a Silicon Valley ethos that prides itself on channeling abundant IQ at problems. Bill Gates supplies the adjacent claim that AI-accelerated innovation will mitigate contemporary problems including climate change.

The essay opens with two grounds for suspicion. First, track record: these are the same actors who advised moving fast and breaking things, and who sold social networks as a seamless way to be closer to friends and family — platforms that turned out to show users roughly 7% of content from their chosen connections while making their operators enormously rich. "Fool me once, shame on you; fool me twice, shame on me." Second, the reassurance that AI "will only diffuse at the speed of trust" is offered by the same parties.

Altinay's own objection sits elsewhere, and it is biographical rather than argumentative. Working at a large international foundation, he heard a senior colleague described as being "too smart for him." The wise friend's cumulative actions transformed a troubled cross-border relationship; the foundation's and that colleague's work amounted to little. The question that follows is whether intelligence is actually intelligent, or wise.

## Key idea

The framing question — "if one does not know to which port one should sail, no wind is favorable" (attributed to Seneca) — locates the problem in direction rather than capacity. Adding wind does not help a ship without a port.

The essay's substantive proposal is that **love, understood as the quality of attention paid, is wiser than intelligence** — and that this is a claim about knowing, not sentiment. Iris Murdoch's definition anchors it: love as the quality of the attention we pay. Listening well is on this account an act of love; several contemplative traditions (Benedictine, Taoist, Sufi) share the phrase "listen with your heart's ear," a formulation that presupposes *intending* to hear the other rather than waiting for one's turn to speak.

The underlying question is put as an existential choice rather than an empirical one: are we atomistic units with negligible ties to natural and social habitats that exist primarily for use and extraction, with no north star beyond more consumption and more power — or are we constitutively interdependent, in which case care, goodwill and love are not trivial but central to wellbeing and flourishing?

The conclusion: humanity's main shortcoming may not be stupidity, and greater instrumental intelligence may not be the solution. **A civilization with meagre capacities to see and cherish its members may end up less competent and wise by becoming more narrowly intelligent and powerful.**

## Method

An essay. Its evidence is testimony, cited authority, and cases.

Authorities assembled across traditions: Sezai Karakoç (Turkish poet — love is wiser than intelligence); John O'Donohue (love as the only light allowing us to really see the other); the Vatican's latest encyclical (grasping a reality with the heart yields fuller knowledge; devaluing the heart means knowing too narrowly and missing poetry); James Baldwin (love as friendship is the work of realizing each other's light); Simon Critchley (if overthinking means underloving, do we still want to overthink?); Iris Murdoch; Maria Popova; and Iain McGilchrist.

McGilchrist carries the analytical weight: two modes of knowing, one narrow and instrumental, one holistic and hermeneutic — one categorizing in a rush and manipulating, the other attending and relating. Movement through the world is their interplay; the contemporary problem is that the former has been overdeveloped at the expense of the latter. Love has been called a muscle; the question is whether that muscle has atrophied, and if so what the intelligence emanating on steroids from the current constellation would end up serving.

Counter-evidence is drawn from practitioners: Ken Powley, who after fifty years organizing whitewater rafting expeditions founded Team Democracy following January 6, using his industry networks to put people from divergent political tribes literally in the same boat under testing conditions — on the conviction that this produces a reality check and a somatic realization of the stakes. Alongside: the Big Lunch (UK, 15 years), Weavers (US, 10+ years), the One Radical Centre Manifesto and St. Ethelburga's Centre framing of the radical centre as a practice and a muscle rather than a political programme or midpoint, Harsh Mander's radical love, and Kumi Naidoo's claim that failing to find a way to love Trump voters would be *our* insufficiency, not theirs.

## Experiment & Results

No empirical claims. The essay's operative observation is that these modest interventions produce deeper and stronger relationships than anyone expects — and that this should, theoretically, interest LeCun and his peers, though the author cannot bring himself to expect it.

The sharpest analytical move is the closing worry about how such dispositions would be represented: whether enhanced intelligence has, or could have, any way to account for them — or would simply treat them as maladaptive choices or errors, in the way many economists have treated generous choices in ultimatum game experiments. This is the essay's one falsifiable-adjacent claim, and it names a real failure mode: a system optimizing an instrumental objective will classify care-driven behavior as noise unless the objective encodes it.

Altinay declines to claim the corrective is easy, self-evident, or assured of success. He notes he was himself a product of a Bildung that treated critical thinking as the obvious standard, and is now glad his son attends a school whose motto is "with love we flourish" — attributing his own shift to specific people acting as "breadcrumbs" that let him discover a different paradigm somatically rather than through argument. He calls this friendship as a theory of change: he could not bring himself to unknow what he learned through those people. The closing position is that we are both the problem and the elusive solution, walking a path with no guarantees and ample evidence for probable failure.

## Limitations

- Not research. Three pages of argument by assembled authority, with no empirical content, no mechanism, and no falsifiable claim.
- **Authority stacking.** Poets, philosophers, a papal encyclical and a novelist agreeing does not constitute evidence; the cited figures are selected for agreement.
- The LeCun position is characterized rather than quoted, and "stupidity is the problem, AGI is the solution" is a compressed rendering that its holders would likely dispute.
- **The central term is undefined.** "Love as the quality of attention" is evocative but not operationalized, so the claim that it is "wiser" than intelligence cannot be assessed, let alone tested.
- The counter-examples are self-selected success stories from civic organizations, reported without outcome measurement.
- The author's own epistemic route — somatic discovery through friendship, explicitly not argument — is presented as a strength but forecloses persuading anyone not already positioned to be persuaded.
- Silent on the possibility that AI systems might be *better* at sustained attention than distracted humans, which is the most obvious objection to the thesis.

## Open questions

- Can the quality of attention be specified well enough to be either designed for or measured? Without this the argument stays a mood.
- The ultimatum-game analogy is the testable core: do current systems in fact classify care-driven and generous behavior as error? Alignment work on prosocial and pluralistic objectives bears directly on this and the essay does not engage it.
- Is the instrumental/hermeneutic split (McGilchrist) a real constraint on machine cognition, or an artifact of a contested neuroscientific framing?
- If wisdom is constituted by relationship and interdependence rather than by information processing, is it in principle unavailable to a system that has neither — and what would count as evidence either way?
- What would an AI system optimized for the second mode of knowing even look like?

## My take

The essay is worth having in the wiki for one argument, not for its aggregate case. That argument is the ultimatum-game point: a system optimizing an instrumental objective will read generosity, care and costly attention as error, exactly as a generation of economists read them as anomalies. That is a concrete, recognizable failure mode of objective specification, it connects directly to [[pluralistic-alignment]] and [[positive-alignment]], and it is stated more cleanly here than in most technical treatments.

The rest is a mood rather than a thesis. Assembling Karakoç, O'Donohue, Baldwin, Murdoch, McGilchrist and an encyclical in agreement is not evidence, and the central move — that love is "wiser" than intelligence — cannot be evaluated because neither term is pinned down. The essay is also careful to note that its own author was persuaded somatically through friendship rather than through argument, which is honest but concedes that the text is unlikely to move anyone not already inclined.

Where it lands against the wiki: it is the humanistic counterpart to [[marginal-returns-to-intelligence]], reaching a similar conclusion — that adding intelligence to a system whose binding constraint is elsewhere buys little — from a normative rather than an analytical direction. Read next to [[ten-advances-mathematics-theoretical-computer-science]], the contrast is instructive: mathematics is precisely the domain where instrumental intelligence *is* the binding constraint and scaling it appears to deliver, which is the strongest available case that Altinay's argument is domain-specific rather than general.
