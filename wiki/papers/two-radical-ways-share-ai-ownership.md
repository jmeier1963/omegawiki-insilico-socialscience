## Problem & Context

A 2021 internal Anthropic memo ("An Economic Model for Compensating Data Producers"), surfaced in *Bartz v. Anthropic*, diagnosed that distributed human labor trains AI models owned by centralized actors who concentrate profits while making that labor obsolete, and predicted growing unpopularity, creator walling-off, and government crackdown — all visible in 2026. The memo's fix was to pay data producers a share of model profits as equity ("guaranteed basic capital for the machine age"), reaching the equilibrium up front rather than after "years of hatred, threats, and mutual acrimony" (Amodei). Five years on, no company has built it, because the market cannot produce it unilaterally.

## Key idea

AI value should be shared through *ownership stakes*, not transfers (UBI/dividends), because a stake makes people durable part-owners with a reason to support and shape AI rather than resent it. Two complementary settlements follow: **data equity** for those whose work trains the models, and **public equity** for society as a whole.

## Method

Position essay (grew out of a RadicalxChange salon) proposing two institutional designs:
- **Data equity.** The training-data market is a monopsony: scattered individual creators cannot price their work against a few frontier labs. Fix the supply side with a narrow antitrust *safe harbor* letting qualifying rights-holder groups form joint negotiating bodies, bargain, and go to binding arbitration (precedent: Capper-Volstead farm cooperatives; Journalism Competition and Preservation Act). Pricing uses the Weyl/Castro-Fernandez insight — data-mixture weights divide the pie, scaling laws size it — and pays creators in equity to keep them invested.
- **Public equity.** Society underwrites AI's risks without sharing upside; the radical-markets view treats property as monopoly (unjust and inefficient). Reject direct share hand-outs (1990s Russian voucher privatization → oligarchy). Instead hold the stake in a collective, hard-to-alienate vehicle, and consider **partial common ownership (PCO)**: a lab self-assesses its model's price, pays a recurring fee on it to a public fund, and must accept that at that price a single public taker can seize it (precedent: Taiwan land 1954–1977). Separate ownership from operation — a public trust holds ultimate control and licenses deployment to safety-approved operators on takeable terms.

## Experiment & Results

No empirics. Policy landscape: Sen. Sanders' American AI Sovereign Wealth Fund Act (stock tax → dividend); OpenAI's own spring-2026 public-wealth-fund paper; the Trump administration's National AI Legislative Framework urging antitrust room for rights holders to band together — "the very rule the memo said was missing" now recommended by the White House. Three recurring salon tensions: collective vehicles grow less trustworthy as they scale (capture risk); a nationally-bounded dividend mismatches globally-produced AI; a public paid to own AI might stop objecting to its use.

## Limitations

- Both designs are institutional sketches; none has been built or piloted.
- PCO's "highest bidder = best use" logic breaks for frontier AI, where maximal monetization may be exactly what the public wants to restrain (the authors concede this is the strongest objection).
- Sovereign wealth funds "invite capture"; collective vehicles' trustworthiness degrades with scale.
- Relies on legal change (the safe harbor) and unresolved fair-use litigation as backdrop.

## Open questions

- What governance vehicle holds public equity without succumbing to capture as it scales?
- How to reconcile a nationally-bounded AI dividend with globally-sourced training data?
- Does public ownership co-opt the public into tolerating harmful AI uses?

## My take

The sharpest move is reframing the fight from *compensation* to *ownership*: a stake is politically stickier than a transfer and aligns incentives to improve rather than wall off. It takes the data-pricing mechanism of the Weyl/Castro-Fernandez paper as an input and pushes past it into equity and public-trust design, where the radical-markets PCO idea is the genuinely novel (and most contestable) contribution. The honest admission that PCO's price-discovery logic misfires for frontier AI — because we may want to restrain, not maximize, monetization — is what separates this from ownership-sharing hand-waving.
