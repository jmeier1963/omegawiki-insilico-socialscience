## Problem

There is widespread anxiety that once AI coding capability crosses a threshold it will cause mass layoffs of software engineers. Narayanan and Kapoor ask how to move past vague predictions and bring data to bear, by studying the profession where AI capability is furthest along and adoption fastest: software engineering.

## Key idea

Treat coding agents as **normal technology**: there is enough evidence to reject the "capability-threshold → mass layoffs" narrative even in a near-unregulated sector, so most other professions are even more cushioned. The mechanism is the **"decide-execute-deliver sandwich"**: AI compresses the *execute* (middle) layer of knowledge work, but the *decide* and *deliver* layers resist automation in ways capability improvements alone will not overcome.

## Method

Synthesis of published economics and software-engineering literature, the authors' own agent evaluations and observations, and software engineers' reflections. Includes case analysis of high-profile "AI-driven" layoffs.

## Results

- **"AI washing" of layoffs:** in every examined story of AI-driven software layoffs, the AI narrative collapsed on inspection — Block (financial pressure after 3× pandemic hiring, "limited productivity gains"), Snap (activist-investor cost-cutting; cuts not concentrated in AI-exposed roles), Intuit (CEO: "none of it had to do with AI"; coordination-heavy roles, management layers). CEOs are prone to overrating AI because they prototype but don't see the 90% of work to finish a product (Levie).
- The decide and deliver layers (problem selection, requirements, judgment, integration, accountability) bound how much execute-layer compression translates into reduced labor demand.
- Cautious optimism on overall software-engineering demand; first in a series (a sequel will address why individual careers may still be rocky even with healthy aggregate demand).

## Limitations

- An essay, not peer-reviewed; relies on case selection and current observations.
- Forward-looking ("and won't") claim is a structural argument, not a guarantee.
- Mostly US/tech-sector framing.

## Open questions

- Do the decide/deliver layers stay automation-resistant as agents take on more planning?
- How much of aggregate demand stability hides painful within-career churn?

## My take

The "decide-execute-deliver sandwich" is the same insight as Anthropic's "humans decide what, agents decide how" division of labor ([[human-ai-division-labor-agentic-work]]), reached from the labor-economics side. The "AI washing" documentation is the most useful empirical contribution. Together with the legal-services essay, it operationalizes the [[ai-normal-technology]] thesis.
