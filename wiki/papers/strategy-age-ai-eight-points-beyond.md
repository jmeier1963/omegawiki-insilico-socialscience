## Problem & Context

Choudary's target is what he calls a category error in current AI strategy discourse: viewing AI through a "mechanisation/automation lens" traps firms into chasing operational efficiency gains (faster research, analysis, deck creation) while missing that AI changes "the very game" being competed in — which activities remain valuable, who can perform them, and where industry boundaries sit. His diagnostic example: traditional consulting firms investing in AI to make consultants faster are optimizing "the shrinking middle" while clients increasingly perform that work themselves and model providers absorb the rest.

## Key idea

The essay's spine is a single reversal: **for several decades, strategy rewarded modularization** — firms outsourced activities, standardized interfaces, and concentrated on one layer, on the implicit assumption that activities could stay separated as long as interfaces between them were stable. AI breaks that assumption whenever performance depends on *learning across* those boundaries — if improving a customer outcome requires simultaneously adjusting product design, production, distribution and demand-sensing together, the valuable asset is not any individual layer but "the proprietary feedback system that learns how the layers should change together. The coupling becomes an asset." Vertical integration is therefore returning, but Choudary is explicit that this is not about owning factories: "The prize is no longer necessarily ownership of every asset. It is ownership of the feedback loops that connect them."

## Method

An essay structured as eight numbered propositions, each with one illustrative company case: (1) the strategic problem is whether the competitive "game" still exists, not how to win it (case: AI-native consulting disruption); (2) advantage tracks scarcity, not capability (case: generative-AI-flooded creative agencies); (3) AI-native advantage comes from redefining the unit of value, not bolting AI onto the old product (case: Figma's object-centric design system vs. Adobe's file-centric model); (4) horizontal strategies may shift from standards-adoption to AI-enabled translation across incompatible systems (case: a hypothetical AI-native entrant spanning Autodesk/Bentley/contractor data in construction software); (5) vertical integration returns via cross-boundary learning, the essay's central thesis (case: Shein's social-signal-to-replenishment feedback loop); (6) discarded intermediate work — drafts, failed experiments, rejected options — becomes reusable capital once machine-readable (case: a hypothetical publisher converting research notes into a knowledge graph); (7) "the machine that builds the machine" (the measurement/improvement system) becomes more valuable than any single output (case: Tesla's usage-to-multiple-product-lines feedback architecture); (8) in structural uncertainty, capabilities should be treated as options revealing new strategies rather than fixed execution assets (case: Kodak's digital-photography investment being evaluated through its existing film-business economics rather than as an option on a different business).

## Experiment & Results

Not applicable. No data, no systematic case selection, no counterfactual — each of the eight cases is a brief illustrative anecdote (one to two paragraphs) chosen to fit its proposition, with no comparison case where the same technology *failed* to produce the predicted strategic shift.

## Limitations

- **All illustration, no test.** Eight propositions, eight hand-picked supporting companies, zero disconfirming or ambiguous cases — a reader can't tell from the essay whether Figma-style unit-redefinition or Shein-style feedback-loop integration are common outcomes of AI adoption or rare, survivorship-selected successes.
- **The Kodak case is doing double duty as both cautionary tale and confirmation** of proposition 8, which is a common move in strategy-consulting writing but weakens its evidentiary value — Kodak's failure is consistent with many strategic-framing stories, not distinctively confirming of "treat capabilities as options."
- **No engagement with counter-evidence on the modularity thesis** — plenty of AI deployment (e.g., API-based point solutions, narrow vertical SaaS-plus-AI wrappers) is *increasing* modularity rather than reversing it, and the essay doesn't address when its "coupling becomes an asset" logic does or doesn't apply.
- **Commercial context**: this is explicitly marketing copy for Choudary's forthcoming book (*Unfair Advantage*) and a paid subscription upgrade, embedded directly in the essay — doesn't invalidate the ideas but is relevant provenance for weighing how much independent scrutiny the propositions received before publication.
- Newsletter format: 10 PDF pages including substantial promotional material interleaved with the substantive content.

## Open questions

- Under what conditions does AI *increase* rather than reverse modularity — is proposition 5 (vertical reintegration via feedback loops) the general case or a special case that applies only when cross-boundary learning has high marginal value?
- Is "ownership of feedback loops" empirically distinguishable from ordinary data-network-effects moats already well documented in platform-strategy literature, or is it the same phenomenon with new packaging for the AI era?
- The by-product-as-capital thesis (proposition 6) assumes discarded work becomes valuable once machine-readable — does this hold once compute/storage/retrieval costs for organizing that "exhaust" are accounted for?
- Proposition 4's construction-software example is explicitly hypothetical — has anything like this translation-layer strategy actually been attempted or succeeded anywhere by the time of writing?

## My take

The essay's most durable idea is the modularity-reversal mechanism itself — coupling becomes an asset when cross-boundary learning drives the outcome — because it's a specific, checkable causal claim (not just "AI changes everything") and it names a real strategic tension between decades of outsourcing/standardization orthodoxy and what integrated learning systems actually require. The Shein and Tesla cases are the strongest illustrations precisely because both are companies whose advantage was already widely attributed to cross-functional feedback *before* this essay, which is closer to independent corroboration than the Figma or hypothetical-construction-software cases, which read as narrative reverse-engineering to fit the thesis.

Where the essay is weakest is exactly where consulting-style writing usually is: eight numbered "propositions beyond the obvious," each paired with a single confirming anecdote, is a rhetorical structure optimized for readability and book marketing, not for testing whether the propositions are true, false, or true only under specific conditions. Read next to [[us-vs-china-how-win-wrong]] — the same author's earlier essay in this wiki — this piece is the more conceptually ambitious of the two (a general theory of strategy under AI vs. a specific geopolitical framing claim), but it inherits the same limitation: an analytically useful lens presented with the confidence of an established finding rather than a hypothesis.
