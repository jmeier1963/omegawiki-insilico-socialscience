## Problem

The standard reassurance against AI job loss — "automation couldn't kill the bank teller, so AI won't kill the accountant" — asks the wrong question. The teller was eventually replaced not by a better machine *in* the bank but by the *customer* outside it (mobile deposit, online transfers). Frey argues that asking "can a machine do this job?" systematically looks in the wrong place.

## Key idea

The right question is **"can the customer do without this job?"** AI's main labour effect is **self-service displacement**: it gives people the means to do work themselves, collapsing demand for the intermediary service — distinct from automating tasks *inside* a service (which, via the **Jevons paradox**, can *grow* employment by making the service cheaper). The Jevons paradox holds only when the technology makes the *existing service model* more efficient; when it lets people self-serve, demand for the service collapses.

## Method

Economic-history argument (op-ed), drawing on Gershuny's 1978 "self-service economy" thesis and a chain of examples; invokes Christian Catalini on verification as the binding constraint.

## Results

- **Self-service pattern repeats:** washing machine (vs. laundress), self-checkout, online travel/brokerages — and now AI extends it to manual trades (chatbot boiler diagnosis → fewer heating call-outs) and professions (patients decoding their own test results).
- **Verification becomes the binding constraint:** when AI pushes execution cost toward zero (Catalini), what's left is human verification/responsibility — and self-service *offloads that burden onto the customer*.
- **A GDP/labour-statistics blind spot:** when work shifts to the consumer it "vanishes from the economy statisticians measure." Replacing a billing department with a chatbot records a productivity gain; the hours patients spend decoding their own results appear nowhere. AI-enabled self-service quietly *reverses* capitalism's centuries-long move of activity from household into market.

## Limitations

- Short op-ed; illustrative examples rather than systematic evidence.
- Doesn't quantify how large the self-service channel is relative to in-service automation.
- The measurement-blindspot point is asserted, not estimated.

## Open questions

- How big is self-service displacement relative to Jevons-paradox job growth?
- How should national accounts capture consumer-absorbed work?
