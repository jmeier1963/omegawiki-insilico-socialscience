## Problem & Context

Organizations have long been designed around the assumption that knowledge is scarce, so authority flows to those who hold the most of it. As AI makes knowledge instantly available, that logic breaks. A June 2026 PA Consulting / Business Reporter breakfast briefing of senior financial-services executives (The Goring Hotel, London) discussed what happens to organizational design when knowledge stops being scarce.

## Key idea

When knowledge is ubiquitous, the scarce, value-differentiating human capacities become judgment, accountability, trust, and adaptability — forcing a rethink of organizational structure, management layers, and how firms judge and reward people.

## Method

Qualitative roundtable summary (Chatham-House-style, anonymized quotes). Themes: AI as an "auditor" exposing where real judgment/expertise reside; SMF accountability when leaders are far from the technology ("feed a system bad data and the fault is yours"); customer-built agents bypassing firms' curated journeys; data observability (one bank's 64,000 dashboards vs. F1-team decision discipline); biased legacy data and synthetic-data fixes; cost (AI contact centre at ~1/5 human cost); trust as the pace-setter for adoption; recruiting for attitude/aptitude over accumulated knowledge; fewer managers over time.

## Experiment & Results

No data. Illustrative claims: an AI contact centre runs at ~20% of human cost and older customers preferred the bot (unlimited patience); second/third-line functions could be automated already but the trust gap holds them back; a proposed discount to nudge customers to AI channels; expectation of fewer managers with larger spans of control.

## Limitations

- Sponsored, anonymized roundtable; no data or methodology, sector-specific (UK financial services).
- Views are practitioner impressions, not evidence.
- Marketing-adjacent (PA Consulting sponsorship).

## Open questions

- What organizational structure replaces knowledge-based hierarchy when judgment/accountability are the scarce inputs?
- How is regulated-firm accountability (SMF) assigned when technology talks to technology?
- If osmosis-based skill transfer is too slow, how do firms develop judgment in juniors who lean on AI?

## My take

A ground-level read of the same shift the labor and firm-productivity literature formalizes: when knowledge is cheap, the moat moves to judgment, accountability, and trust — the "context advantage" reframed for org design. The sharpest practical tension is accountability (SMF liability when leaders are distant from the automated work), and the sharpest data point is trust, not capability, gating customer-facing automation. Thin as a source, but a useful qualitative complement to the harder evidence on AI and firm structure.
