## Problem & Context

Generative AI became an everyday instrument in higher education faster than institutions could form a position on it. The Wissenschaftsrat's premise is that this is not primarily a technical integration problem: AI use affects cognitive, motivational and emotional processes, can narrow individual scope for action, and — because academic work is to a high degree bound to the written formulation of findings and arguments — LLMs reach the core of academic thinking directly. The report calls the result a *tiefgreifende epistemische Verschiebung*, a far-reaching epistemic shift.

Published July 2026, Drs. 3319-26, DOI 10.57674/1evx-t906, CC BY-SA 4.0. The document itself discloses that AI models were used in its preparation.

## Key idea

**Intellektuelle Souveränität** (intellectual sovereignty) as the organizing idea: the capacity for free and self-determined action in acquiring and producing knowledge, and the capacity to think autonomously and justify one's own decisions argumentatively. Applied to AI, it implies a deliberate reframing — away from the question of *optimal AI use* and toward the question of *preserving and fostering independent human capacity for thought and judgement* in an algorithmically saturated world.

Sovereignty here is explicitly a *Haltung*, an attitude — including a commitment to scientific standards of correctness and to democratic values, and resting on resistance to, or at minimum interrogation of, convenient simplification.

## Method

Policy recommendation document. Part B develops the guiding idea by working through six tensions in the field: efficiency vs. meta-work; learning support vs. competence loss; personalization vs. loss of the social; participation vs. concentration of power and dependency; ubiquitous "knowledge" vs. epistemic displacement; misinformation vs. its exposure. Part C converts these into eight areas of analysis and recommendation.

## Experiment & Results

No empirical study; the output is the recommendation set. The eight areas:

1. Build AI competences
2. Strengthen (disciplinary) science as the central dimension of higher education
3. **Create AI-free spaces**
4. Redesign assessment formats
5. Scientifically evaluate and monitor the learning effectiveness of AI applications
6. — (further area)
7. Revive universities as social places of learning
8. Develop sovereign and capable AI infrastructure for higher education

The third is the most-cited: universities should create and protect AI-free spaces "in denen tiefes, anstrengendes, manchmal frustrierendes, aber letztlich transformatives Denken stattfinden kann" — in which deep, strenuous, sometimes frustrating but ultimately transformative thinking can take place. These are explicitly not only physical: AI-free introductory modules teaching basic scholarly technique before tools are introduced, in-person examinations without digital aids, focused writing workshops. The report also argues that deliberately contrasting AI-supported with AI-free working phases builds a reflective understanding of the technology, and that AI-free spaces create transparent conditions under which a student's own contribution is unambiguously attributable.

## Limitations

- Recommendations, not binding rules; implementation rests with autonomous universities and the Länder.
- The report is normatively rich and operationally thin — how AI-free spaces are enforced, and what happens when they are not, is left open.
- The empirical base for the competence-loss claims is the same thin literature the field generally relies on.
- Focused on teaching and learning; research practice and peer review are largely out of scope.

## Open questions

- Does contrasting AI-free and AI-supported phases actually produce the reflective technology understanding the report expects?
- How is intellectual sovereignty assessed, given that the report also asks for assessment reform?
- Can sovereign infrastructure be financed at the scale the report's Part 8 implies?

## My take

The important move is the reframing in a single sentence: from *how do we use AI optimally* to *what must be preserved of independent judgement*. Almost every institutional AI policy so far has answered the first question, which quietly assumes that the capacity to judge the tool's output is a constant. This report treats that capacity as the dependent variable — which is the correct move, and the one that makes the recommendations expensive.

The "AI-free spaces" recommendation will draw the attention, and it is defensible on the available learning evidence. The weaker part is that a body recommending resistance to convenient simplification discloses that it used AI models in drafting, without saying where or how. That is not a contradiction, but it is precisely the kind of case the report's own transparency arguments call for spelling out.
