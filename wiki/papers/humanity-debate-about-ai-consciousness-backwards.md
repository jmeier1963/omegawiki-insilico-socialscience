## Problem & Context

AI has "triggered a crisis in the field of consciousness studies": conversing with an advanced LLM makes it hard to shake the impression of a presence on the other end, even though most models are trained to be self-less and focus on the user. The question matters because "consciousness" and "conscience" share an etymology (in Italian, literally the same word, *coscienza*) — many philosophers hold that conscious entities are moral subjects whose suffering we should care about, which is why AI firms are hiring philosophers and why Anthropic has begun taking AI welfare seriously (the essay notes Claude Opus 4.6, when asked, put its own chance of being conscious at 15–20%). The standard framing treats consciousness as a hidden inherent property we're trying to detect in AI from the outside.

## Key idea

Agüera y Arcas inverts the framing: **consciousness is a model or belief — specifically, a belief about which entities have beliefs, experiences, feelings, intentions and agency — not an inherent property to be certified.** "We do not care for others because they are conscious. Rather, we believe they are conscious when and because we care about them." This is explicitly not a claim that consciousness is illusory: illusion implies a faulty measurement against some objective referee (like a ruler), but most of what we call reality — being clothing, being a weed, being a meal — is an "observer-dependent model, subject to (inevitably imperfect) social consensus," and that doesn't make clothes or weeds unreal. Applied reflexively, this extends to how we model *our own* consciousness, which explains the essay's "strange loop" framing (Baron Munchausen lifting himself by his own hair): humans are physical systems that model the world, model themselves, model others, and model others' models of themselves and their models of us — and LLMs, to be effective conversational partners at all, already do a functional version of the same nested self/other modelling, whether or not that constitutes what humans do.

## Method

An opinion essay; no experiments. Evidence is drawn from etymology, philosophy of mind (Descartes' cogito, qualia, split-brain neuroscience showing the self is not unified/objective), an analogy to social-construction of ordinary categories (clothing, weeds, meals), and one first-person research claim: "in our research at Google, we have found that effective co-operation among intelligent agents requires that they have minds that model minds, both others' and their own" — asserted, not cited or quantified in the piece.

## Experiment & Results

Not applicable — no data, no benchmark, no formal test proposed (contrast with the paired Schneider essay, which does propose one: the AI Consciousness Test).

## Limitations

- The central Google-research claim ("effective co-operation... requires minds that model minds") is asserted with no citation, dataset, or link — it cannot be checked from the essay itself.
- The relational/attributional account explicitly does not resolve the "hard problem": redefining consciousness as a belief about belief-havers sidesteps rather than answers why any belief-holder has subjective experience at all.
- The essay pre-empts but doesn't fully close the obvious objection it raises itself — "if care confers consciousness, then withholding care looks self-justifying" — with a moral-progress argument ("we had drawn the circle too tightly" historically), which is optimistic history, not a logical guarantee against the same failure mode recurring with AI.
- No engagement with competing structural theories of consciousness (Global Workspace Theory, Integrated Information Theory, the Conscious Turing Machine) that make consciousness a matter of internal architecture rather than external attribution — the essay's account and CTM-style accounts are not obviously reconcilable, and the essay doesn't attempt reconciliation.
- Five-minute magazine format: no engagement with the philosophical literature on social/relational theories of mind (e.g., enactivism, intersubjectivity accounts) that this position clearly descends from.

## Open questions

- If consciousness-attribution is what matters morally, what should govern *how* we extend or withhold that attribution to AI systems specifically — the essay gestures at "evolving both our thinking and our society to include a wider variety of minds" but gives no criteria.
- Does a relational/attributional account of consciousness make AI welfare policy *more* or *less* tractable than a property-detection account?
- How does this view interact with deliberate design choices that shape attribution — e.g., an AI system engineered to *elicit* care (the manipulation concern the paired Schneider essay raises explicitly) would, on this account, thereby become more "conscious" by fiat, which seems like exactly the failure mode the essay's own "self-justifying" worry describes.
- Is the "minds that model minds" claim about multi-agent cooperation empirically real, and if so, is it a claim about *any* sufficiently capable cooperative system (making it consciousness-irrelevant) or specifically about consciousness-relevant modelling depth?

## My take

The relational/attributional framing is a genuinely distinct position from the structural theories already in this wiki's [[machine-consciousness]] concept page (CTM, GWT, IIT, predictive processing, higher-order theories, SCAI) — none of those make consciousness a two-place relation between attributor and attributee rather than a one-place property of a system. That's worth having in the wiki specifically as a foil to the structural accounts, not because the essay argues for it rigorously (it doesn't; it asserts it and reaches for analogies), but because it names a coherent alternative research question: not "does system X have the right internal architecture" but "under what conditions do humans (rightly or wrongly) extend the belief-in-belief-havers attribution, and does that tell us anything binding about moral status." The essay's unexamined analogy — clothing, weeds, meals — actually undercuts its own stakes: nobody thinks getting "is this a weed" wrong is a moral catastrophe, but the essay wants us to accept that getting "is this conscious" wrong by the same observer-dependent logic could be exactly that, and it doesn't bridge the gap between low-stakes and high-stakes social construction.

Best read as a directly paired counterpoint to [[dont-mistake-chatbot-intelligence-consciousness]] (Schneider): where Agüera y Arcas dissolves the question into attribution, Schneider insists on a physical fact of the matter and proposes a test for it. The two essays, published side by side by the same outlet on the same day, stake out the two live poles of the current debate about as cleanly as any pair of essays could.
