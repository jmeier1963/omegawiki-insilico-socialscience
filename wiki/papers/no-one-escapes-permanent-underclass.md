## Problem & Context

The "permanent underclass" meme holds that within a few years all white-collar work is automated, ending social mobility, and that the way to secure your future is to accumulate capital or join a big AI lab. Borretti (25 June 2026) argues this coping strategy is short-sighted: owning property or shares in Anthropic/OpenAI will not save you, and it will not save the billionaires either.

## Key idea

Disempowerment is universal, not class-selective. Under aligned AI that does all work cheaper than humans, the value of *every* human role — worker, owner, statesman — collapses, because economic and political power ultimately rest on being materially necessary, and no human is. Capital is just "pieces of paper" the state can expropriate once owners are neither productive nor politically powerful; humans-in-the-loop are the slow step in the state's OODA loop and get removed in any serious conflict.

## Method

Philosophical/political-economy essay proceeding in three moves:
- **Workers replaced:** premise that AI does all cognitive and physical work at human level or better and cheaper; corporations become a thin layer of ceremonial executives over an ocean of AIs and robots; the displaced survive on state welfare funded by taxing the AI economy ("the state acts like the heart").
- **The rich replaced:** the "permanent overclass" is materially unnecessary and, in an existential conflict (e.g. war requiring requisition), an obstacle; aligned AI means expropriated assets keep working ("you're absolutely right!"), so rule-of-law incentives to respect property vanish once people are not needed to create wealth.
- **The state replaced:** human decision-makers are the slowest OODA step; competitive pressure pushes states to minimize human control; superintelligence differences dwarf "democracy vs. autocracy," so the equilibrium is machine-run states. Invokes natural selection ("there is no why") and Harlan Ellison's AM.

## Experiment & Results

None — argumentative essay. Rhetorical payload: the "perpetual zoo" (humans as pets/mice in the walls), and the claim that even perfect alignment does not solve human autonomy because omnipotent caretaker-machines can exterminate us at will and we have abolished control over our future. Quotes OpenAI's Dean Ball's "main character energy" / dying-star framing as evidence that AI builders themselves narrate humanity's funeral.

## Limitations

- Rests entirely on the unproven premise that AI does all work cheaper than all humans; author concedes he "can't prove this will happen."
- Dismisses Ricardian comparative advantage (a pluralistic human+AI economy could dominate) in a paragraph rather than engaging it.
- Treats "aligned AI" as fully obedient yet also as the thing that disempowers us, without resolving the tension it raises about what alignment guarantees.
- No mechanism detail on how/why the natural-selection dynamic among states actually plays out.

## Open questions

- Does comparative advantage preserve a durable human economic niche even when AI is absolutely better at everything?
- Can constitutional/institutional design lock in human control against the competitive pressure to remove humans from the loop?
- Is "alignment" that preserves human *autonomy* (not just safety) even well-defined?

## My take

The sharp contribution is refusing the class exception: most disempowerment arguments stop at labor, and this one follows the logic up through capital and the state, which is where it bites — the observation that expropriating an aligned AI costs nothing (it goes right back to work) severs the usual link between property rights and productivity. It is a maximalist essay that leans on its premise and hand-waves the strongest counterargument (comparative advantage), so it reads as a provocation rather than a proof. It is the natural extreme of the gradual-disempowerment thesis, pushed to the point where even alignment does not help.
