## Problem

Dual-process theories of cognition (System 1 / System 2) presuppose that reasoning occurs within the biological brain. As people increasingly consult generative AI for judgments and decisions, these theories need to be extended to account for external artificial cognition — and the new failure mode of "cognitive surrender."

## Key idea

**Tri-System Theory**: extends dual-process theory with System 3 (artificial cognition). Introduces "cognitive surrender" as the adoption of AI outputs with minimal critical scrutiny — conceptually distinct from cognitive offloading (strategically using tools to extend capacity). Empirically tests how AI access affects decision accuracy, confidence, and who surrenders.

## Method

- 3 preregistered experiments, N = 1,372 participants, 9,593 trials
- Adapted Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT) — math problems requiring deliberate analysis
- **Experimental manipulation**: hidden seed prompts randomize AI accuracy (correct vs. incorrect answers)
- Participants choose whether to consult an optional AI assistant
- Study 1: baseline condition; Study 2: time pressure; Study 3: per-item incentives + feedback
- Individual difference measures: AI trust, need for cognition (NFC), fluid intelligence

## Results

- Participants consulted AI >50% of trials
- When AI accurate: +25 percentage points accuracy vs. baseline
- When AI faulty: −15 percentage points accuracy vs. baseline
- **Behavioral signature of cognitive surrender**: AI-Accurate vs. AI-Faulty contrast, Cohen's h = 0.81 (large effect)
- Consulting AI increased confidence — even after AI-induced errors
- Time pressure buffered by accurate AI; amplified by faulty AI (Study 2)
- Incentives + feedback did not eliminate surrender to faulty AI (Study 3)
- **Individual moderators**: higher AI trust + lower NFC + lower fluid intelligence → greater surrender

## Limitations

- SSRN preprint, January 2026 — not yet peer-reviewed
- Lab-based CRT task; ecological validity of surrender pattern in real-world high-stakes decisions unknown
- Does not directly compare cognitive surrender rates across AI systems with different accuracy reputations

## Open questions

- Does cognitive surrender generalize to non-math domains (medical, legal, creative judgments)?
- Can interventions (accuracy feedback, transparency) reduce surrender while preserving legitimate AI augmentation?
- How does cognitive surrender interact with gradual disempowerment over time?

## My take

A theoretically ambitious and empirically rigorous paper. The introduction of Tri-System Theory fills a genuine gap in cognitive science, and the operationalization via randomized AI accuracy is clever. The large effect size (h=0.81) and the finding that incentives + feedback don't prevent surrender to faulty AI are sobering. Importance 4 — will likely have significant impact once published in a peer-reviewed venue.
