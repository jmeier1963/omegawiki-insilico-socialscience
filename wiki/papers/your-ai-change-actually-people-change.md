## Problem

Most AI initiatives fail to unlock value: a 2025 BCG study found only 5% of companies achieve substantial value from AI, while 60% report no material value at all. Leaders are puzzled because AI capability has advanced rapidly but organizational outcomes have not followed. Why?

## Key idea

AI-driven change is fundamentally human in nature: when it fails, it fails due to human factors (unclear goals, insufficient buy-in, poor take-up, poor storytelling), not technical limitations. BCG's observation across successful AI transformations: **70% of value is derived from people-related action, not technology-related action**. Seven behavioral science principles from half a century of research explain the difference.

## Method

Practitioner synthesis / consulting case study analysis from BCG's advisory work on AI transformation programs. Draws on BCG's "AI at Work" survey data (2025: only 36% of employees say they've been trained; 76% of executives believe employees are excited, but only 31% of employees actually are). Authors include behavioral scientists (Julia Dhar); based on forthcoming book "How Change Really Works."

## Results

**7 behavioral science principles for successful AI-driven change:**

1. **Get True Agreement, Not False Alignment**: Executives must truly agree on where AI matters (3–4 critical use cases vs. 6–7 in less-successful organizations); surface underlying intent and trade-offs.

2. **Increase Agency, Not Just Involvement**: Middle managers must have meaningful design authority over their own AI-enabled workflows (IKEA effect — ownership → motivation). Requires dedicated time and peer learning.

3. **Expect Take Up to Be Earned, Not Automatic**: Three key barriers — skills gap (broader than technical AI skills; 64% undertrained), permission gap (cultural acceptance, cybersecurity concerns), and identity threat (fear that AI replaces valued expertise). Leaders must address each explicitly.

4. **Understand Emotions Through Feedback, Not Instinct**: Employees experience fear, grief, and sadness — executives systematically overestimate excitement. 76% of executives think employees are excited; only 31% are. Requires regular structured listening.

5. **Enable Micro-Experiments**: Allow small-scale safe pilots where failure is low-cost; build bottom-up experimentation culture.

6. **Shape the Story**: Authentic, purpose-driven narratives about what AI enables (not just efficiency) are required; leaders must be able to articulate a personal AI use case.

7. **Build for Durability, Not Just Adoption**: Sustain change through management reinforcement, performance systems, and community practices.

## Limitations

- BCG consulting research; potential selection and publication bias toward clients and outcomes BCG worked on.
- The "70% people-related value" figure is a BCG internal observation without an independent validation citation.
- Behavioral science principles are general; AI-specific evidence base is limited.
- Short article — principles are outlined but not formally tested.

## Open questions

- Are the 7 principles equally applicable across AI adoption in knowledge work vs. operational/physical work contexts?
- How does the advice change when AI adoption is *mandated* (Meta AI mandate case) vs. encouraged?
- What does "durable" AI adoption look like 5 years out — do habits persist or decay?

## My take

Bridges the AI capability adoption paradox: organizations have access to powerful AI but can't capture value because they treat it as a technology problem. The executive/employee excitement gap (76% vs 31%) is striking and well-sourced. The behavioral science framework is sensible though not novel — it applies established change management research (Prosci, Kotter) to AI. Complements and somewhat contradicts the meta-embrace paper (which shows forced AI mandates make employees miserable). The BCG prescription: earn adoption through agency, not compel it through mandate.
