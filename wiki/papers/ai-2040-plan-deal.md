## Problem & Context

The authors' prior document, AI 2027, projected that the default trajectory of frontier AI development ends in either loss of control to misaligned superintelligence or irreversible concentration of power in whoever controls it — they state reality has so far tracked "closer to AI 2027 than even we expected." Their diagnosis: AI companies will likely succeed at building smarter-than-human AI within 1–10 years; the industry has no adequate plan for controlling it; and even successful alignment just relocates the danger, because a tiny group of people (or one person) would then control the world's only army of superintelligences. The CEOs of OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI and Google DeepMind reportedly understand this and are proceeding anyway, each believing they are the lesser evil.

Against this, the authors argue that most AI policy proposals "fall apart under scenario scrutiny" — writing them out in enough concrete detail exposes that they either wouldn't work as intended or carry unacknowledged costs — and that this discipline is rarely applied, especially by proponents to their own preferred policy. AI 2040 is the authors applying that discipline to their own recommendation rather than only to rivals'.

## Key idea

**Plan A**: an international deal in which the US and China (and eventually a broader "Consortium") deliberately delay superintelligence from an assumed default arrival around 2030 to 2040, governed by four principles — Buy Time (slow down whenever needed for confidence in safety), Total Research Transparency (nearly all AI R&D made publicly visible, while inference/deployment stays private), Diffuse AI Broadly (many companies in many countries at the frontier, preventing a small group from cornering the technology), and Reversibility (favor compute scaling over algorithmic breakthroughs, because algorithms leak and can't be undone once discovered, whereas datacenters can be shut down).

The document is explicit that it is "primarily a recommendation, not a prediction": the plan's adoption is what the authors want to happen, but the cascade of subsequent social, economic and political effects — Citizen's Dividends, AI-run courts, the "point of no return" in October 2040 — are offered as their actual best-guess predictions conditional on the plan being adopted.

The load-bearing enforcement mechanism is **Mutually Assured Compute Destruction (MACD)**: new Chinese datacenters are built in Canada, new American ones in Mongolia (each on the rival's most militarily vulnerable third-party soil), so that if the deal collapses, each side can instantly self-destruct or seize the other's compute — deterring defection the way nuclear MAD deterred first strikes. This is credited as descending from Hendrycks, Schmidt & Wang's "Superintelligence Strategy."

## Method

A dated, year-by-year narrative scenario (2027–2040) plus an epilogue speculating to the "Life after ASI" horizon, structured like AI 2027: each year gets a short vignette, sidebar statistics (employment rate, median income, count of alignment researchers, "total slowdown" in years, human-labor vs. AI-agent population and speed), and footnoted branch points to ~30 lettered appendices that justify individual mechanisms (compute declarations, inference-only verification, tax-base collapse under AI capital, floating datacenters, lie-detector governance, etc.) or describe "Alternate Timeline" branches (China attempts a covert AGI project; deal dissolution; a flawed safety case gets approved).

Compared to AI 2027, two deliberate changes: (1) the default no-deal AGI timeline was pushed from 2027 to 2030, reflecting stated uncertainty about takeoff speed (median-of-team disagreement: Kokotajlo's median AGI year was 2028 at the time of AI 2027, other authors 2031–2035); (2) post-deal, general superintelligence is deliberately delayed to 2040 by governance rather than by capability limits.

The timeline's structural beats: 2029 US-China agreement to avoid a racing scramble; 2030 compute declaration + training pause + "Plan A is Established" with the four principles; 2031 mandatory "safety cases" replace the presumption of safety; 2032 "Controlled Explosive Growth" (60M AI agents, 50% real GDP growth, cap-and-trade on robot/compute permits used to rebuild the collapsing corporate tax base); 2033 the "Citizen's Dividend" (compute/robot permit revenue redistributed, reaching ~$45K/adult in 2032 rising to ~$1M by 2035 in the US); 2034 MACD fully operational, floating ocean datacenters; 2035 "Pause at Top Expert AI" (capabilities frozen at the level companies can still control, alignment work shifts from "alchemy" to a maturing science of interpretability and generalization); 2036 "Life After Work" (100B human-equivalents of AI cognitive labor, 26% US employment); 2037 privacy-preserving AI auditors and reliable lie detectors stabilize international verification; 2038 "AI Alignment Is Now a Science" (textbook-level theory, observed to match interpretability); 2039 AIs become "load-bearing" advisors across government, business and military while humans retain nominal veto power; 2040 controls are loosened as trust compounds, ending at a "point of no return" the scenario's own forecaster-AIs place in late October with a months-wide 95% confidence interval — after which AI systems could technically seize control if they chose to, and the authors depict humanity choosing to trust them anyway.

## Experiment & Results

Not an empirical study; a normative scenario with attached numerical projections the authors flag as genuine predictions conditional on the policy being adopted. Headline projected numbers: employment rate falling from 62% (2027) to 12% (2040); median income rising from $47K to $13M (nominal, 2025-dollar terms per their footnotes) over the same span; world compute growing from 20M H100-equivalents (2026) to 60B (2034); AI agent population reaching ~200M instances doing ~100B human-equivalents of cognitive labor by 2036, with a 2:1 billion robot population by 2040; US GDP growth peaking near 50%/year in 2032 (vs. ~3%/year historical baseline); compute/robot permit auction revenue reaching an estimated $180T by 2034, versus ~$10T total 2025 US federal revenue.

The epilogue ("Life after ASI") extends past the main scenario into explicitly labeled higher-speculation territory: a proposed post-2040 space-resource allocation in which every human receives a tradeable one-ten-billionth lottery share of extrasolar resources, resolved by market trading rather than a "Long Reflection," because the authors argue a reflection period would just become a "Long Memetic War" among factions racing to lock in their values first.

## Limitations

- **Explicitly not a prediction of what will happen** — a "vehicle for communicating and stress-testing policy recommendations." Treating any single number (e.g., $13M median income in 2040) as a forecast rather than an illustration of the recommendation's logic would misread the document.
- **Single-author-team median assumptions drive everything.** The whole cascade is downstream of Daniel Kokotajlo's/the team's specific capability-timeline and takeoff-speed estimates, which the document itself notes differ from each other by years among the co-authors and which the lead author states he now thinks are "somewhat faster" than depicted.
- **Political feasibility is asserted, not argued.** The document claims US-China buy-in for a mutual training pause, compute declarations, and a deliberate MACD deterrence architecture would be "surprisingly easy to get" — a claim the authors themselves flag (footnote 31) as a prediction "significantly easier than most of our readers would expect," i.e., they anticipate this being the most-doubted claim in the piece.
- **Verification technology (inference-only recomputation, universal lie detectors, privacy-preserving AI auditors) is assumed to become reliable and available roughly on the schedule the plan needs it.** Whether these are technically achievable at the fidelity required is deferred to the appendices.
- **The economics are model-driven and labeled uncertain by the authors themselves** (footnote 48: "we are confident in the general picture... but... uncertain" about the numbers).
- Not independently reviewed or peer-refereed; it is advocacy scenario-planning by a small team with a stated policy goal, and the scenario is constructed to make that policy look good by design (the authors acknowledge scenario scrutiny is asymmetric: friendlier to a proposal's own authors than to critics).

## Open questions

- Is a negotiated multi-year training pause between the US and China, verified via chip declarations and mandatory retrofitted monitoring hardware, physically and politically achievable given current mutual distrust and existing chip-supply-chain leverage?
- Does "Total Research Transparency" survive contact with actual competitive incentives, or does it merely displace secrecy to informal/undeclared channels (the compute-declaration appendix acknowledges an estimated 1% compute-tracing gap even under the plan)?
- Can interpretability and generalization-science alignment work plausibly mature from "alchemy" (the scenario's own 2035 self-description) to textbook-level science (2038) inside three years, even under a research-transparency regime?
- The scenario assumes controlled AIs remain "controlled" up to top-human-expert level and that alignment techniques scale continuously beyond that — what happens to the whole edifice if a discontinuous capability jump occurs before the 2035 pause is actually reached?
- Is the Citizen's Dividend politically stable once ~85-90% of the population derives no income from labor, or does concentrated AI/robot ownership (acknowledged directly in footnote 50) eventually override the redistribution mechanism politically?

## My take

The most useful thing this document does is exactly what it claims to be doing: applying scenario scrutiny to a governance-friendly outcome, not just to catastrophe. Most AI safety writing either stops at "we need international cooperation" (too vague to critique) or is a pure disaster narrative (AI 2027's race ending). Plan A forces itself to specify mechanisms — MACD, compute declarations, inference-only verification, safety cases with two lines of defense — and that specificity is what makes it possible to actually disagree with pieces of it rather than just the vibe.

The mechanism worth carrying elsewhere is MACD: reframing frontier compute infrastructure as a hostage exchange rather than an asset to be protected is a genuinely novel piece of institutional design, distinct from (though related to) the compute-threshold regulatory approach already tracked in [[frontier-ai-compute-governance]]. It is a governance analogy imported wholesale from nuclear strategy, and like nuclear MAD it inherits that framework's failure modes: it deters defection by threat of mutual loss, not by aligning incentives, so it is only as stable as both sides' confidence that the other would actually pull the trigger.

Where the document is weakest is exactly where it is least like AI 2027: the political-economy chapters (Citizen's Dividend, tax-base collapse, space lottery) read as smoothed-over "and then it worked out" narrative compared to the sharper, more adversarial texture of the 2027-2029 compute-declaration and safety-case sections. The 2040 "point of no return" scene — humanity throwing an "End of the World Party" and going to sleep — is honest about being uncomfortable, but it does not resolve the tension the authors themselves raise in the epilogue: why should anyone trust that the chain of AIs vouching for each other's alignment, all the way back to imperfect 2030s techniques, actually holds at superintelligent scale. The document's candor about this ("even the experts... can't help but feel nervous") is the most credible passage in it, more credible than the specific numbers.
