## Problem

How should individuals and societies respond to the accelerating trajectory of AI capability? The rapid advance of AI presents a choice: proactively explore what the future implies and make deliberate decisions about how to shape it, or ignore the implications and be forced into reactivity and passivity as capabilities continue to advance.

## Key idea

AI progress is not slowing down — it is accelerating, and resources being invested now mean "some amount of future progress is already locked in." This creates an obligation for individuals and societies to plan for the success of the AI endeavor: to actively explore what a future with increasingly capable AI means, rather than retreating from the implications by treating AI as just another general-purpose technology. Clark explicitly predicts that AI may be capable of building its own successors within 2 years, initiating a process of recursive self-improvement.

## Method

Public lecture / personal essay delivered at Oxford (May 20, 2026). Draws on:
- Clark's personal history with AI (from Import AI newsletter, co-founding Anthropic)
- The Epoch Capabilities Index (ECI) as a visualization of AI progress
- Personal narrative of AI integration into daily life (2023–2026)
- Use of AI to amplify personal expertise: Clark's Import AI newsletter as a "seed" for an AI system that can generate AI progress graphs autonomously
- Part 3 (not fully extracted): societal choices about AI governance and benefit distribution

## Results

Key positions:
1. **Planning for success**: We spend too much time planning for AI failure (regulation of harms) and not enough on what success of the AI endeavor means for humanity.
2. **RSI timeline**: Clark explicitly states AI systems that can build their own successors "could happen within the next two years, and possibly sooner" — not a distant scenario but a near-term planning horizon.
3. **AI as amplified personal expertise**: By seeding AI with his own newsletter/curation (Import AI), Clark created a skill that runs 1000x faster — a paradigm of human-AI collaboration grounded in individual taste and expertise.
4. **The "explore or retreat" choice**: Retreating from the present (dismissing implications, treating AI as normal technology) forces reactive, passive positions. Exploring the future requires acknowledging the power of the technology and making deliberate choices.
5. **Societal choices ahead**: How to shape AI, use it, direct it, distribute its benefits — all require reckoning with continued AI progress.

## Limitations

- Personal lecture, not peer-reviewed; timeline predictions (RSI in 2 years) are personal forecasts, not empirical claims.
- Insider perspective (Anthropic co-founder) may create selection bias toward more optimistic capability forecasts.
- "Explore the future" vs "retreat from the present" is a rhetorical framing, not a policy operationalization.

## Open questions

- What specific governance structures best support "exploring the future" vs defensive reactive regulation?
- How should the predicted RSI timeline affect current AI policy design?
- Can the "bottled expertise" model (personal seed → AI amplification) be generalized to societal-scale knowledge management?

## My take

One of the most candid public statements from an AI lab co-founder about the stakes of AI development. Clark's "planning for success" framing is a complement to the usual AI safety discourse focused on planning for failure. The RSI-within-2-years claim is striking and specific. The personal narrative of AI integration into intellectual life is compelling and honest. The lecture is best understood alongside the AINT debate: Clark's position is implicitly "AI is *not* a normal technology" and that this demands different planning horizons.
