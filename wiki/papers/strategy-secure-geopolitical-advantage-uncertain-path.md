## Problem & Context

The United States has no settled strategy for the possible emergence of superintelligence. Public debate is dominated by starkly different prescriptions — race to a decisive lead, co-develop safely with rivals including China, pause development globally, actively suppress rival programs, or remove regulatory friction and accelerate — each championed by different camps (Aschenbrenner's "The Project," CERN-for-AI proposals, responsible-scaling-policy advocates, the Pause movement, Hendrycks/Schmidt/Wang's Mutually Assured AI Malfunction, and effective-accelerationism/techno-optimism respectively), with no shared framework for comparing them. This paper, from RAND's Center for the Geopolitics of Artificial General Intelligence, builds on the authors' companion paper ("Finding Common Ground in AGI Strategy Debates," Predd et al. 2026) to argue that the apparent disagreement is not really about goals (most camps want both geopolitical advantage and humanity's survival with agency) but about unresolved empirical and strategic uncertainties, and that the standard framing of "commit now vs. wait for evidence" is itself a false choice because both paths destroy options.

## Key idea

Seven archetypal strategies exhaust the live options in current debate, organized into three families by whether they judge human-AI coexistence feasible: **Coexistence strategies** (A. Dominance — race to an exclusive lead and suppress rivals; B. Co-Development — a CERN/consortium-style joint-governance approach including rivals; C. Preparedness — informal coexistence via responsible-scaling-policy-style voluntary commitments and incident response) accept that coexistence is achievable and differ on how to build toward it. **Denial strategies** (D. Moratorium — a verifiable global halt above capability thresholds; E. Deterrence — Mutually-Assured-AI-Malfunction-style suppression of rival programs by threat or force; F. Continuity of Society — hardened, distributed human settlements as a fallback) judge coexistence infeasible and seek to prevent or outlast advanced AI instead. **Acceleration** (G) stands alone, judging the dangers of constraint to exceed the dangers of AI development itself. Which strategy is sensible depends on five pivotal uncertainties — danger proximity, coexistence feasibility, restraint feasibility, decisive-strategic-advantage attainability, and suppression feasibility — that current evidence cannot resolve, and four of the five resolve asymmetrically (evidence can confirm but not rule out danger, coexistence, decisive advantage, or restraint; only suppression infeasibility can be conclusively demonstrated, by a rival succeeding despite it). Since neither committing now nor waiting for conclusive evidence is safe (both can irreversibly foreclose strategic options through five distinct mechanisms), the paper recommends [[freedom-action-ai-strategy]]: deliberately build the capabilities and foundations that keep multiple strategies alive, signal intentions carefully in a competitive environment, and commit only when a pivot's evidence becomes compelling or a capability's "latest decision date" forces the choice.

## Method

Structured comparative-strategy analysis rather than empirical research: (1) a taxonomy construction exercise deriving seven mutually exclusive archetypes from combinations of judgments on the five pivots (Figure 1 maps how pivot combinations resolve to strategies); (2) for each archetype, identification of a canonical public proponent/statement, an explicit "win state," and a "structural pathology" (the way the strategy degenerates even when working as designed) — summarized in a comparison table; (3) a mechanism analysis of "risks of foreclosure," cataloguing five distinct ways strategic options are lost (institutional decay, deployment lock-in, geopolitical fait accompli, agency erosion, epistemic foreclosure), each illustrated with a historical analogue (Open Skies Treaty withdrawal; carbon-energy lock-in; the 1949 Soviet nuclear test foreclosing the Baruch Plan's monopoly premise; and 1990s-2000s encryption-export-control liberalization, respectively); (4) a signaling analysis treating strategic investments as communications in a competitive multi-actor environment, using the Taiwan-strait ambiguity policy as an analogue for how the U.S. might disclose counter-AI capability while withholding intent; (5) synthesis into a seven-objective "Freedom of Action" program (build the human-AI ecosystem; build an AI security architecture; adapt legacy national-security institutions — nuclear, biosecurity, conventional military, intelligence, counterterrorism, cybersecurity, diplomacy; build citizen/firm/government response capacity; monitor for indications and warnings; guard against catastrophe and lock-in; seize advantage and signal deliberately); and (6) a self-critique section addressing four standing objections to the recommended strategy.

## Experiment & Results

No empirical experiment; the paper's "results" are its argued conclusions. Headline claims: (a) the seven strategies cannot be objectively ranked given current evidence on the five pivots; (b) premature commitment to any one strategy forecloses others by the five cataloged mechanisms (e.g., committing to Dominance lets the cooperative-verification institutions Co-Development and Moratorium need atrophy through institutional decay; committing to Acceleration deepens deployment lock-in that forecloses governance-dependent strategies); (c) deferring commitment forecloses options too, since long-lead capabilities (verification tech, diplomatic channels, evaluation institutions, coexistence research) decay through neglect while waiting for conclusive evidence, and a government that defers while deployment continues is "effectively executing Acceleration in practice" by default; (d) resource cost is argued to be dominated by the gap between any serious strategy and the status quo, not by the marginal cost of preserving optionality across strategies, drawing a historical analogy to the post-1947 national-security-state buildup and the post-9/11 Homeland Security buildup as precedents for the scale of institutional reorientation required; (e) the paper anticipates and responds to four critiques of its own recommendation (cost of optionality; whether the shortest AI timelines leave no room for the strategy at all; whether "keep everything open" just produces institutional paralysis / lack of will to commit; and whether the Freedom of Action apparatus itself creates a new deployment-lock-in-style pathology by acquiring a constituency for permanently avoiding commitment).

## Limitations

- Purely a qualitative strategy-comparison framework; a companion technical report (cited as forthcoming) is meant to supply the comparative evidentiary analysis this paper's claims are built on, which is not yet available to assess.
- The seven-archetype taxonomy is explicitly a simplification of "current debates," and its exhaustiveness is asserted rather than derived — the paper does not rule out that novel strategies or hybrids outside this space could emerge.
- The "Freedom of Action" recommendation's own critique section concedes it may not survive the shortest AI-timeline scenarios (a fast takeoff leaves no time to build foundations or wait for evidence) and may itself develop the deployment-lock-in-style pathology (a monitoring/review apparatus that resists ever committing) it warns other strategies about.
- The signaling-and-foreclosure analysis is argued largely by historical analogy (Cold War buildups, Open Skies, encryption export controls, Taiwan-strait ambiguity, the Baruch Plan) rather than by any model of the current AI-specific strategic environment, and analogical reasoning of this kind is inherently contestable case by case.
- Disclosed LLM use in drafting (organizing/synthesizing research memos and drafting initial text, per the acknowledgments) with all factual claims stated to be independently verified — a limitation of process transparency worth noting given the paper's own subject matter concerns AI's effects on human judgment and agency.

## Open questions

- Which specific pivot-factor evidence would practically suffice to trigger commitment under the paper's own "commit by evidence" rule, and who adjudicates when evidence has become "compelling" given that four of five pivots cannot be conclusively resolved?
- Can "latest decision dates" for specific perishable capabilities (verification infrastructure, diplomatic channels, coexistence research) actually be estimated with enough precision to drive real institutional timelines, or does the framework mainly supply useful vocabulary rather than an operational trigger?
- How does the Freedom of Action strategy's seven-objective program interact with the AI race dynamics and compute-governance mechanisms already documented elsewhere in the literature (e.g., compute thresholds, mutually-assured-compute-destruction-style deterrence architectures) — does building toward all seven strategies simultaneously worsen or dampen competitive racing pressure relative to committing early to one?
- What institutional design would let the monitoring function (Objective 5) reliably detect the paper's own flagged "agency erosion" foreclosure mechanism, given the paper's own admission that collective, strategic-level agency erosion has not yet been observed and measuring it is an open research problem?

## My take

The paper's most durable contribution is not the specific recommendation (Freedom of Action, essentially "buy optionality") but the analytical apparatus for stating precisely *why* the debate between AI strategy camps resists resolution: the five-pivot decomposition gives each camp's disagreement a specific, nameable locus (which pivot they judge differently) rather than leaving "some people want to race and some want to pause" as an unstructured clash of intuitions, and the five-foreclosure-mechanism taxonomy gives "keep your options open" real analytical teeth instead of leaving it as a platitude. The self-critique section is unusually honest for a strategy-recommendation paper — explicitly conceding that a fast-takeoff scenario could make the whole recommendation moot, and that the recommended apparatus could itself become the very kind of option-foreclosing lock-in the paper warns against. Where the paper is weakest is in resource-realism: the claim that the marginal cost of cross-strategy optionality is "fairly modest" relative to the cost of any serious strategy at all is asserted via historical Cold-War/post-9/11 analogy rather than demonstrated, and a plan requiring "complete societal and institutional reorientation" whose central selling point is that it avoids hard commitment seems politically harder to sustain across administrations than the paper's own political-economy analysis (of *other* strategies' lock-in dynamics) would suggest.
