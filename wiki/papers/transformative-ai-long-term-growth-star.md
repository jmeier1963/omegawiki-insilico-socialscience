## Problem & Context

Earlier growth frameworks (Trammell and Korinek 2023; Jones and Tonetti 2026) are supply-side: AI either substitutes for labour and creates super-exponential growth, or complementarity (a weakest-link task) caps aggregate gains. They assume consumer demand keeps pace with supply, which may fail if automation diverts income from labour to AI investment.

## Key idea

Add a **demand bottleneck**: every displaced worker is also a lost consumer, so the spending that rewards innovation shrinks, and forward-looking firms stop investing in automating the next task. Productivity stalls for lack of demand, not technology.

## Method

A task-based production model in which AI capital automates a rising share of tasks, two household classes (capital owners and workers) with different marginal propensities to consume, and a free-entry condition governing innovation and automation investment. Calibrated to standard benchmarks for trend productivity, markups, product turnover and innovation rents, with pre-AI labour share and r-star matched to data. Varying automation scope and innovation's reliance on demand yields four scenarios (Rungcharoenkitkul 2026a).

## Experiment & Results

Four scenarios: business as usual (2% trend growth, r-star unchanged), bounded productivity boost (permanent growth shift, higher r-star), transformative AI (exponential growth, labour share toward zero, higher r-star), and demand bottleneck (growth first rises then falls below trend; labour-share decline between the cases; r-star initially rises then falls below its pre-AI baseline).

## Limitations

A short box summarizing a separate paper; calibration and assumptions (two household types, demand reliance parameters) are uncertain by the authors' own account, and the scenarios are illustrative simulations rather than forecasts.

## Open questions

How much do AI profits and innovation rely on consumer demand? How fast does competition erode AI rents? How should monetary policy respond to scenario uncertainty about r-star?

## My take

Valuable because it brings a central-bank perspective to the displacement debate, with a clear mechanism (lost consumers) that also appears in the wiki's displacement-fragility thinking. It reads as a mechanism sketch more than evidence.
