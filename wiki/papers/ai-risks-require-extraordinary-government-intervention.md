## Problem

Derek Thompson critiques the "AI as Normal Technology" (AINT) framework, agreeing with Kapoor & Narayanan on labor market impacts but arguing that AI's emergent, unpredictable risks justify *extraordinary* government intervention including pre-deployment restrictions. This essay responds: is extraordinary intervention justified, and is it more effective than resilience-building?

## Key idea

Extraordinary government intervention (precautionary restrictions, nonproliferation regimes, licensing) is extraordinarily costly, hard to enforce in the AI context (no physical bottleneck equivalent to enriched uranium), and risks permanent expansion of state control over research and publishing. The alternative — investing in societal resilience against AI misuse — is more effective, distributes defenses broadly, and does not require the same degree of unilateral state authority. AI nonproliferation buys at most a few months of delay given open-weight models; resilience prepares society for when capabilities inevitably become widely available.

## Method

Policy analysis / argumentative essay. Not an empirical study. References nuclear nonproliferation as comparative case, plus examples of existing AI-related interventions (export controls, chip restrictions, voluntary predeployment evaluations).

## Results

Key arguments:
1. **Extraordinary interventions are costly**: nuclear nonproliferation required the IAEA, the NPT, decades of diplomacy, and military confrontation — and was partially enforceable because of a physical bottleneck (enriched uranium) that AI lacks.
2. **AI nonproliferation is brittle**: no physical bottleneck; frontier capabilities can be matched within months; open-weight models and API access mean effective nonproliferation requires licensing regimes and restrictions on what research can be published.
3. **Resilience is preferable**: distributes defenses across society rather than relying on a single chokepoint; does not require extraordinary state authority; prepares society for inevitable capability diffusion.
4. **Slippery slope risk**: "abnormal technology" framing, if institutionalized, could become a permanent expansion of government control over digital technologies beyond AI.

## Limitations

- Argumentative piece, not empirical research; claims are not empirically tested.
- Provides limited operationalization of "resilience" — what specific investments are proposed is underspecified.
- Does not address scenarios where AI capabilities have no precedent and no known resilience strategies.

## Open questions

- What specific resilience investments are most cost-effective for which AI risk categories?
- Is there a threshold of AI capability at which even the authors would endorse extraordinary intervention?
- How should democracies balance resilience investment with the pace of AI capability development?

## My take

A thoughtful extension of the AINT argument to the governance domain. The nuclear analogy is instructive: nonproliferation required enormous ongoing effort and was only partially successful even with a physical chokepoint. Without an equivalent for AI, the case for nonproliferation-style intervention looks weaker. The resilience alternative is underdeveloped in the essay but points to an important research agenda.
