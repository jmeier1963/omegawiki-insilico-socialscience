## Problem

AI leaders predict transformation of knowledge work, and legal services are often flagged as especially vulnerable (GPT-4 passed the bar; some predict AI replacing lawyers by 2035). Legal services are expensive — millions are priced out, big-firm partner rates exceed $2,300/hr — so many expect AI to make them cheaper and more accessible.

## Key idea

Applying the **AI as Normal Technology** framework: advanced AI will *not, by default*, deliver desired legal outcomes at lower cost. Capability is not the binding constraint; three structural **bottlenecks** stand between capability and transformation — **regulatory barriers, adversarial dynamics, and human involvement.**

## Method

Conceptual analysis (Lawfare Research Paper, co-authored with Justin Curl, Harvard Law), mapping each bottleneck and historical analogies (e.g., digitization and e-discovery).

## Results

- **Regulatory barriers:** unauthorized-practice-of-law (UPL) rules and entity-based ownership restrictions limit how consumers and lawyers can use AI; without reform, capability gains don't reach end users regardless of how advanced AI becomes.
- **Adversarial dynamics:** legal outcomes depend on *relative* not absolute quality. When both sides get more productive, the competitive equilibrium shifts upward — an arms race where achieving the same result (settlement, trial win) requires *more* legal work, so cost-per-task falls but cost-per-outcome need not. (Analogy: digitization could have cut discovery costs, but litigators expanded review instead.)
- **Human involvement:** trust, accountability, and judgment keep humans in the loop, capping disintermediation.

## Limitations

- US-centric (UPL, adversarial common-law system); conclusions may differ elsewhere.
- Essay/working-paper, not empirical measurement.
- Argues against automatic cost reduction, not against any benefit from AI.

## Open questions

- Which regulatory reforms (if any) would let AI lower cost-per-outcome rather than cost-per-task?
- Do adversarial arms-race dynamics generalize to other professional services?

## My take

The adversarial "relative quality" argument is the sharpest contribution — a structural reason capability gains can be competed away rather than captured as consumer surplus. A clean second instantiation of [[ai-normal-technology]], complementary to the software-engineering essay's decide-execute-deliver mechanism.
