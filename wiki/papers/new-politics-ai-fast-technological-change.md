## Problem

Traditional technology policy is process-neutral and incremental. AI represents qualitatively different technological change (smart, human-like, agentic), requiring a new approach that is outcomes-focused and bold rather than technology-neutral.

## Key idea

Argues for "bold policy targets" approach to AI governance: policy should aim at specific societal outcomes (health, productivity, climate) rather than neutral technology facilitation. Fast AI change requires proactive shaping of AI deployment to solve societal challenges rather than waiting for market-driven outcomes.

## Method

Policy analysis and conceptual argument. IPPR report by Carsten Jung (February 2025). Reviews AI capabilities, labor market projections, and existing policy frameworks.

## Results

- AI is qualitatively distinct: models achieve graduate-level reasoning, pass Turing-equivalent tests, and can act as autonomous agents
- Current AI policy too neutral and reactive; fails to direct AI toward pressing social problems
- Proposes "challenge-led AI" approach: set specific outcomes (e.g., compress cancer drug discovery timelines) and mobilize AI investment toward those goals
- Warns: without policy direction, AI will be deployed primarily for commercial purposes that may not align with public interest

## Limitations

- Bold policy targets require political will difficult to sustain across electoral cycles
- Challenge-led approach may crowd out AI applications in areas not targeted by policy
- UK-specific policy context; may not generalize to other jurisdictions

## Open questions

- How can democratic accountability be maintained in AI policy targets?
- Does outcomes-focused regulation constrain innovation?

## My take

Useful progressive policy framework. The distinction between neutral and outcomes-focused AI governance is important. Practically useful for UK policy discussions. Importance 3 (think-tank policy analysis).
