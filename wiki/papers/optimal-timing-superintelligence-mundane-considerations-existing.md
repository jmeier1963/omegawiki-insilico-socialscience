## Problem

Some advocates call for a pause or halt to AGI development given catastrophic risks. But not developing superintelligence also has costs: disease, aging, and death continue. What is the *optimal* timing for developing superintelligence, given both the risks of developing it and the costs of not doing so?

## Key idea

**Risky surgery analogy**: developing superintelligence is not like playing Russian roulette but like undergoing risky surgery for a condition that will otherwise prove fatal. Decision-theoretic models incorporating safety progress, temporal discounting, QALY differentials, and concave utility functions suggest that even high catastrophe probabilities are often worth accepting. The optimal strategy may be "swift to harbor, slow to berth" — move quickly to AGI capability, then pause briefly before full deployment.

## Method

- Decision-theoretic framework from a **person-affecting stance** (not expected utility over all future beings; focuses on currently existing people)
- Models incorporate:
  - Safety progress (probability of aligned superintelligence increases with time)
  - Temporal discounting (people die while waiting)
  - Quality-of-life differentials (pre-superintelligence suffering vs. post-superintelligence flourishing)
  - Concave QALY utilities and prioritarian weighting

## Results

- For many parameter settings, **even high catastrophe probabilities are worth accepting** because forgoing superintelligence means continued disease, aging, and death
- **Prioritarian weighting** (favoring the worst-off) further shortens the optimal timeline
- Optimal strategy in many scenarios: move quickly to capability, then pause briefly before deployment ("swift to harbor, slow to berth")
- Poorly implemented pauses could do more harm than good (losing safety progress, increasing racing dynamics)
- The "everyone dies if we build AGI" framing ignores the "everyone dies if we don't" baseline

## Limitations

- Working paper, not peer reviewed
- Person-affecting stance excludes considerations about future generations — controversial ethical position
- Does not engage with the specific technical pathways to aligned superintelligence
- Parameters for catastrophe probability and safety progress rate are highly uncertain

## Open questions

- How sensitive are the conclusions to the choice of ethical framework (person-affecting vs. total consequentialist)?
- What is the empirical evidence for safety progress as a function of time?
- How should governance institutions implement a "brief pause before deployment"?

## My take

Bostrom (the author of "Superintelligence") arguing from first principles for an accelerationist-leaning conclusion is noteworthy and provocative. The person-affecting framing is underused in AI safety discourse. The "risky surgery" analogy effectively reframes the risk calculus. Importance 4 — intellectually significant working paper from a key figure in AI safety.
