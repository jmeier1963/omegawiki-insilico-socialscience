## Problem

The AI boom has minted vast fortunes (Huang's ~4% Nvidia stake worth $175bn; Anthropic's ~$1tn round doubling Amodei's wealth), yet fewer than one in three Americans expect AI to make ordinary people richer. With US wealth already concentrated (top 1% own ~⅓; bottom half ~2.5%), if AI raises returns to capital over labour the divide could widen — and superintelligence could leave gains to "whoever owns the machines." So proposals to redistribute AI's gains are gaining traction across the political spectrum.

## Key idea

A skeptical cost–benefit analysis of **AI wealth-fund / equity-sharing schemes**. The serious underlying idea — give the public a *stake* in AI's upside as insurance against concentration — is sound, but the leading mechanisms (voluntary equity donations à la Altman/Trump; Sanders's forced 50% transfer; broad AI wealth levies) face severe practical problems and, by the numbers, would deliver only a few hundred dollars per American per year — "not enough to make anyone rich."

## Method

Op-ed/analysis. Works through mechanism design (how assets reach public hands), quantified scenarios, and disbursement models (Norway's oil fund, Alaska's Permanent Fund, "Trump accounts").

## Results

- **Mechanisms all have flaws:** voluntary donations dilute existing investors (incl. Microsoft, pensions, retail) and "can be meaningful or painless, not both" (Altman owns no OpenAI equity to donate); direct state purchases put taxpayers on the hook at frothy valuations; forced transfers (Sanders) resemble expropriation, inviting legal battles and chilling investment.
- **The math is small:** 3% each from OpenAI and Anthropic ≈ a $55bn fund → ~$140bn after a decade at 10%; 4% annual payout ≈ **$20/American/year**. Even with 10–20× appreciation and picking winners, only a few hundred dollars per year. A broad 0.2% market-value levy across labs, chipmakers, and cloud providers ≈ $40bn/year — still only a few hundred dollars each, "well short of a UBI."
- **Taxing corporate profits** already gives citizens an efficient claim without picking winners; the case for explicit funds is largely *political* (visibility, insurance).
- Disbursement models map onto politics: Norway's fund (left), Alaska dividends / "Trump accounts" (right).

## Limitations

- Short op-ed; US-centric; scenario figures are illustrative at current valuations.
- Assumes valuations and appreciation paths that are highly uncertain.

## Open questions

- Is the value of AI wealth funds mainly symbolic/political rather than material?
- Would a broad-based levy be administrable given the difficulty of defining an "AI firm"?

## My take

A useful empirical deflation of the "give the public equity" enthusiasm: even generous schemes yield trivial per-capita payouts, so their value is visibility/insurance, not income. Sharpens [[sharing-ai-prosperity]] by quantifying why the headline mechanisms underwhelm — a quantitative counterpoint to the OpenAI/Amodei prosperity rhetoric.
