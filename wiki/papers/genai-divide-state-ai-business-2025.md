## Problem & Context

Enterprises poured $30–40B into GenAI, yet transformation is scarce. This MIT NANDA report (Challapally, Pease, Raskar, Chari; research Jan–June 2025) asks why, using a multi-method design: systematic review of 300+ public AI initiatives, 52 structured organizational interviews, and 153 senior-leader surveys across four industry conferences.

## Key idea

There is a stark bimodal split — the **GenAI Divide** — between the ~5% of integrated pilots extracting millions in value and the ~95% stuck with no measurable P&L impact. The divide is determined by approach, not model quality or regulation, and its core barrier is a **learning gap**: most GenAI systems do not retain feedback, adapt to context, or improve over time.

## Method

Mixed-methods enterprise research plus a composite "AI Market Disruption Index" scoring nine industries 0–5 on five indicators (incumbent market-share volatility, AI-native revenue growth, new business models, user-behavior change, AI-attributed org changes). Adoption/deployment funnel measured across buyers (enterprise/mid-market/SMB) and builders (startups/vendors/consultancies).

## Experiment & Results

- 95% of organizations get zero return; only 5% of integrated pilots extract millions.
- Generic tools (ChatGPT, Copilot): 80%+ explored, ~40% deployed, but they lift individual productivity, not P&L. Enterprise-grade custom/vendor systems: 60% evaluated → 20% piloted → 5% production; most fail on brittle workflows, no contextual learning, and operational misalignment.
- Four patterns: limited disruption (only 2 of ~8 sectors — Tech, Media — show structural change), enterprise paradox (big firms lead pilots, lag scale-up), investment bias (budgets favor visible top-line over high-ROI back office), implementation advantage (external partnerships ~2× the success of internal builds).
- Crossers see selective workforce impact (support, software eng, admin) and BPO/agency savings; a "shadow AI economy" of employees using personal tools is widespread.

## Limitations

- Preliminary findings; anonymized, partly interview/survey-derived; conference-sampled leaders may not represent all firms.
- The disruption index is a constructed composite with subjective indicators.
- "95% get zero return" is a headline figure sensitive to how return and integration are defined.

## Open questions

- Is the learning gap a fundamental limitation or an engineering/product problem (memory, feedback loops)?
- Does the external-partnership advantage reflect capability transfer or just selection?
- Will the 95% cross the divide as tooling matures, or is the divide durable?

## My take

The useful, contrarian claim is that the binding constraint is *learning*, not infrastructure/talent/regulation — systems that don't retain feedback or adapt to a specific process stall regardless of model quality. That reframes enterprise AI failure from "not enough model" to "no learning loop," which matches the observation that generic chat tools help individuals but not P&L. The "95% get zero return" number is doing a lot of rhetorical work and depends heavily on definitions, but the funnel (60%→20%→5%) and the external-partnership advantage are the load-bearing evidence.
