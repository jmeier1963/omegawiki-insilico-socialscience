## Problem & Context

Retraction counts had been rising for a decade, but as a background trend. Richard Van Noorden's Nature analysis (*Nature* 624, 479–481, 12 December 2023) marks the year the trend became a structural finding: retractions are rising at a rate that outstrips the growth of scientific papers, which means the rate, not just the count, is increasing.

## Key idea

The record is not distributed misconduct but a concentrated failure of one publishing model. Roughly 8,000 of the 10,000-plus retractions came from the catalogue of Hindawi, a subsidiary of Wiley, following concerns about compromised peer review and systematic manipulation of the publication process. Hindawi's reliance on **special issues** — article collections overseen by guest editors — became the attack surface: scammers posed as guest editors in order to publish fake papers at volume.

## Method

Journalistic analysis drawing on Retraction Watch and Crossref data, publisher statements, and comparison of retraction rates across countries and over time.

## Experiment & Results

- Retractions issued in 2023 exceeded **10,000**, a roughly **2.5-fold** increase over the preceding year and an annual record.
- About **8,000** originated from Hindawi titles; Wiley subsequently wound down the imprint.
- The retraction rate outpaces the growth in publication volume.
- Among large research-producing nations, **Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, Russia and China** show the highest retraction rates over the past two decades.

## Limitations

- News analysis, not a peer-reviewed study.
- Retraction counts measure detection, not incidence — a rise can indicate better policing as easily as more fraud, and the Hindawi cluster is clearly a detection event.
- Country-level rates are confounded by field mix, publication venue and incentive structures.

## Open questions

- What fraction of paper-mill output is being detected at all?
- Does the special-issue vulnerability generalize to other high-volume publishing models?
- How does the detection capability scale if generation costs fall further?

## My take

The right use of this source is narrow and it is often stretched. It documents a **retraction record**, and specifically a single publisher's collapse — it is not evidence for the replication crisis, which is a different phenomenon with a different literature. Used precisely, it establishes something more pointed than a general malaise: a publishing model built on volume and delegated editorial oversight can be industrialized against, and the industrialization was detected only after roughly 8,000 articles had entered the record.

That is the pre-AI baseline against which any argument about cheap generation has to be read. The vulnerability was already structural when producing a plausible manuscript still cost something.
