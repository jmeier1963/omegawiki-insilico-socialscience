## Problem & Context

The pandemic produced phases of severe epistemic uncertainty and, simultaneously, considerable moral uncertainty arising from conflicts of values and goals. Public debate treated trust as a resource to be measured (the COSMO study tracked population trust in physicians, scientists, health authorities and government) without much clarity about what trust is or what it does epistemically. Judith Simon, who holds the chair for ethics in information technology at Hamburg, supplies the philosophical account.

## Key idea

Trust is not a substitute for knowledge but one of its conditions. Because no individual can verify more than a fraction of what they must rely on, the trustworthiness of members of epistemic communities does substantial epistemological work — on the strong reading Simon reports from John Hardwig, trust is often epistemologically more basic than empirical data or logical argument, and the trustworthiness of members of epistemic communities is the ultimate foundation for much of what we know.

The corollary is that trust, trustworthiness and responsibility form a triad: trust is warranted only where trustworthiness is real, and trustworthiness is demonstrated through the assumption of responsibility.

## Method

Philosophical analysis drawing on the trust literature — Annette Baier's "Trust and Antitrust" as the entry point, Hardwig on epistemic dependence — applied to the pandemic case.

## Experiment & Results

Not empirical. The argument's payload is the distinction between trust as a psychological attitude (what COSMO measures) and trust as an epistemic relation with normative conditions (what makes reliance rational). Where the two come apart — high measured trust without trustworthiness, or warranted trustworthiness without measured trust — the diagnosis and the remedy differ entirely.

## Limitations

- Short essay in an edited volume, aimed at a broad medical-scientific audience rather than at the specialist trust literature.
- Pandemic-specific; the transfer to routine science is left to the reader.
- Does not engage with what happens to the trust relation when the epistemic community includes non-human producers of claims.

## Open questions

- What makes an epistemic community trustworthy in a way that can be checked from outside it?
- How should trust be allocated when the chain of dependence includes systems that cannot bear responsibility?
- Does the triad survive when the volume of claims exceeds any community's capacity to vouch for them?

## My take

The reason this piece matters beyond the pandemic is the Hardwig line it carries. If trust is epistemologically basic — if most of what we know rests on the trustworthiness of others rather than on our own checking — then the question raised by machine-produced claims is not whether they are correct but whether anything in the chain can be trustworthy in the required sense. Trustworthiness presupposes the capacity to take responsibility, and that is exactly what a model cannot do. Simon does not draw this consequence; the argument supports it.
