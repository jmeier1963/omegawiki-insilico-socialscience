## Problem

Educators face a dilemma: LLMs can assist student learning but risk replacing the cognitive work students need to do for genuine learning. There was no principled framework for integrating AI into classroom assignments in ways that preserve learning outcomes while harnessing AI capabilities.

## Key idea

Propose seven distinct AI roles for classroom use — AI-tutor, AI-coach, AI-mentor, AI-teammate, AI-tool, AI-simulator, and AI-student — each with explicit pedagogical benefits, associated risks, example prompts, and mitigation guidelines. The core principle across all roles: keep students as the "human in the loop," actively evaluating and challenging AI output rather than passively consuming it.

## Method

Conceptual framework development: the authors synthesize pedagogical theory (desirable difficulties, metacognition, active learning, Bloom's taxonomy) with prompt engineering practice to design seven role archetypes. Each is presented with example prompts, sample AI outputs, a risk analysis, and concrete guidelines for teachers and students.

## Results

Framework articulation rather than empirical results. The seven roles are:
1. **AI-tutor** — personalized direct instruction on demand
2. **AI-coach** — metacognitive scaffolding and reflection prompts
3. **AI-mentor** — personalized career and domain guidance
4. **AI-teammate** — collaborative thinking partner for group tasks
5. **AI-tool** — task-specific functional use (writing aids, data analysis)
6. **AI-simulator** — role-play and scenario simulation for practice
7. **AI-student** — students teach the AI, deepening their own understanding

The overarching recommendation is to shift class time toward active discussion and collaborative exercises, reserving AI for personalized outside-of-class coaching and tutoring.

## Limitations

Conceptual rather than empirical — no controlled study of learning outcomes. Risks flagged include: AI complacency (students uncritically accepting outputs), error propagation, bias amplification, and over-reliance replacing independent thinking.

## Open questions

- Which role archetypes most effectively preserve learning gains vs. those that produce cognitive off-loading?
- How should AI role design differ across disciplines, grade levels, and learner populations?
- What assessments can detect when students have shifted from productive AI collaboration to passive AI consumption?

## My take

A highly practical practitioner-facing framework from Mollick, who has become one of the most read voices on AI in higher education. The taxonomy of roles is intuitive and immediately actionable. Its limitation is the lack of empirical validation — but as a design scaffold for educators, it fills an important gap. Widely cited in education literature.
